ABSTRACT: Few enzymes are known to catalyze reductive
cyclizations via nucleophile-mediated C−C bond formation.
Medium-chain dehydrogenases/reductases (MDRs) typically func-
tion as dehydrogenases or reductases. However, a distinct subclass
of MDRs involved in polycyclic tetramate macrolactam (PoTeM)
biosynthesis catalyzes reductive cyclizations via hydride-mediated
C−C bond formation. Here, we present the apo and substrate-
bound structures of OX4 and CftD, two enzymes responsible for
the third ring formation in PoTeMs biosynthesis. Structural and
mutational analysis reveal a catalytic mechanism wherein OX4
initiates a NADPH-dependent 1,6-reduction, followed by cycliza-
tion to form the C11−C22 bond, water-mediated protonation of
the C7-carbonyl oxygen, and a final tautomerization to produce the
cyclized product. Precise substrate positioning and stabilization of the enolate intermediate by the conserved residue W260 are
critical for catalysis. These findings represent the first structural and mechanistic understanding of this newly identified cyclase
subgroup and offer promising new avenues for enzyme engineering and natural product biosynthesis.
■INTRODUCTION
Cyclization is a pivotal process in natural product biosynthesis,
playing a key role in generating diverse molecular scaffolds,
increasing structural rigidity, and enhancing biological activity.
Reductive cyclizations involving nucleophile-mediated C−C
bond formation are notably rare.1 One such example is iridoid
synthase, a short-chain NADPH-dependent dehydrogenase
(SDR) initially proposed to catalyze the cyclization of 8-
oxogeranial to iridodial through a Rauhut-Currier reaction.2
However, subsequent studies suggested that the transformation
occurs via a Michael reduction mediated by NADPH, potentially
without direct enzymatic catalysis.3−6 In contrast, the biosyn-
thesis of polycyclic tetramate macrolactams (PoTeMs) involves
enzymes that catalyze hydride-mediated reductive cycliza-
tions,7−10 showcasing a distinct and unique catalytic mechanism.
PoTeMs are widely distributed bioactive polyketide-non-
ribosomal peptide (PK-NRP) hybrids produced by bacterial
strains, characterized by a tetramic acid moiety and a fused 5−
5−6 or 5−6−5 tricyclic system with a 16-membered macro-
lactam.11,12 These compounds have attracted significant
attention due to their structural complexity and concise
biosynthetic pathways.7−16 Their biosynthesis begins with the
synthesis of a shared precursor, polyene-tetramate-polyene, by a
single-module PKS-NRPS. The first two rings (5−5 or 5−6
system) are assembled by one or two flavin-dependent
oxidoreductases, while the third ring is constructed by an
NAD(P)H-dependent medium-chain dehydrogenase/reduc-
tase (MDR) homolog.
MDRs are a ubiquitous family of oxidoreductases found
across all domains of life. They are characterized by a highly
conserved protein fold and typically function as zinc-dependent
NAD(P)H-binding dehydrogenases or zinc-independent reduc-
tases.17 Uniquely, PoTeM-related MDRs are the first identified
to catalyze cyclization reactions.8−10 Phylogenetic analysis
shows that these enzymes are closely related to the zinc-
independent prostaglandin reductase subfamily of MDRs
(Figure S1). Some members of this group have been
characterized by enzyme assays and isotopic labeling studies.
OX4 and PtmC catalyze the formation of the third 6-membered
ring in the 5−5−6 tricyclic PoTeMs, HSAF (Heat-Stable
Antifungal Factor) and pactamides, respectively.8,9 OX4
selectively transfers the pro-R hydride from NADPH to C21

Despite these advances, the precise structural basis and catalytic
mechanisms underlying PoTeM-related MDR activity remain
unclear.
In this study, we present crystal structures of OX4 and CftD in
their apo forms and OX4 complexed with NADP+ and its
substrate. Structural and mutational analysis reveal the
molecular details of hydride-mediated reductive cyclization
catalyzed by OX4 and its homologs, elucidating the enzymes’
regioselectivity, stereoselectivity, and substrate specificity.
Furthermore, we demonstrate the rational engineering of CftD
to alter its substrate selectivity, revealing the potential for
designing novel MDR-based biocatalysts. This work advances
our understanding of cyclase mechanisms and lays a foundation
for developing engineered cyclases in synthetic biology and drug
discovery.

Structural alignment using Dali server revealed that OX4 is

superfamily. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first

and α3−β6 loop of its catalytic domain compared to At5g16970
and PA1648 (Figure S4), which may be related to its unique
cyclase activity.
Cofactor Binding and Specificity of OX4. Our previous in
vitro assay demonstrated that OX4 prefers NADPH over
NADH.8 To investigate the structural basis of this specificity, we
solved the structure of the OX4-NADP+ binary complex at 3.1 Å
resolution (Figure S5 and Table S2), revealing no significant
structural changes upon NADP+ binding (RMSD: 0.4 Å)
(Figure S6). The electron density for NADP+ was deeply buried
in the cleft between the nucleotide-binding and catalytic
domains (Figure S5), adjacent to the canonical NAD(P)H-
binding motif (166GXXGXXG172), resembling the conserved
NAD(P)H binding observed in other MDRs.21 Specific
interactions stabilize the extra 2′-phosphate group of NADP+
by OX4 is likely mediated by hydrogen bonds with the side
chains of S168, K195, and Y210, and the backbone amide
nitrogen of G191. Replacement of S168 and K195 with alanine
reduced NADPH binding affinity and enzymatic activity by 30%
and 90% (Figures S7−S9). Similar effects were observed in CftD
mutants, with the G169A-K196A double mutant completely
abolished catalytic activity (Figures S7−S9). These results
demonstrate the crucial role of these conserved residues in
NADPH binding and cofactor selectivity.
Substrate Binding and Recognition of OX4. To
investigate the molecular mechanism of enzyme−substrate
interactions, the ternary complex structures of OX4-NADP+-1
and OX4-NADP+-1a were determined at resolutions of 2.0 and
2.6 Å, respectively (Figure 2a and Table S2). The OX4-NADP+-
1 structure closely resembled the apo form (RMSD: 0.6 Å) and
exhibited high similarity to the OX4-NADP+-1a structure
(RMSD: 0.3 Å, Figures S6 and S10a), indicating that OX4
employs a rigid binding pocket for both NADP+ and its
substrate.
Unexpectedly, the electron density maps of 1 and 1a in the
ternary complexes did not fully align with the previously
reported absolute configuration,23 suggesting that the reported
structures might contain incorrect assignment for absolute
configuration. To address this discrepancy, we generated the
derivative 3-deOH-16-OH HSAF through combinatorial
biosynthesis and solved its crystal structure at 0.81 Å resolution
(Figures S11−S15 and S25−S30, and Tables S3 and S4). This
analysis enabled us to determine that the tautomeric state of the
tetramic acid moiety is the exo-Z-enol form, which is also
supported by the 13C chemical shift values for C1 (δC 195.8),
C25 (δC 171.9), and C27 (δC 175.3) (Table S3). As a result, we
revised the absolute configuration of 1, 1a, and related HSAF
analogs (Figures 1a and S16). The revised structure of 1 was
found to be identical to compound c, which was initially
identified from Streptomyces15 and later synthesized via
combinatorial biosynthesis.24
Now, compound 1 perfectly matched the substrate electron
density in the cleft between the catalytic and nucleotide-binding
domains, forming a T-shape with NADP+ (Figure 2a). In this
configuration, the bicyclic system of 1 extends into the enzyme’s
active site, positioning its C21 atom 3.4 Å from the C4 atom of
the nicotinamide ring. This geometry facilitates a direct pro-R-
specific hydride transfer (Figure 2b). These findings explain how
OX4 selectively catalyzes the transfer of a deuterated hydride
from (R)-[4-2H]NADPH to C21 of 1, but not from (S)-
[4-2H]NADPH.18
The substrate-binding pocket of OX4 forms a hydrophobic
environment comprised of residues Y50, D57, D67, F90, T110,
F111, M113, F115, W260, F290, S291, and L292 from one
subunit, alongside M280 and F281 from the other (Figure 2b).
The C1-carbonyl oxygen is stabilized by a hydrogen bond with
the indole nitrogen of W260 and interacts with the main-chain
carbonyl oxygen of Y50 via a water molecule (W1). The
hydroxyl group of Y50 forms a hydrogen bond with the carbonyl
oxygen of the side chain of D48 and participates in a water-
mediated hydrogen bond with the main-chain amide nitrogen of
D67. This intricate hydrogen-bonding network likely plays a
critical role in maintaining the spatial conformation of the
substrate-binding pocket. Moreover, the aromatic ring of Y50,
along with the nicotinamide ring of NADP+, stabilizes the two
diene moieties of substrate 1 through π-π stacking interactions.
The C7-carbonyl group forms hydrogen bonds with both the
main-chain carbonyl oxygen and the amide nitrogen of F111
through another water molecule (W2), while the C20-hydroxyl
group forms a hydrogen bond with the side chain of S291. The
C27-carbonyl oxygen forms a hydrogen bond network with the
main-chain amide nitrogens of M280 and F281, mediated by a
water molecule (W3). Further stabilization of substrate 1 is
provided by additional hydrophobic residues (F90, M113, F115,
F290, and L292) through van der Waals interactions. Super-
position of the structures of OX4-NADP+-1 and OX4-NADP+-
1a reveals that the two reacting atoms (C11 and C22) are
similarly positioned within the enzyme’s active site (Figure
S10b). The proximity of C11 and C22, which approaches each
other at a van der Waals distance of 2.9 Å and at an angle
comparable to that observed in 1a, suggests that substrate 1
adopts a near-attack conformation.
Mutations in these residues reduced 1a production and
increased Kd values for 1 binding, with mutants F115A, W260F,
F281A, and Y50FW260F completely abolished the production
of 1a (Figures 2c, S7 and S8, and Table S5). These findings
highlight the critical role of precise substrate positioning,
achieved through a complex network of residue interactions,
most of which do not directly participate in catalysis but are
necessary for proper geometry in the active site. Interestingly, no
residues were identified as directly responsible for proton
transfer to the C10 position (Figure 2b), suggesting that water
most likely serves as the proton source.18
Notably, several OX4 mutants showed increased production
of a previously unnoticed minor product (1b) in the OX4
reaction (Figures 2d and S12). To characterize this product, 1b
was prepared from a scaled-up in vitro reaction of the OX4
mutant W260F. Spectroscopic analysis revealed that 1b is a new

could support the cyclization process, we constructed OX4

and favoring production of 1b. To confirm this observation, we
introduced a corresponding mutation (W261A) in CftD. As
anticipated, the CftD W261A mutant failed to produce the cyclic
product 3a, instead producing a new product, 3c, with a UV
spectrum similar to that of 1b (Figures 2d and S12). Preparative
reactions with CftD W261A allowed for the isolation of 3c,
which was identified as a reduced product of 3 by HRMS and
NMR analysis (Figures S13, S18, and S36−S40 and Table S7).
This finding is consistent with the effects observed for the W260
mutations in OX4. These results underscore the critical role of
W260 in orienting the substrate for efficient ring formation
during catalysis.
Substrate Stereospecificity of OX4. Previous studies
showed that OX4 catalyzes the reduction but not ring formation
of 2. To investigate its substrate stereospecificity, we solved the
OX4-NADP+-2 complex structure at 2.5 Å resolution (Figure 2a
and Table S2). Clear electron density for 3-deOH ATB (2) was
observed at the same binding site as 1 (Figure 3a). As expected,
structural alignment of the OX4-NADP+-1 and OX4-NADP+-2
complexes revealed no significant conformational changes in
OX4 itself (Figure 2a), but notable differences were identified in
the substrate conformations (Figure 3b). In 2, the Z-
configuration of the C10=C11 double bond significantly alters
the geometry of the substrate. Specifically, the distance between
C11 and C22, the atoms involved in ring closure, increases from
2.9 Å in 1 to 3.3 Å in 2, likely hindering the cyclization step
(Figure 3b). Furthermore, the C7-carbonyl group of 2 is
oriented toward a hydrophobic environment, preventing
favorable interactions with water molecules. This exclusion of
water reduces the electrophilicity of the C11 carbon and
eliminates the proton source necessary for cyclization (Figure
3b).
Mutagenesis studies further support these observations.
Substitutions at residues near the C7-carbonyl group, including
D57, F111, and M113, had minimal impact on OX4 activity
toward 2 (Figure 2c), consistent with the lack of water-mediated
interactions in this region. In contrast, mutations affecting
substrate positioning, such as the Y50FW260F double mutant,
completely abolished activity toward 2 (Figure 2c), revealing the
essential role of these residues in properly orienting 2 relatives to
the NAD(P)H nicotinamide ring for reduction. These findings
suggest that OX4’s rigid binding pocket enforces distinct
substrate conformations, creating environments that influence
catalytic outcomes (cyclization or reduction). For 2, the altered
geometry, higher energy barrier, and absence of proton donors
prevent ring formation, resulting in reduction as the sole
catalytic outcome. Overall, these findings demonstrate how
substrate geometry influences the catalytic function of OX4,
particularly in the ring formation step.
Substrate Flexibility of OX4. Previous studies showed that
IkaC is highly specific to its native substrate, the 5−6 bicyclic
preikarugamycin (3). In contrast, PtmC, which shares 65%
identity with IkaC, exhibits greater substrate flexibility,
recognizing both 5−5 and 5−6 bicyclic intermediates to
produce a tricyclic system.9,10 PtmC also converts substrate 3
into two products, 3a and 3b, which differ only in the position of
a double bond (Figure 4a).9 However, the mechanism
underlying this substrate flexibility remains unknown.
Phylogenetic analysis divided the PoTeM-related MDRs into
two clades. PtmC and OX4 (68% sequence identity) belong to
the “5−5−6 tricyclic clade”, while IkaC and CftD (65%
sequence identity) are grouped within the “5−6−5 tricyclic
clade” (Figure S19). In vitro assays revealed that OX4, like
PtmC, can convert 3 to both 3a and 3b, while CftD, the cyclase
responsible for forming the third ring in clifednamides,25 can
convert 3 only to 3a, similar to IkaC (Figure 4b). Given 3b
appears to be unstable, we investigated whether it could
spontaneously convert to 3a. We isolated 3a and 3b from the in
vitro OX4 reaction with substrate 3, but no interconversion
between 3a and 3b was observed (Figure S20a). Furthermore,
CftD can catalyze the reduction of 1 and 2 (Figures 4b and
S20b).
To investigate OX4’s ability to produce both 3a and 3b, the
OX4-NADP+-3 complex structure was solved at 3.4 Å resolution
(Figure S21), although electron density for 3 was observed only
in one subunit. Comparison of the OX4-NADP+ structures

To further investigate the differing substrate tolerance of OX4
and CftD, the apo structure of CftD was solved at 2.0 Å
resolution and found to be nearly identical to that of OX4
(Figure 4d). However, attempts to obtain the CftD-NADP+-3
complex were unsuccessful. Instead, structural comparisons of
CftD’s apo structure with the OX4-NADP+-1 complex revealed
three regions near the substrate-binding site that differed
between the two enzymes (Figure 4e). Sequence alignment
revealed that these motifs are relatively conserved within each
clade (Figure S22), suggesting they may play a role in shaping
the substrate-binding site architecture.
To test the functional effects of these structural differences, we
created CftD mutants containing chimeric sequences from the
corresponding regions of OX4 (L51-D57, Q265-H271, or
S291−W300). All CftD chimeras exhibited altered catalytic
activity. The CftDL51‑D57 mutant catalyzed the conversion of 3 to
both 3a and 3b, likely due to a backward shift in the catalytic-
binding pocket edge (Figure 4e). This structural change may
expose the C8 atom of 3 to the solvent, enabling the formation of
3b. However, this mutant was unable to convert substrate 1 into
1a (Figure S23). The CftDQ265‑H271 mutant, in contrast,
converted 1 to 1a but failed to produce 3b from 3 (Figure
S23). Interestingly, the CftDS291−W300 mutant exhibited catalytic
activity similar to OX4, accepting both substrates 1 and 3 and
producing 1a/1b and 3a/3b, although with a relatively low yield
for 1a (Figure 4b). These findings suggest that the S291−W300
loop plays a critical role in substrate versatility. To further
confirm the role of this loop, we replaced the S291−W300
segment of OX4 with the corresponding sequence from CftD to
generate the chimera OX4A291‑L300. Unfortunately, the soluble
OX4A291‑L300 failed to bind Ni NTA (Figure S7) and exhibited
no activity toward substrates 1 or 3 (Figure S23), implying it
may be misfolded. We propose that the CftDS291−W300 mutant
narrows the bottom of the substrate-binding pocket, altering the
insertion angle of substrate molecules. Consequently, the C7-
carbonyl oxygen of 1 and the C8 atom of 3 are exposed to the
solvent, facilitating cyclization and enabling the production of 1a
and 3b. Overall, our results highlight how differences in these
loop regions directly influence substrate specificity and product
diversity, highlighting their importance in shaping the functional
diversification of PoTeM-related MDRs.

completely abolished OX4’s cyclase activity while significantly
increasing the production of the reduced shunt product 1b. This
finding indicates that the intermediates involved in the
cyclization process are related not only to the C7-carbonyl
group but also to the C1-carbonyl group. In all substrates (1−3),
the C1-carbonyl group and the Δ21,22, Δ23,24, and Δ25,26 double
bonds form the first conjugated system, while the C7-carbonyl
group and the Δ8,9 and Δ10,11 double bonds, form the second
one. The reductive cyclization mechanism of OX4 proceeds
through three steps. First, the correct positioning of substrate 1
in the active site enables NAD(P)H to donate the C4 pro-R
hydride to C21, the electrophilic carbon in the first conjugated
system in 1. This reaction forms an enolate intermediate I, where
the negative charge on the C1-carbonyl oxygen is stabilized by
the indole nitrogen of the conserved residue W260. The second
step involves the C7-carbonyl oxygen forming a hydrogen bond
with W2 (2.7 Å), which increases the electrophilicity of C11 in
the second conjugated system. This facilitates an intramolecular
Michael-type addition, resulting in the formation of a C−C bond
between C11 and C22. The resulting π-electron translocation
leads to protonation of C7, likely mediated by W2, and the
formation of an s-cis diene structure between C7 and C10
(intermediate II). In the final step, tautomerization converts
intermediate II into product 1a, which contains a cis Δ8,9 double
bond (Figure 5a).
In contrast, the formation of the noncyclized byproduct 1b
arises from an alternative pathway. The negatively charged C1-
carbonyl oxygen in intermediate I lacks proper stabilization due
to the mutation of W260 or improper position of the substrate.
This instability leads to the protonation of C1, potentially
mediated by the nearby W1 (2.9 Å). Consequently, the reaction
favors the formation of intermediate III, bypassing cyclization.
Intermediate III then undergoes tautomerization, resulting in
the protonation of C24 rather than C22. This preference is
attributed to the formation of a transition state that resembles a
6-membered ring, similar to that observed in the formation of
1a, ultimately yielding 1b (Figure 5b). These results emphasize
the importance of the elongated and narrow architecture of the
OX4 substrate-binding site, which ensures proper substrate
positioning for efficient catalytic activity. Mutations in active site
residues can misalign the substrate, destabilizing the enolate
intermediate I and reducing the efficiency of the initial hydride
transfer from NAD(P)H to C21 or subsequent cyclization steps.
Such disruptions may favor the protonation of the C1-carbonyl
oxygen, shifting the reaction toward the formation of 1b, as
observed in OX4 mutants (Figure 2c).

configuration, unlike the E configuration in 1, increasing the
C11−C22 distance from 2.9 Å in 1 to 3.3 Å in 2. This structural
change raises the energy barrier for cyclization. Second, the lack
of accessible water molecules to interact with the C7-carbonyl

■CONCLUSIONS
MDRs typically function as dehydrogenases or reductases,
playing a crucial role in diverse biochemical processes. However,
the PoTeMs-related MDR uniquely operates as a cyclase,
catalyzing the reductive formation of 5- or 6-membered rings. In
this study, we revealed that OX4-mediated cyclization occurs
through a mechanism that is both structurally and mechanis-
tically distinct from previously characterized cyclases. Although
a similar NAD(P)H-dependent reductive cyclization mecha-
nism was originally proposed for the conversion of 8-oxogeranial
to iridodial,4,6 subsequent studies suggested that the cyclization
step may not be enzyme-catalyzed.3 Our structural analysis
reveals how a canonical MDR architecture has evolved to
mediate C−C bond formation with substrate selectivity,
adopting a novel cyclization function. Furthermore, we
demonstrate the rational engineering of CftD to modify its
substrate selectivity, enabling the formation of rings with
different sizes. These findings highlight the versatility and
tunability of MDR enzymes, providing a platform for the design
of MDR-based biocatalysts to synthesize novel cyclic com-
pounds. This work advances our understanding of enzymatic
cyclization and has significant implications for drug discovery
and the biochemical synthesis of complex natural products.
