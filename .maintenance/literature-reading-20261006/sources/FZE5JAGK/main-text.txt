ABSTRACT: Pimprinine-type indolyloxazole alkaloids (PIAs),
originally discovered in Streptomyces, show a diverse range of
important pharmaceutical and agricultural bioactivities, yet their
biosynthesis remains unknown. Herein, we report the identification
of the biosynthetic enzymes responsible for the formation of the
indolyloxazole structure of PIAs from a rhizosphere-derived
Streptomyces netropsis NZY3, which involves three key enzymes,
PimA (GCN5-related N-acetyltransferase, GNAT), PimB (pyr-
idoxal 5′-phosphate-dependent tryptophan racemase), and PimC
(FeII/α-ketoglutarate-dependent dioxygenase, FeII/αKGD), nota-
bly by an unprecedented D-configurational substrate metabolic
streamline. First, PimB acts as a gatekeeper to donate the D-
tryptophan precursor for the PIA pathway from the L-tryptophan
pool of primary metabolism. Subsequently, a unique D-tryptophan GNAT, PimA, catalyzes the formation of N-acyl D-tryptophan.
Finally, another novel N-acyl D-tryptophan-specific FeII/αKGD, PimC, finishes the formation of an indolyloxazole structure through
a proposed radical rearrangement-mediated ring closure mechanism, which is supported by a series of deuterium- and 18O-labeling
experiments in vitro. PimC also catalyzes the formation of the trans-vinyl group containing shunt products 1a to 3a through an
oxygen-rebound mechanism followed by dehydration and decarboxylation or a carbocation-involved decarboxylation pathway.
Furthermore, comparative genomic mining reveals that PIA biosynthetic gene clusters (PIAs BGCs) are widely distributed in
Actinobacteria and Myxobacteria, suggesting the potential for discovering new PIA-producing strains. This work expands our
knowledge about the biosynthetic mechanisms of pharmaceutic-valued indolyloxazole alkaloids, laying an important foundation for
their future production through synthetic biology and metabolic engineering strategies.
■INTRODUCTION
Indolyloxazole alkaloids featuring a characteristic 5-(3-
indolyl)oxazole core structure are mainly produced by two
types of bacteria, Streptomyces and Pseudomonas, as well as by
several marine macroorganisms, such as Martensia fragilis,
Diazona angulata, and Thuiaria breitfussi (Figure S1).1−6 These
compounds exhibit a diverse range of unique and excellent
pharmaceutical bioactivities. For example, pimprinine-type
indolyloxazole alkaloids (PIAs) previously discovered from
Streptomyces show antienterovirus,7 antiepileptic,1 and antith-
rombotic activities.8 Another Streptomyces-derived chlorinated
indolyloxazole alkaloid, streptochlorin, displays significant
inhibitory activity on human leukemia cells (IC50 = 1.05 μg/
mL)9 and could also act as a potent inhibitor of tyrosinase
oxidase involved in melanin biosynthesis.10 Martefragin A
isolated from red alga M. fragilis efficiently inhibits lipid
peroxidation in rat liver microsomes (IC50 = 2.8 μM).3
Diazonamide A produced by D. angulata exhibits excellent in
vitro activity against human HCT-116 colon carcinoma and
murine B-16 melanoma cancer cells (IC50 < 15 ng/mL).4
Pimprinine, streptochlorin, and almazoles were also frequently
utilized as basic pharmacophore in organic synthesis to develop
new indolyloxazole derivatives with potent agricultural
bioactivities, such as anti-phytopathogenic fungal, antiplant
viral, herbicidal, and insecticidal properties.11−13
The biosynthesis of oxazole or oxazoline motifs containing
natural products has been widely reported, with different
enzymes involved in their formation (Figure 1). In the
biosynthetic pathways of post-translationally modified peptides
(RiPPs), a cyclodehydratase first catalyzes intramolecular 2-

three types of compounds: (i) normal PIAs, such as pimprinine

(±)-pimprinol D, and pimprinone A; (iii) dimeric PIAs,

dipimprinine B, dipimprinine C, and dipimprinine D (Figure

Streptomyces netropsis NZY3, which was found to produce

similar chemical features, the biosynthetic mechanisms of PIAs
and LIAs may be different. By combining a series of strategies
including feeding isotope-labeled precursors, genome mining,
heterogeneous expression, gene knockout, and complementa-
tion, a three-gene locus pimABC fromS. netropsis NZY3 was
confirmed to be responsible for the biosynthesis of PIAs, 1, 2,
and 3 (Figure 2A). PimA, a GCN5-related N-acetyltransferase
(GNAT), PimB, a PLP-dependent tryptophan racemase, and
PimC, a homologue of TauD/TfdA family taurine dioxygenase,
were all characterized by a series of in vitro biochemical assays
and in vivo biotransformation experiments. Notably, these

■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Identification of the PIA Biosynthetic Gene Cluster in
the Genome of S. netropsis NZY3. S. netropsis NZY3
(Figure S2), isolated from the rhizosphere ofPrunus purpurea,
shows 99.79% identity with that of S. netropsis SXYM1629
based on its 16S rRNA sequence (GenBank: PP741723.1).
Four known PIA compounds (1, 2, 3, and 4) were identified
from the fermentation products of S. netropsis NZY3 (Figure
2B, Figures S3−S10, and Tables S6 and S7). To understand
their biosynthesis in Streptomyces, we first analyzed theS.
netropsis NZY3 genome using the Pseudomonas-derived LIA
biosynthetic genes ioxA and ioxB as probes. As a result, no hit
was obtained as expected, consistent with that described in the
literature.25 Previously, halogenated PIAs had been identified
from a PIA-producing strainStreptomyces netropsis WLXQSS-4
by feeding halogenated (6-Cl or 6-Br) L-tryptophan, suggesting
that
L-tryptophan possibly serves as a precursor in PIA
biosynthesis.29 Given the highly similar structures of PIAs and
LIAs, we speculated that the indolyloxazole moiety in PIAs
may also come from N-acylated L-tryptophan as that of LIAs,25
that is, an unknown acyltransferase may assemble different
acyl-CoAs onto L-tryptophan. To confirm this hypothesis, we
fed sodium propionate-1-13C (at 2 mM) during the
fermentation ofS. netropsisNZY3, and compounds 2 and 4
were successfully
13C-labeled (Figure S11), indicating that
short-chain fatty acids such as acetic acid, propionic acid, and
n-butyric acid should be the precursors for PIAs 1, 2, 3, and 4
according to their structural features. Therefore, we deduced
that the biosynthesis of PIAs in Streptomyces probably also
involved an acyl group transfer onto
L-tryptophan by an
acyltransferase, followed by an oxygenase-catalyzed oxazole
ring formation, similar to the LIAs pathway in Pseudomonas but
using distinct enzymes.
Next, we conducted an HMM search of theS. netropsis NZY3
genome using the conserved acetyltransferase domains
(PF13718, PF13302, PF13673, PF13508, and PF00583 from
the Pfam database), and finally, three-gene cassettes containing
both acyltransferases and oxygenases were found (Figure S12).
Heterogeneous expression of these cassettes, respectively, in
Streptomyces coelicolor M1154 andStreptomyces albus J1074
revealed that gene cassette 2#, containing three genes (named
as pimA, pimB, and pimC, as shown in Figure 2A), was
sufficient to produce PIAs, 1, 2, and 3, indicating that pimABC
is responsible for the biosynthesis of PIAs (Figure 2B, panels iv
and vii). Bioinformatic analysis shows that PimA belongs to
GNAT family but displays no homology to the GNAT IoxA
from the LIA pathway.25 Instead, PimA shares a 20.45%
identity with PamZ (ID: WP_023484187.1), which catalyzes
the N-acetylation of the Aga-6 position in paenilamicin
biosynthesis fromPaenibacillus larvae.30 PimB belongs to the
tryptophan synthase beta superfamily (fold type II) and shows
31.74% identity with
L-threonine dehydratase TdcB (ID:
P0AGF6) fromEscherichia coli (strain K12).31 PimC shows
39.65% identity with FeII/α-ketoglutarate-dependent dioxyge-
nase (FeII/αKGD) TauD (ID: P37610) fromE. coli (strain
K12)32 (Figure S13).
To verify their involvement in PIAs’ production inS. netropsis
NZY3, each gene was respectively knocked out through in-
frame deletion,33 and all the mutants (NZY3::ΔpimA,
NZY3::ΔpimB, and NZY3::ΔpimC) lost the ability to produce
PIAs 1, 2, 3, and 4 (Figure 2C and Figures S14−S16).
However, no intermediates were accumulated. In addition, we
also inactivated the four genes located upstream (orf(−1) and
orf(−2)) and downstream (orf(+1) and orf(+2)) of pimABC,
and these deletions did not affect the production of PIAs
(Figures S17−S21). Genetic complementation experiments
were further carried out by introducing a copy of pimA/pimB/
pimC under the control of double strong promotors ermEp*
and kasOp*34 into the corresponding mutant. All three genetic
complementation strains (NZY3::ΔpimA + cpimA, NZY3::Δ-
pimB + cpimB, and NZY3::ΔpimC + cpimC) restored the
ability to produce PIAs (Figure 2C). These results confirmed
that all three genes were essential for PIA biosynthesis inS.
netropsis NZY3. Interestingly, compared to the LIA pathway in
Pseudomonas, except for an acyltransferase gene pimA and an
oxidase gene pimC, an additional gene pimB was also necessary,
suggesting a differential biosynthetic mechanism of PIAs inS.
netropsis NZY3 from that of LIAs.25
Characterization of PimA as a Novel GNAT Acyl-
transferase with
D-Configurational Selectivity. To
investigate the function of PimA, it was solubly expressed
and purified to homogeneity inE. coli BL21(DE3) (Figure
S22A). L-Tryptophan was first tested as the substrate using
acetyl-CoA as an acyl group donor in the in vitro biochemical
assays. Surprisingly, as shown in Figure 3, although N-
acetylated
L-tryptophan (5a) was detected in HPLC and
HR-MS, the yield was very low (Figures S23−S25).
Considering that PimA shared 20.45% identity with PamZ
(ID: WP_023484187.1,Paenibacillus larvae), which catalyzes
the N-acetylation of the Aga-6 position in paenilamicin notably
with specific D-configuration selectivity,30 D-tryptophan was
then tested as a substrate in the in vitro biochemical assays.
Intriguingly, N-acetylated D-tryptophan (5b) was detected with
an apparent higher yield, about 50 times greater than that of N-
acetylated L-tryptophan (5a) (Figure 3 and Figures S25 and
S28). Propionyl-CoA and n-butyryl-CoA were also utilized as
acyl group donors, and the production of the N-acyl
(propionyl, butyryl) L-tryptophans (6a, 7a) was respectively
∼25 and 90% of the corresponding N-acyl (propionyl, butyryl)
D-tryptophans (6b, 7b) (Figure 3 and Figures S25−S28).
These in vitro biochemical assays suggested that PimA
preferred D-tryptophan over the more common L-tryptophan
as a substrate. To further verify it, we respectively fedS.
netropsis NZY3 with either L-tryptophan or D-tryptophan (at 2
mM) during the fermentation and found that the yields of
PIAs 1, 2, 3, and 4 were significantly higher in the
D-
tryptophan-feeding group, compared to the
L-tryptophan-
feeding and no-feeding groups (Figure S29). Therefore, the
above in vitro and in vivo experiments supported that PimA is
more likely to act as a D-tryptophan-dependent N-acyltransfer-
ase able to accept different short-chain acyl-CoAs in the
biosynthesis of PIAs.

eukaryotic sources together with PimB homologues by NCBI
blast (Figure S30). We found that PimB is located in a new

feeding D-serine (at 5 mM) or D-serine with indole (at 5 mM/5

To investigate the in vitro biochemical function of PimB, it
was expressed and purified into homogeneity inE. coli
BL21(DE3) (Figure S22A). The protein solution of PimB
showed a bright-yellow color and had a typical PLP-binding
absorbance at 415 nm in the spectrometer38,39 (Figure S32).
When tested with L-tryptophan and D-tryptophan as substrates,
respectively, and analyzed by chiral-HPLC, we found that
PimB could catalyze the interconversion of the two isomers in
vitro (Figure 4B). Interestingly, the ratios of D/L-tryptophan
isomers (0.8395 and 0.8482, respectively) in both reactions
were comparable, suggesting that the equilibrium of reaction
catalyzed by PimB prefers to keep more L-tryptophan, an
essential amino acid for cell growth. Previous studies showed
that D-amino acids can be toxic to the cell growth in various
organisms, and amino acid racemases could be utilized for
detoxification by transforming D-isomers into their nontoxic L-
isomers.40,41 Therefore, we propose that PimB acts as a
specialized tryptophan racemase to collect partial L-tryptophan
from the primary metabolism pool of S. netropsis NZY3 to
provide D-tryptophan precursors, thus regulating the entry of
substrates into the PIA biosynthetic pathway.
PimC, a FeII/αKGD, Catalyzes the Formation of the
Indolyloxazole Motif with D-Configurational Selectivity.
After identifying the functions of PimA and PimB, we deduced
that PimC, a FeII/αKGD homologue, should be responsible for
producing the indolyloxazole motif from N-acyl tryptophans.
We fed N-acetyl L-tryptophan (5a) and N-acetyl D-tryptophan
(5b) to the mutant NZY3::ΔpimA during fermentation.
Intriguingly, only N-acetyl D-tryptophan led to the production
of PIA 1 (Figure 4C), suggesting that PimA and PimC also
showed D-configurational selectivity. To further verify this,
PimC was also purified as a soluble protein such as that of
PimA and PimB from E. coli BL21(DE3) and tested with N-
acetyl L-tryptophan (5a) and N-acetyl D-tryptophan (5b) in
vitro (Figure S22A). As expected, similar to PimA, although
PimC could convert both isomers, it more preferred the N-
acetyl D-tryptophan as a substrate (Figure 4D). Two major
products, 1 and 1a, were detected, and 1 showed an identical
retention time and HR-MS with the pimprinine standard
(Figure 4D and Figures S33 and S34). Through scaled-up
reactions and semipreparative HPLC, 1a was purified and its
chemical structure was elucidated by HR-MS (Figure S34) and
1D, 2D NMR (Tables S8 and S9 and Figures S35−S41). 1a is
a new compound without the oxazole ring, featuring a trans-
vinyl group, which might be produced through decarboxylation
and dehydrogenation based on 5a/5b. To test if 1a was an
intermediate in the formation of compound 1, we tested
whether PimC could convert 1a to 1 in vitro, but it could not
(Figure S42). A time-course study (0 to 8 h) of PimC with N-
acetyl D-tryptophan (5b) showed that 1 and 1a were almost

Verification of the PimC-Catalyzed Oxazole Ring
Formation Mechanism by In Vitro Stable Isotope-
Labeling Assays. Although FeII/αKGDs catalyzed a diverse
type of reactions including hydroxylation,32,42−44 ring for-
mation,24,45,46 ring rearrangement,47−49 endoperoxide forma-
tion,50−52 halogenation,53−55 desaturation,56,57 demethyla-
tion,24,58 decarboxylation,59 oxidative deamination,60 and
epoxidation,61,62 the formation of the unique indolyloxazole
ring by FeII/αKGDs has not been reported. Multiple sequence
alignment (MSA) shows that PimC shares the conserved metal
ion-coordinating residues (H102, D104, and H261 equivalent
to H99, D101, and H255, respectively, in TauD), and the α-
KG-binding residue (R272 equivalent to R266 in TauD fromE.
coli32) (Figure S76). Site mutagenesis of these residues into
alanine was conducted individually, revealing that all the
mutations either lost or reduced the activity of PimC in
producing 1 and 1a from both substrates, N-acetyl L-
tryptophan (5a) and N-acetyl
D-tryptophan (5b) (Figures

■CONCLUSIONS
In this study, we identified the PIAs BGC pimABC fromS.
netropsis NZY3 through combined strategies, including stable
isotope labeling of precursors, genome mining, heterogeneous
expression, gene knockout, and complementation. Three key
enzymes PimA, PimB, and PimC involved in PIA biosynthesis
were functionally characterized. Of note, we revealed an
unprecedented D-configurational substrate metabolic stream-
line. First, PimB (a PLP-dependent tryptophan racemase) acts
as a gatekeeper to donate the D-tryptophan precursor for the
PIA pathway from the L-tryptophan primary metabolism pool.
Subsequently, a unique D-tryptophan-specific GNAT, PimA,
catalyzes the formation of N-acyl D-tryptophan by condensing
acyl-CoA and D-tryptophan. Finally, PimC, a novel FeII/αKGD
specific for N-acyl D-tryptophan, catalyzes the formation of an
indolyloxazole moiety through a radical rearrangement-
mediated ring closure mechanism. 18O-labeling experiments
confirmed that the oxygen atom in the indolyloxazole motif
originates from the N-acetyl group of the substrate. An oxygen
rebound, followed by dehydration and decarboxylation
mechanism or a carbocation-involved decarboxylation path-
way, may be used for producing the trans-vinyl group
containing shunt products 1a/2a/3a by PimC. Interestingly,
compared to the indolyloxazole alkaloid biosynthetic pathway
inPseudomonas,25 an additional gatekeeper enzyme PimB is
required in Streptomyces to regulate substrate entry, possibly
contributing to precise metabolic control. Furthermore,
comparative genomic analysis and in vitro biochemical assays
revealed that the PIA BGC homologues are widely distributed
in Actinobacteria and Myxobacteria. These findings expand our
understanding of the biosynthetic mechanisms of indolylox-
azole alkaloids and establish a foundation for the future
application of these pharmaceutically and agriculturally
valuable compounds by synthetic biology and metabolic
engineering.
