ABSTRACT: Amide bond-forming enzymes play a crucial role in
generating structural diversity in natural products by assembling
them from relatively simple precursors. Two distinct types of
standalone amid-bond-forming enzymes are commonly involved in
natural product biosynthesis, including ATP-grasp enzymes and
amide bond synthetases. Here, we report a new family of amide
bond synthetases that catalyze amide bond formation between
deazapurine as the sole carboxylic acid substrate and various amine
substrates, which we have designated as deazapurine amide bond
synthetases (DABS). This evolutionarily related enzyme family
plays a central role in diversifying the structures of peptidyl
deazapurine natural products. Our gene mining analysis reveals that
most DABS-associated biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) remain
cryptic. Therefore, systematic characterization of these cryptic BGCs holds great potential for discovering novel peptidyl deazapurine
natural products with diverse biological activities.
■INTRODUCTION
7-Deazapurine nucleosides are a rare family of microbial
natural products, with only a few members reported to date.1,2
Examples include huimycin, sangivamycin, tubercidin, and
toyocamycin (Figure S1).3−6 Toyocamycin has been identified
as a selective inhibitor of X-box binding protein 1 (XBP1) and
cyclin-dependent kinase 9 (CDK9), with IC50 values of 80 nM
and 79 nM, respectively, making it a promising anticancer
candidate.7,8 Sangivamycin showed broad-spectrum antiviral
activity, with efficacy against Ebola and COVID-19 in-
fections.9,10 These natural products share a common precursor,
7-carboxy-7-deazaguanine (CDG), derived from guanosine-5′-
triphosphate (GTP) by three cluster-situated genes, including
GTP cyclohydrolase I, 6-carboxy-5,6,7,8-tetrahydropterin
synthase, and 7-carboxy-7-deazaguanine synthase.1,11 Subse-
quent tailoring of this CDG core governs the structural
diversity of the 7-deazapurine natural products. The major
structural difference between these natural products lies in the
modification of the 7-carboxyl group of CDG and
glycosylation. For example, the 7-carboxyl group in CDG is
converted into an amide in sangivamycin and into a nitrile in
huimycin and toyocamycin by 7-cyano-7-deazaguanine syn-
thase (HuiB and ToyM), whereas decarboxylation by
decarboxylase (TubF) leads to the structure of tubercidin.
This indicates that enzymes modifying the 7-carboxyl group in
CDG are likely key drivers of the structural diversity of 7-
deazapurine natural products.
The formation of amide bonds is a common strategy in
natural product biosynthesis to assemble structurally diverse
molecules from relatively simple precursors, such as amino
acids. Enzymatic formation of an amide bond between a
carboxylic acid and an amine substrate requires activation of
the carboxyl group to make the overall reaction thermody-
namically favorable. This activation is typically driven by ATP,
either through hydrolysis to AMP and pyrophosphate (PPi) or
to ADP and inorganic phosphate (Pi).12,13 There are two
major classes of stand-alone, ATP-dependent amide-forming
enzymes in nature that directly couple carboxylic acid and
amine substrates: ATP-grasp enzymes, which form acyl-
phosphate intermediates, and amide bond synthetases, which
form acyl-adenylate intermediates (Figure S2). Of these, ATP-
grasp enzymes have received the most attention due to their
frequent occurrence in both primary and secondary metabo-

Here, we report the discovery of a new family of amide bond
synthetases that catalyze amide bond formation between CDG
and various amine substrates, which we have named
deazapurine amide bond synthetases (DABSs). Deletion of
the major antibiotic daptomycin biosynthetic gene cluster
(BGC) in Streptomyces roseosporus significantly increased the
production of cryptic metabolites. Structural elucidation and
biosynthetic study of these metabolites, named roseomycins,
led to the discovery of a new amide synthetase, RoyL, which
catalyzes the coupling of CDG with the ε-amine of L-lysine to
yield peptidyl deazapurine structures. Gene mining for RoyL
homologs revealed the wide distribution of DABSs across the
bacterial kingdom. Phylogenetic analysis, combined with in
vivo functional characterization, showed that DABS enzymes
clade into four distinct evolutionary groups, each associated
with a specific amine substrate, including ammonia, L-lysine, L-
ornithine, or D-Ala-D-Ala. This work demonstrates that the
structural diversification of peptidyl deazapurine natural
products is driven by the evolution of DABSs. Given that
most of the DABS-associated BGCs remain cryptic, systematic
characterization of these BGCs may uncover novel peptidyl
deazaguanine natural products with diverse biological activities.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Discovery of Roseomycins. Previous research suggested
that hidden metabolites in actinomycetes can be discovered by
the deletion of major antibiotics.17 To explore this possibility,
we deleted the major antibiotic daptomycin BGC in
Streptomyces roseosporus.18,19 This deletion led to the increased
production of five metabolites, which were classified into three
groups (A−C) based on their molecular weights and UV
spectra (Figures 1A and S3). The production of metabolites in
Groups A, B, and C was enhanced by 557, 225, and 184%,
respectively (Figure 1B). Since S. roseosporus possesses a total

from S. roseosporus, suggesting that they are potentially new
(Figure S4). To elucidate their structures, we isolated these
two metabolites from the cultures of the high-yielding S.
roseosporus Δdpt strain. Comprehensive 1D and 2D NMR
analyses (Figures S23−S32 and Table S5) revealed the
structures of 1a and 1b as new peptidyl deazapurine natural
products, which we named roseomycins A (1a) and B (1b)
(Figure 1C and Supplementary discussion). The roseomycin
structures are characterized by an amide bond between the 7-
carboxyl group of CDG and the ε-amine of lysine. Notably,
roseomycin B (1b) contains a desaturated lysine with a double
bond between the γ and δ carbons, representing a unique
structural feature.
Heterologous Expression of the Roseomycin BGC.
Since roseomycin structures contain a CDG moiety, we
searched the S. roseosporus genome for a BGC containing genes
required for CDG biosynthesis to identify the putative
roseomycin BGC. Previous studies have shown that CDG
biosynthesis requires three enzymes, including GTP cyclo-
hydrolase I (FolE), 6-carboxy-5,6,7,8-tetrahydropterin synthase
(QueD), and 7-carboxy-7-deazaguanine synthase (QueE).1
These three genes are highly conserved in BGCs of CDG-
derived natural products. We identified one cryptic BGC (SR-
BGC3) that contains all three CDG biosynthesis genes
(Figures 2A and S5). To determine whether SR-BGC3 is

Given the overall structural similarity of roseomycins to
dihydrofolate, we tested their inhibitory activity against human
dihydrofolate reductase (hDHFR), a well-known molecular
target in anticancer chemotherapy.24 The results showed that
roseomycins exhibit moderate inhibitory activity against
hDHFR compared to the well-known inhibitor methotrexate,
with roseomycin D (3b) displaying the strongest activity
(Figure S21).
Proposed Biosynthesis of Roseomycins. With the BGC
in hand, we next sought to propose the biosynthetic pathway
of roseomycins based on gene deletion studies (Figures 3 and
S9). The roy cluster contains three genes of royK/royJ/royI,
which are homologous to toyB/toyC/toyD from the toyoca-
mycin BGC and were therefore assigned roles in the CDG core
biosynthesis. The roy cluster also contains three genes of ATP-
grasp enzymes (royF, royM, and royP), which are well-known
amide bond-forming enzymes characterized by three conserved
domains: an N-terminal A-domain, a central B-domain, and a
C-terminal C-domain.25 Roseomycin D (3b), the end product
of the roseomycin biosynthetic pathway contains two distinct
amide bonds: one between CDG and desaturated Lys, and the
other between desaturated Lys and β-OH-Asn. To identify

modification of Lys and Asn (Figure 3D). RoyG shares 36%

RoyL, an enzyme that shares 43% a.a. identity with ToyM, a
nitrile-forming enzyme involved in toyocamycin biosynthesis.
ToyM, a member of the amide synthetase family, has been
known to convert the 7-carboxyl group of CDG to a nitrile
through a two-step mechanism involving an amide inter-

interaction with amine substrates, while R104, D138, and

substrate specificity control of RoyL.

Phylogenetic Analysis and Functional Character-
ization of the DABS Enzyme Family. Since RoyL exhibits
a new amid-bonding activity between CDG as a carboxyl
substrate and the side-chain amines of basic amino acids, we
designated this enzyme as a 7-deazapurine amide bond
synthetase (DABS). Given the potential existence of DABSs
with alternative amine-substrate specificities in nature, we next
aimed to explore the diversity of this enzyme family through
phylogenetic-guided gene mining. Using the EFI-Enzyme
Similarity Tool ,32 we searched the UniProt database with a
30% sequence identity cutoff, yielding 971 hits from the
bacterial kingdom, including 108 members from the phylum
Actinobacteria. To assess the evolutionary relationship among
these members, we constructed a maximum-likelihood
phylogenetic tree with all 957 sequences, along with QueC
from E. coli, QueC from B. subtilis, and ToyM from S. rimosus
as reference sequences (Figures 6A and S17). The resulting
tree revealed two major evolutionary clades, which we
designated as clades A (red) and B (blue). Both RoyL and
ToyM fell within Clade B, and thus, we focused our analysis on
this clade. Clade B contained a total of 49 DABSs, which was
divided again into four subclades, designated B1 −B4 (Figure
S18). All the BGCs containing Clade B DABSs also harbor a
conserved CDG (4) operon composed of royI, royJ, and royK
homologues (Figure 6B). This suggests that Clade B DABSs
are likely to use CDG as their sole carboxyl substrate, with
variation likely occurring in the amine substrates, as both RoyL
(Clade B2) and ToyM (Clade B1) fell into different subclades.
Surprisingly, all of the BGCs containing Clade B DABS genes
are cryptic, except for the roseomycin and toyocamycin BGCs,
hindering the identification of amine substrates for Clades B3
and B4.
To explore the functional diversity of DABS enzymes in
nature, we next aimed to characterize representative members
from each phylogenetic subclade. To this end, we constructed
a CDG-producing cassette by cloning the royK, royJ, and royI
genes under the synthetic promoter A1033 (Figure 6B). We
then selected one DABS gene from each of the four subclades:
Clade B1 from Streptomyces rimosus ATCC 14673, Clade B2
from Streptomyces sp. NRRL F-5755, Clade B3 from
Streptomyces sp. AG109-O5-10, and Clade B4 from Strepto-
myces rapamycinicus NRRL 5491. These genes were PCR-
amplified or chemically synthesized and cloned upstream of
royK for coexpression with the CDG cassette. The resulting
constructs were introduced into the heterologous host S. albus
J1074 for functional characterization (Figure 6C). As expected,
the Clade B1 member, clustered with ToyM, used NH3 as an
amine substrate to produce ADG (7-amino-7-deazaguanine,

1D and 2D NMR analyses (Figures S42−S56). Structural
elucidation revealed that the Clade B3 member primarily uses
N-acetylated ornithine (Nα-Ac-Orn) as an amine substrate,
although overlapping in substrate spectrum with the Clade B2
members. In contrast, the Clade B4 member exclusively utilizes
Ala-Ala as its amine substrate. Marfey’s analysis determined the
amino acid configuration as Nα-Ac-L-Orn and
D-Ala-D-Ala
(Figure S20). This finding is notable, as D-Ala-D-Ala ligase,
involved in peptidoglycan biosynthesis, belongs to the ATP-
grasp enzyme family, and this represents the first example of an
amide synthetase using D-Ala-D-Ala as an amine substrate.
Moreover, many BGCs associated with Clade B4 DABS
contain an additional copy of D-Ala-D-Ala ligase genes, further
supporting D-Ala-D-Ala as a true substrate for Clade B4 DABS
enzymes. Our phylogenetic analysis, combined with functional
characterization of DABSs, suggests that this enzyme family,
likely originally diverged from the PreQ0 biosynthesis pathway
in primary metabolism, has diversified into multiple subfamilies
by evolving distinct amine substrate specificities while retaining
CDG as the conserved carboxyl substrate. Since most DABS-
associated BGCs remain cryptic in bacterial genomes,
systematic characterization of these BGCs would lead to the
discovery of novel natural products with diverse biological
activities.
■CONCLUSION
Peptidylation is a common strategy found in nature for
diversifying natural product structures, utilizing a diverse range
of amino acids as biosynthetic building blocks, including both
standard and nonstandard amino acids.34 This strategy has
been observed in various uridyl nucleoside natural products,
including muraymycins, nikkomycins, and polyoxins.35 How-
ever, peptidylation has not been previously observed in
deazapurine natural products, which limits their structural
diversity. The discovery of roseomycins represents the first
example of peptidyl deazapurine natural products. Biosynthetic
studies of roseomycins revealed the involvement of two
distinct amide-bond-forming enzymes. The ATP-grasp enzyme
RoyF catalyzes the formation of an amide bond between
desaturated
L-Lys and β-OH-Asn, whereas the amide
synthetase RoyL catalyzes the amide bond formation between
CDG and desaturated L-Lys. RoyL represents a new amide
synthetase subfamily, which we designated as deazapurine
amide bond synthetases (DABSs). This enzyme specifically
utilizes CDG as its sole carboxyl substrate while accommodat-
ing diverse amine substrates, thereby contributing to the
structural diversification of deazapurine natural products.
Phylogenetic analysis, combined with functional character-
ization provides evolutionary insights into the diversification of
DABS enzymes. Our in vitro enzyme assays revealed that RoyL
utilizes L-Lys as a major substrate while retaining residual
nitrile-forming activity with ammonia. This suggests that
DABS enzymes likely evolved from ancestral nitrile synthetases
through changes in their substrate specificity toward amino
acids. Phylogenetic analysis of DABS enzymes revealed the
presence of four distinct subclades, designated Clades B1−B4,
each defined by its unique amine substrates: ammonia for
Clade B1, L-Lys for Clade B2, L-ornithine for Clade B3 and D-
Ala-D-Ala for Clade B4. Notably, DABS enzymes from Clades
B2 and B3 specifically utilize the side-chain amines of L-Lys (ε-
amine) and L-Orn (δ-amine), respectively, as amine substrates.
Remarkably, Clade B4 DABS also represents the first amide
synthetase to use the dipeptide
D-Ala-D-Ala as an amine
substrate. Polyoxins and nikkomycins are another family of
peptidyl nucleoside natural products. In their biosynthesis, the
ATP-grasp enzymes PolG and NikS have been shown to
catalyze amide bond formation between identical amino
nucleosides as amine substrates but distinct peptidyl moieties
as carboxyl substrates: carbamoyl polyoxamic acid and 4’-
hydroxy-2’-pyridinyl homothreonine, respectively.36 Notably,
these two distinct families of nucleoside natural products
employ different amide-bond-forming enzymes to diversify
peptidyl moieties through opposing substrate diversification
strategies: diversification of amine substrates in DABS enzymes
and diversification of carboxyl substrates in ATP-grasp
enzymes (Figure S22).
Overall, our study suggests that DAB enzymes constitute a
novel enzyme family involved in natural product biosynthesis,
characterized by the formation of amide bonds between the
CDG carboxylic acid and structurally diverse amine substrates.
We believe that systematic characterization of DABS-
associated BGCs will facilitate the discovery of novel
nucleoside natural products with diverse biological activities,
which is currently underway.
