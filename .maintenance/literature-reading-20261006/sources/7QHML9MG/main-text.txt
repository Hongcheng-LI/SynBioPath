ABSTRACT: Orthoester-containing natural products possess unique oxygen-rich architectures; however, the enzymatic mechanism
responsible for constructing these functional groups has remained unclear. In this study, we report the structure−function analysis of
NvfE, a nonheme iron enzyme from Aspergillus novofumigatus, which catalyzes the Fe(II)-dependent isomerization of a reactive
endoperoxide to generate the orthoester fumigatonoid C. Structural analysis of NvfE revealed that, although structurally similar to
Fe(II)/αKG-dependent oxygenases, NvfE lacks the canonical αKG-binding pocket and instead features a nonconserved Glu149
residue that governs substrate recognition. Site-directed mutagenesis and QM/MM calculations confirmed the critical role of Glu149
in catalysis. Notably, Glu149 variants produced an alternative orthoester isomer, indicating its importance for product selectivity.
Based on these results, we propose a mechanism for the unique αKG-free NvfE-catalyzed orthoester formation reaction. These
results unveil a remarkable catalytic strategy for orthoester biogenesis and demonstrate the functional diversification of nonheme
iron enzymes beyond oxidative chemistry.
■INTRODUCTION
Orthoesters are rare and unique functional groups charac-
terized by a central carbon atom bonded to three alkoxy
substituents. Orthoesters are found in natural products such as
daphnetoxins,1,2 plant limonoids,3 the potent neurotoxin
tetrodotoxin,4 the orthosomycin class of oligosaccharide
antibiotics,5 including hygromycin B, everninomicin, and
avilamycin, and the fungal meroterpenoids austalide6 and
novofumigatonin.7 Fe(II)/α-ketoglutarate (αKG)-dependent
oxygenases catalyze orthoester formation in the biosynthesis of
various orthoester compounds5−7 (Figure S1). In orthosomy-
cins, the enzymes EvdO1 and EvdO2 (everninomicin biosyn-
thesis), AviO1 (avilamycin biosynthesis), and HygX (hygrom-
ycin B biosynthesis) catalyze the oxidative cyclizations to
construct the orthoester linkage5 (Figure S1). In the austalide
biosynthetic pathway,6 AstB catalyzes the C5-hydroxylation of
13-O-deacetylaustalide I to yield austalide T, which exists in
equilibrium with its orthoester form (Figure S1). This
equilibrium is then locked by AstL, a methyltransferase that
methylates the resulting dialkyl orthoester. These Fe(II)/αKG-
dependent oxygenases typically employ αKG and molecular
oxygen as cosubstrates to catalyze these transformations.
In the biosynthesis of novofumigatonin,7 NvfE, which was
initially annotated as an Fe(II)/αKG-dependent oxygenase,
catalyzes the isomerization of the highly reactive endoperoxide
intermediate 1 into the orthoester 2 (Figure 1). Interestingly,
although NvfE shares sequence homology with Fe(II)/αKG-
dependent oxygenases, previous biochemical studies revealed
that NvfE does not require αKG for the enzyme reaction.
Sequence comparisons with canonical Fe(II)/αKG-dependent
oxygenases involved in fungal meroterpenoid biosynthesis
revealed that a conserved active-site glutamine residue,
typically responsible for αKG binding, is uniquely replaced
by a glutamate (Glu149) in NvfE8−11 (Figure S2). This

■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Characterization of αKG-Binding Ability and Metal
Selectivity of NvfE. To gain insight into the cofactor
requirements and catalytic mechanism of NvfE, we investigated
its αKG-binding affinity, molecular oxygen requirement, and
divalent metal ion selectivity. First, we used isothermal titration
calorimetry (ITC) to test whether NvfE binds to αKG (Figure
2A,B). For the comparison, NvfF,7 an Fe(II)/αKG-dependent
oxygenase in the novofumigatonin biosynthetic pathway (42%
sequence identity with NvfE), was used as the positive control
of an αKG-binding enzyme (Figure 1). While NvfF showed
significant αKG binding with a dissociation constant (KD) of
446.2 μM (Figure 2B), no detectable αKG binding was
observed for NvfE under the same conditions, and no αKG
was detectable from denatured NvfE (Figure S3). Next, to
examine the oxygen requirement of the NvfE-catalyzed
isomerization, we conducted in vitro enzyme reactions of
NvfE and NvfF, the latter of which catalyzes an oxygen-
dependent oxidative rearrangement, under anaerobic con-
ditions (Figure 2C). In contrast to NvfF, no substantial
differences in the enzymatic activity of NvfE were observed
compared to reactions performed under aerobic conditions,
indicating that the NvfE reaction does not require molecular
oxygen (Figures 2C and S4).
NvfE exhibits significant enzymatic activity, even in the
presence of 20 mM EDTA, reflecting its tight Fe(II) binding.
In fact, an inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry
(ICP-MS) analysis showed that purified NvfE retained 21%
iron occupancy even after EDTA treatment (Figure S5). To
further investigate the metal dependency of NvfE, the H152A
variant was constructed to disrupt the His2-Asp1 facial triad for
Fe(II)-binding. Although this variant still retained substantial
enzymatic activity in the presence of Fe(II), a preincubation
with EDTA reduced the iron occupancy and abolished the
enzymatic activity in the absence of externally supplied Fe(II)
(Figure S5A,C). Therefore, the metal dependency of NvfE was
analyzed by using the EDTA-treated NvfE H152A variant.
Interestingly, NvfE retained the isomerase activity in the
presence of Fe(II), Mn(II), Co(II), or Ni(II) (Figure S6), but
no activity was detected with Cu(II), Zn(II), or Fe(III). While
the redox potentials of Mn(II), Co(II), and Ni(II) (1.54, 1.92,
and 2.3 V, respectively) are higher than that of Fe(II) (0.77 V),
reductive cleavage of the endoperoxide bridge in artemisinin
has nevertheless been observed with less reducing metal ions
such as Cu(II) (2.4 V).12,13 In addition, ring opening of 1,2-
dioxane by Co(II) salen complexes has also been demon-
strated.14 Furthermore, the isomerase activity of wild-type
NvfE decreased significantly in the presence of H2O2. (Figure
S7). The observed metal promiscuity and oxidant susceptibility
suggest that the metal serves as a redox-active center, changing
between the +2 and +3 oxidation states to facilitate electron
transfer during the enzyme reaction, although the possibility
that the addition of H2O2 induces enzyme inactivation through
oxidative structural changes cannot be ruled out.
Structural Analysis of NvfE. To obtain structural insights
into the NvfE-catalyzed orthoester-forming reaction, we
determined the crystal structure of NvfE complexed with
Fe(II) at 2.33 Å resolution (Figure S8 and Table S1). The
overall structure of NvfE possesses a double-stranded β-helix
(DSBH) fold with a conserved funnel-like reaction cham-
ber.15,16 NvfE exists as a homodimer, constructed mainly
through hydrogen-bonding interactions between the residues
from each monomer. NvfE exhibits structural similarity to
canonical Fe(II)/αKG-dependent oxygenases, such as AndA,8
which catalyzes sequential desaturation and isomerization
reactions in the biosynthesis of anditomins (PDB ID: 5ZM4,
RMSD value of 2.3 Å for 294 Cα atoms) and PrhA,9 which
catalyzes sequential desaturation and ring expansion reactions
in the biosynthesis of paraherquonin (PDB ID: 5YBO, RMSD
value of 2.3 Å for 294 Cα atoms).
In the active site of NvfE, the iron is coordinated by a
His152-Asp154-His234 facial triad that is highly conserved in
Fe(II)/αKG-dependent oxygenases (Figure 3). Additionally,
two water molecules are coordinated to the iron, opposite the
sites of His152 and His234. In canonical Fe(II)/αKG-
dependent oxygenases, a highly conserved Gln residue is
positioned near the iron center to assist in αKG recog-
nition.8−11,17−20
In contrast, in NvfE, conserved Gln is characteristically
substituted with Glu149, which interacts indirectly with iron
via an iron-coordinated water molecule. This interaction is

The main structural differences between NvfE and the
related Fe(II)/αKG-dependent oxygenases AndA and PrhA
mainly lie in the three loop regions surrounding the active site
(Figure S9). One key region is loop A, a lid-like loop that is
highly flexible and encapsulates the meroterpenoid substrate in
AndA and PrhA. Notably, loop A of NvfE, consisting of
residues Ser70 to Cys83, is significantly shorter than the
corresponding loops in AndA and PrhA. The electron density
of loop A in AndA is only observed in the substrate-bound
(closed) conformation. In contrast, the density of this loop in
NvfE is detected even in the absence of a substrate in
monomer A, suggesting that NvfE naturally adopts a closed
active site. This closed conformation is stabilized by a
hydrogen bond between Tyr78 and Glu149, which anchors
loop A in a fixed position (Figure S9D). Loop B, comprising
residues Pro288 to Gly294 from the adjacent NvfE monomer,
is also involved in active site construction and shorter than
those of AndA and PrhA. Finally, NvfE possesses a distinct and
significantly elongated characteristic loop C (Thr115 and
Ser130) that originates from the adjacent monomer and
extends near the active site. These unique structural features,
particularly the inherently closed conformation of the active
site in apo NvfE, are likely key contributors to its distinct
catalytic function compared with canonical Fe(II)/αKG-
dependent oxygenases. Despite the lack of αKG-binding
affinity, NvfE possesses an Arg246 residue conserved in
Fe(II)/αKG-dependent oxygenases that interacts with the
carboxylate group of αKG.8−11,17−20 In Fe(II)/αKG-depend-
ent oxygenases, αKG binding induces conformational changes
of the conserved Arg residue and two β-sheets as well as the
amino acid side chains on the β-sheets (Figure S10). These
structural rearrangements create a well-defined αKG-binding
pocket that stabilizes the interactions with αKG. A conserved
hydrophobic residue occupies this pocket. For example,
Leu123 in AndA and Ile118 in PrhA contribute to forming a
hydrophobic environment to accommodate the methylene
moiety of αKG (Figure S10B−G). The corresponding pocket
in NvfE is similar in size to those in the apo forms of AndA and
PrhA (Figure S10A). However, in contrast to the hydrophobic
residues in AndA and PrhA, this position in NvfE is substituted
with Ser140, a polar amino acid with a smaller side chain.
Along with the characteristic replacement of the conserved Gln
residue involved in αKG binding by Glu149, these
substitutions introduce electrostatic repulsion toward the
carboxylate group of αKG and eliminate hydrophobic
interactions. Together, these differences likely account for
the lack of αKG-binding affinity in NvfE.
QM/MM Insights into the Mechanism of NvfE-
Catalyzed Orthoester Formation. First, substrate 1 was
docked into the catalytic pocket of wild type NvfE, where it is
anchored by hydrogen-bonding interactions with Tyr78,
Ser140, and Arg246 (Figure 3B). Based on the docked
complex, 100 ns MD simulations were performed. Afterward, a
representative snapshot from the final stage of the MD
trajectory was chosen for the subsequent QM/MM calcu-
lations (Figure 4).
In line with previous studies on nonheme iron enzymes, our
calculations (Table S4) showed that the quintet state is the
ground state.20−24 Therefore, all calculations are focused on
the quintet spin surface. In the QM/MM-optimized reactant
complex RC, the Oa atom of the substrate is positioned near
the iron center, at a distance of 2.88 Å. Starting from RC, the
Fe(II) center mediates the reductive cleavage of the substrate
O−O bond through a single-electron transfer (SET) process,
leading to the Fe−O linked and O-centered radical
intermediate Int1, where the O atom carries a spin population
of −0.74. This step experiences an energy barrier of 13.0 kcal/
mol (RC →TS1) and an exothermicity of 3.6 kcal/mol (RC
→Int1). Then, the C1−C2 bond in Int1 undergoes facile
cleavage with a small barrier of 6.1 kcal/mol (Int1 →TS2), to
form Int2, a C2-centered radical intermediate. Notably, Int2 is
20.2 kcal/mol lower in energy than Int1. This stabilization
primarily arises because the Ob-centered radical species in Int1
is much less stable than the tertiary carbon radical in Int2.
Subsequently, a SET occurs from the C2-centered radical in
Int2 to the Fe(III) species, generating a C2-centered cation.

observed in reactions of artemisinins and plakortins with heme.
This peroxide bridge cleavage by heme, followed by the
formation of oxygen radicals leading to broad-spectrum protein
adducts, is believed to be the action mode responsible for the
antimalarial activity of artemisinins.25,26 In this reaction,
cleavage also proceeds via SET from Fe(II) and generates
radicals; however, controlling these radicals is difficult,
resulting in a variety of decomposition products. In contrast,
NvfE utilizes nonheme iron instead of heme and fixes the
substrate conformation within its active site, allowing a similar
Fe(II)-mediated reaction but producing only product 2
specifically.
NvfE Mutagenesis Experiments. Based on the docking
model and proposed mechanism, NvfE variants of His84,
Tyr78, Ser140, His152, Arg246, and Glu261 were constructed
to further confirm the key residues for the interaction with
substrate 1 (Figures 5A and S12). As a result, the single
substitutions of Ser140, Arg246, and Glu261 with Ala
dramatically reduced the isomerase activity to 5, 23, and
13% of the wild type, respectively. Notably, the loss of activity
in the R246A variant (23% of the wild type activity) was
restored in the R246 K variant (95% of the wild type activity),
indicating the importance of a positively charged residue at this
position. The substitution of His84 with Ala led to a modest
reduction in enzyme activity (63% compared to that of the
wild type). These observations are quite consistent with the
docking results.
Meanwhile, the substitution of Tyr78, which is postulated to
maintain the closed conformation of the active site through its
interaction with Glu149, resulted in significantly reduced
isomerase activity in both the Y78A and Y78F variants (6 and
19% of wild-type activity, respectively). Furthermore, the
Y78F/E149Q double variant retained 1% of the wild-type
activity, suggesting that the proper anchorage of the lid-like
loop A, the most prominent structural feature of the NvfE
active site, is critical for the initial stages of the NvfE catalytic
cycle such as substrate positioning but not important for the
isomerization mechanism itself. To further investigate the role
of Glu149 in NvfE catalysis, a site saturation mutagenesis study
was conducted at position 149. All variants exhibited
significantly reduced enzymatic activities (relative activities =
3−16%), indicating the importance of the glutamate at
position 149 and suggesting the participation of Glu149 in
substrate binding through hydrogen bonding or iron binding
via a water molecule (Figures 5E and S13). Additionally, no
oxygenase activity was observed with these variants, even in the
presence of αKG.
Interestingly, the E149D variant newly produced an isomeric
product 3, in addition to the wild type product 2, whereas this
variant exhibited reduced activity compared to the wild type
(9.8% relative to wild type) (Figures 5B and S13). Comparison
with an authentic standard identified compound 3 as asnovolin
G, another orthoester-containing shunt metabolite7 (Figures
5C and S14). This compound is known to be generated
nonenzymatically via the acid-catalyzed isomerization of the
wild type product 2. While only trace amounts of compound 3

Among these NvfE variants, the kinetic parameters of the
H84A, S140A, E149D, R246A, and E261A variants were
further analyzed to understand their importance for the
reaction (Figure S16 and Table S2). Kinetic analyses revealed
that all variants exhibited significantly reduced catalytic
specificity (kcat/KM) compared to the wild type enzyme, in
which a 36- to 219-fold decrease was observed. In the case of
the E149D variant, a 2.1-fold increase in KM and a 103-fold
decrease in kcat were observed. These results clearly indicate
that Glu149 is essential for both substrate binding and catalytic
activity. While the KM values of all variants, except for E149D,
were comparable to that of the wild type enzyme, the kcat
values were dramatically reduced by 43-, 189-, 103-, 169-, and
154-fold in the H84A, S140A, E149D, R246A, and E261A
variants, respectively (Figure S16). The minimal changes
observed in the KM values suggest that substrate binding is
mediated through interactions with multiple residues. As a
result, single amino acid substitutions have a limited influence
on the overall substrate affinity. In contrast, the substantial
decreases in kcat indicate that these mutations likely disrupt the
correct positioning or conformation of the substrate within the
active site. This would prevent the effective interaction
between the endoperoxide moiety and the iron center, leading
to impaired catalytic turnover. These findings collectively
demonstrate that the examined residues play critical roles in
ensuring the proper substrate alignment for efficient catalysis.

Figure 6 shows the QM/MM calculated energy profile for
the E149D-NvfE variant. Starting from RC′, the initial three
reaction steps closely resemble those observed in wild type
NvfE (Figure 4), including (1) Fe(II)-mediated reductive
cleavage of the Oa−Ob bond, leading to the formation of the
Fe−Oa bond in Int1′; (2) homolytic cleavage of the C1−C2
bond, resulting in a tertiary carbon radical in Int2′; and (3)
formation of the C2−Ob bond, leading to the tertiary
carbocation intermediate Int3′. Following the introduction of
Int3′, two competing pathways may occur. In pathway a, C1−
Oa bond coupling leads to the formation of compound 2
(Int4a′), which involves a small barrier of 2.6 kcal/mol (Int3′
→TS4a′). In pathway b, Asp149 abstracts the proton from the
substrate Oc−H, which is coupled to C1−Oc bond formation
(Int3′ →TS4b′), leading to the formation of Int4b′. This step
involves a barrier of 4.2 kcal/mol, suggesting that the
formation of Int4a′ is kinetically favored over that of Int4b′.
Given the small energy difference between TS4a′ and TS4b′
(1.6 kcal/mol), Int4b′ can be formed as a minor product by
the E149D-NvfE variant, which is consistent with our
experiments. In addition, our calculations suggest that base
catalysis from Asp149 is the key to the formation of Int4b′,
which is also in line with our mutagenesis experiments,
showing that the Int4b′ side product is not detected with wild
type NvfE. Here, as the substrate is covalently coordinated to
Fe in Int4b′, we assume that the waters ligated to Fe may
mediate the protonation of the substrate Oa and facilitate the
substrate dissociation from the Fe center. As shown in Figure
7A, two water molecules are coordinated to Fe and may donate
a proton to the Oa atom. One favorable pathway involves
proton transfer from the water molecule proceeding through a
barrier of 8.4 kcal/mol (Int4b′ →TS5′). The resulting neutral
complex Int5′, is 2.0 kcal/mol more stable than Int4b′.
Another pathway that is kinetically less unfavorable is shown in
Figure S18. Since the active site is located near the protein
surface, the product in Int5′ may readily diffuse into the buffer
solution. Accordingly, the HEPES buffer may function as the
general acid−base catalyst to mediate further transformation to
Int6′. As shown in Figure 7B, the proton transfer from the
sulfonic acid group to the Og site, which is coupled to the
proton transfer from substrate Oa−Hb, can drive the
simultaneous C1−Oa bond formation and C1−Og bond
cleavage through TS6′. The step involves a barrier of 17.8
kcal/mol, ultimately yielding the final product 3 in PC′.
These calculations revealed that the E149D variant exhibits a
distinct substrate binding mode compared with the wild-type
enzyme. In the mutant, substitution of glutamate with the
shorter aspartate side chain disrupts the original hydrogen
bond with a water molecule, while enabling the formation of a
new hydrogen bond with the Oc−H group of the substrate.
This reconfigured hydrogen-bonding network facilitates
deprotonation of the Oc−H group and promotes C1−Oc
bond formation, ultimately leading to the generation of
compound 3. These results support the critical role of
Glu149 in the catalytic mechanism of the wild-type enzyme
and highlight the potential for further engineering of the
product specificity through additional mutations.
Phylogenetic analysis revealed that NvfE and its homologues
represent a clade discrete from known fungal meroterpenoid
biosynthetic enzymes (Figure S19A,B). Within this clade, the
hypothetical protein IFM53868_02454 from Aspergillus
udagawae (accession number GFF78869.1, 89% sequence
identity to NvfE) exhibits a similar Gln to Glu substitution and
is localized within a biosynthetic gene cluster that encodes for
a novofumigatonin derivative (Figure S19B,C). Other NvfE
homologues retain canonical features for αKG-binding and
thus likely function as Fe(II)/αKG-dependent oxygenases
(Figure S19A). Taken together, the restricted distribution and
lineage-specific evolution of NvfE homologues with Q149E
substitution highlight a unique trajectory of enzymatic
diversification, providing new insights into how noncanonical
Fe(II)/αKG-dependent enzymes can evolve novel catalytic
functions.
■CONCLUSIONS
The structure−function and calculation analyses of NvfE
revealed a unique mechanism for orthoester formation that
proceeds in an oxygen- and αKG-free manner. Compared with
canonical Fe(II)/αKG-dependent oxygenases, NvfE lacks key
features, including a hydrophobic pocket for αKG and a
conserved glutamine residue, resulting in the disruption of
αKG binding. Rather than generating a high-valent ferryl oxo
species, NvfE utilizes a redox-active Fe(II) cofactor to cleave
the endoperoxide. The subsequent rearrangement depends on
proper substrate alignment within the active site, which is
enforced by enzyme−substrate interactions as well as the
closed conformation of the active site. Here, Glu149 stands out
as a key catalytic residue involved in substrate binding,
conformational stabilization, and product selectivity. NvfE thus
appears to represent the evolutionary repurposing of an
oxidative oxygenase into a nonoxidative isomerase, expanding
the functional diversity of the nonheme iron enzyme family in
natural product biosynthesis.
