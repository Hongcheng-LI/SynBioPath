ABSTRACT: Arginine-containing cyclodipeptide synthases
(RCDPSs) from fungi constitute a new family of tRNA-dependent
enzymes that can biosynthesize cyclo-Arg-Xaa dipeptides. The
incorporation of an arginine residue significantly expands the chemical
space of guanidine-containing natural products. Here, we mined fungal
biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) containing different RCDPS to
discover aza-polycyclic natural products. The pno BGC from Aspergillus
pseudonomius produced pentacyclic pyrroloindoline diketopiperazines
(DKPs) of which the arginine side chain is oxidatively cyclized into a
guanidino-proline. Two RCDPS-encoding BGCs, car and esh from
Aspergillus carlsbadensis and Eupenicillium shearii, respectively, produced
DKPs connected to five- and seven-membered spirocycles as a result of oxidative cyclization of the guanidino group catalyzed by α-
ketoglutarate/Fe(II)-dependent oxygenases. The esh pathway involves a tandem cyclization and epimerization to generate the
guanidino-bridged tricyclo-[3.31,2.2.2]-piperazinedione core. The aza-polycyclic structures characterized in this work demonstrate
the potential of using RCDPS as a starting point for the discovery of new natural products.
A
rginine derived aza-polycyclic compounds such as
tetrodotoxin,1 saxitoxin,2 and streptothricin F3 (Figure
1A) have received attention due to their strong biological
activities and structural complexity. Cyclization of the arginine
side chain typically involves oxidation of β or γ positions,
which leads to formation of six- or five-membered rings,
respectively (Figure S1).4−9 Compared to other basic amino
acids such as lysine, ornithine, or histidine, however, arginine is
used less frequently as a natural product building block.
Genome mining efforts using enzymes involved in arginine
incorporation can therefore lead to the discovery of new
guanidine-containing aza-polycyclic natural products.
Toward this objective, we focused on using a newly
discovered family of arginine-containing cyclodipeptide
synthases (RCDPSs) from fungi as beacons for genome
mining.10 Members of this family catalyze the formation of
arginine-containing diketopiperazines (DKPs) using amino-
acylated-tRNA as an amino acid donor. The first example was
discovered in the biosynthetic pathway of the pseudopeptide
NK13650, in which AnkA generates cyclo-L-Arg-L-Tyr (cRY)
(Figure S2). Searching of fungal genomes using AnkA showed
homologues are widely found in fungal biosynthetic gene
clusters (BGCs), nearly all with uncharacterized products.
Expression of different RCDPs and assaying the DPK products
led to diverse cyclo-L-Arg-L-Xaa products, in which Xaa
represents different proteinogenic amino acids, including L-
Trp, L-Leu, L-Pro, etc. The discovery of RCDPSs expanded the
chemical space of natural DKPs, as arginine-containing DPKs
are rarely found in nature.
In this work, we targeted genome mining efforts on RCDPS-
containing BGCs that also encode C−H hydroxylating
enzymes such as non-heme, α-ketoglutarate/Fe(II)-dependent
oxygenase (α-KG) and cytochrome P450 monooxygenase
(P450). We reasoned that these enzymes can lead to oxidative
cyclization of the arginine side chain in the DKP. By examining
three such BGCs, new aza-polycyclic compounds were
discovered (Figure 1B) and the biosynthetic pathways were
characterized.

flavin-dependent oxygenase (FMO, PnoD), and N-acyltrans-
ferase (N-AT, PnoE) (Figure 2A, Table S1). PnoA showed the

scale cultivation of this strain led to isolation of 2, which was
structurally determined to be cyclo-Trp-Pro with a guanidino
group at C-5 (Figures S19−S24 and Table S6). Therefore,
P450 PnoB catalyzes C−N bond formation between C-5 and
N-10 of 1 to form the guanidino-proline moiety in 2 (Figure
2C). P450s that catalyze C−N bond formation have been
reported, including TleB,18−21 NzeB,22,23 OkaD,24 and FrzC25
(Figure S4). One proposed mechanism for these enzymes
involves consecutive H atom abstractions to generate the N-
centered and C-centered diradical species, which can be
followed by radical coupling to form the C−N bond.
Alternatively, in the case of StaN26 in the biosynthesis of
staurosporin, it was proposed that StaN catalyzes C-
hydroxylation and dehydration of the sugar moiety to form
an oxonium, followed by a nucleophilic attack of the indole
nitrogen to form the C−N bond. Here, we propose that PnoB
performs hydroxylation at C-5 of 1 to form a hemiaminal,
which undergoes dehydration to yield iminium that can be
attacked by the amide N-10, forming the C−N bond and the
guanidino-proline moiety in 2 (Figure S5).
Expression of PnoA and the prenyltransferase PnoC
produced a product (S1) with MWT that is 68 Da higher
than that of 1 (Figure S3). This compound was not
characterized and is proposed to be the prenylated derivative
of 1. Coexpression of PnoABC produced compound 3, which
was identified to be the C-2′ reverse prenylated product of 2
(Figures S25−S30 and Table S7). Further coexpression of the
FMO PnoD resulted in the formation of compound 4, which
was identified to be the pentacyclic product that contains the
3-hydroxypyrroloindolenine structure (Figure 2B, ii, Figures
S31−S36, and Table S8). The reactions catalyzed by PnoC
and PnoD closely resemble the modification steps involved in
the biosynthesis of notoamides and brevianamides:27 (i)
reverse prenylation of indole at C-2′ by the prenyltransferase
and (ii) FMO-catalyzed 2′,3′-β-face epoxidation which leads to
formation of the indole iminium that is attacked by the N-13′
amide to form the pyrroloindoline. The last step in the
biosynthesis of 5 is acetylation of the guanidine group in 4 by
the N-AT PnoE. Acetylation of guanidine is rare in natural
product biosynthesis, and this modification resembles the
function of another N-AT that acetylates the product of a
previously characterized RCDPS product.10 Therefore, it
appears acetylation of the arginine side chain in cyclo-Arg-
Xaa may be a recurring tailoring step.
Next, we examined the role of coclustered α-KG/Fe(II)-
dependent oxygenases in RCDPS BGCs. The car BGC from A.
carlsbadensis was identified to encode an RCDPS (CarA) and
an α-KG/Fe(II)-dependent oxygenase (CarB) (Figure 3A and
Table S2). Flanking genes did not encode putative biosynthesis
enzymes. CarB displays moderate sequence identity (∼50%) to
LolO in the biosynthetic pathway of loline.28,29 LolO catalyzes
tandem oxidation reactions on the bicyclic pyrrolizidine
intermediate to generate an ether bridge that results in the
strained caged structure found in loline (Figure S6). The car
BGC displays sequence homology to other RCDPS-containing
BGCs, including the esh BGC from Eupenicillium shearii
(Figure 3B). The esh BGC encodes the anchoring RCDPS
(EshA), two α-KG/Fe(II)-dependent oxygenases (EshB and
EshD), and a putative epimerase (EshC) (Figure 3B and Table
S3). Both EshB and EshD showed ∼60% sequence identity to
CarB. HHpred analysis of EshC showed high structural
identity (99.9%) to the racemase AfIIR,30 which epimerizes
the α-carbon stereochemistry of allantoin (Figure S7).
Expressing the RCDPS CarA in A. nidulans resulted in the
formation of cyclo-L-Arg-L-Pro (6, cRP) that was identical to a
synthetic standard (Figure 3C, ii). Coexpression of CarB with
CarA resulted in a new metabolite 7 with a MWT of 251,
which is 2 Da less than that of 6 (Figure 3C, iii). However, the
titer of 7 from the heterologous expression was insufficient for
isolation and structural characterization. This led to an effort to
isolate 7 from large scale enzymatic reaction using purified
CarB obtained from E. coli overexpression. Incubation of
recombinant CarB using synthetically prepared 6 and required
cofactors of α-KG/Fe(II)-dependent oxygenase led to the
formation of two products 7 and 8 with the same MWT, of

chemical shifts with either S- or R-configuration at C-2. With
both compounds, the observed NMR chemical shifts matched

The proposed biosynthetic pathways to 7 and 8 are shown
in Figure 3E. Following CarA-catalyzed formation of cRP (6),
CarB is proposed to act as a desaturase to convert 6 to the
imine-containing 9. This reaction could proceed through
radical-mediated hydroxylation at C-2 followed by dehydration
to form 9. Nucleophilic attack of either N-6 or N-8 of the
arginine side chain results in the formation of 7 and 8,
respectively. With the stereoselective formation of the (2R,
2′S) isomer in both compounds, the cyclization step is most
likely controlled in the active site of CarB. It remains unknown
why compound 8 is not observed under heterologous
expression conditions, although evidence from the study on
the esh pathway (vide infra) suggests the seven-membered ring
product 8 is more likely to ring open and form 9, while the
five-membered product 7 is more stable.
The structures of 7 and 8 revealed a new mode of cyclization
of the arginine side chain in cyclo-Arg-Xaa DKPs as a result of
the activities of an α-KG/Fe(II)-dependent oxygenase. Based
on the ∼65% sequence identify of CarB to EshB, we
hypothesized similar products can be obtained from the esh
pathway of which can be further modified by the remaining
enzymes in the BGC. To test this, the esh biosynthetic genes
were heterologously expressed in A. nidulans (Figure 4A).
Expression of only EshA led to the formation of cyclo-L-Arg-L-
Leu (10, cRL), which matched a synthetic standard (Figure
4A, iv). Expression of EshA and EshB resulted in two
compounds (11 and 12) with the same MWT (−2 Da less
than 10) and coeluted together (Figure 4A, iii). Similar to that
of 7, the titers of 11 and 12 were not sufficient for isolation.
Coexpression of EshA-C, however, resulted in the emergence
of a new product 13 that was at a higher titer (1.0 mg/L)
(Figure 4A, ii). Extensive NMR analysis of 13 (Table S14 and
Figures S69−S74) isolated from the scaled-up cultivation
revealed that 13 has an unprecedented guanidino-bridged
tricyclo-[3.31,2.2.2]-piperazinedione structure. Formation of
the cage-like structure in 13 requires two nitrogen atoms
from the guanidino group to form new C−N bonds with both
α-carbons (C-2 and C-2′) of 10. Finally, expression of the
entire esh pathway in A. nidulans led to the emergence of 14,
which is the C-3′ hydroxylated product of 13 (Table S15 and
Figures S75−S83). The (2R, 2′R) configurations of 14 were
determined by application of J-based configuration analysis31
(Figure S8), while the 3′R configuration was determined by
using the modified Mosher’s method32 (Figures S9, S84, and
S85). Formation of 14 in the presence of EshD established this
enzyme to be an αKG/Fe(II)-dependent hydroxylase (Figure
4C).
To gain more insight into the mechanism of tricyclo-
[3.31,2.2.2]-piperazinedione ring formation, EshB, EshC, and
EshD were expressed and purified from E. coli BL21(DE3).
Incubation of all three enzymes and necessary cofactors with
10 afforded 14 (Figure 4B, iv), confirming that all enzymes are
functional and the final product can be formed. Consistent
with in vivo results, the reaction of 10 in the presence of EshB
and EshC resulted in the formation of 13 (Figure 4B, iii).

Reacting 10 with only EshB resulted in the formation of 11
(minor) and 12 (major) (Figure 4B, ii), similar to the product
profile when CarB is incubated with 6. The two compounds
were isolated by a scaled up enzymatic reaction and
structurally characterized by NMR (Tables S12 and S13 and
Figures S59−S68), while the stereochemistry assignments were
aided by DFT calculations (Tables S18 and S19). Similar to
the structures of 7 and 8, 11 and 12 are five-membered and
seven-membered spirocycles with (2R, 2′S) stereochemistry,
respectively. Extensive efforts to crystallize these molecules for
structural confirmation did not succeed. However, a three-
dimensional structure of 11 was obtained by microcrystalline
electron diffraction (MicroED) (Table S20 and Figure S10),
which enabled the assignment of (2R, 2′S) absolute stereo-
chemistry, considering the 2′S configuration from the
incorporation of L-Leu.
These enzymatic assays suggested the role of the epimerase
EshC in the biosynthesis of 13. Epimerization of the C-2′
position from S to R by EshC is required for desaturation of
the C−N bond between C-2′ and N-7, which is also catalyzed
by EshB based on the assay results. Surprisingly, incubation of
11 with EshB and EshC led to no further reactions (Figure
S11), suggesting that 11 is an off-pathway shunt product. In
contrast, incubation of 12, which is relatively unstable, with
EshB and EshC led to the formation of 13 (Figure S13),
suggesting that the seven-membered spirocycle is an on-
pathway intermediate. In the presence of EshC alone, 12 can
be rapidly converted to a new product with the same MWT
(S2 in Figure S12). Following removal of EshC from the
reaction via ultrafiltration, addition of EshB to the filtrate
resulted in robust conversion of S2 to 13, whereas addition of
both EshB and EshD led to the formation of 14 (Figure S14).
Due to the instability of S2, we were unable to isolate the
compound for structural determination. Based on the
annotated function of EshC and the MWT of S2 (same as
12), we proposed the structure of S2 to be the (2R, 2′R)
epimer of 12.
Combining our biochemical results and isolated compounds,
a biosynthetic pathway from 10 to 13 can be proposed (Figure
4C). EshB is a multifunctional desaturase that functions twice,
interrupted by the function of the epimerase EshC. EshB first
converts 10 to imine intermediate 15, which can undergo
intramolecular cyclization to give either 11 or 12. The seven-
membered spirocycle 12 can be subjected to epimerization of
C-2′ to give the (2R, 2′R) product, which can ring open to 16
(Figure S15). It is possible that 15 may also be the substrate of
EshC to directly afford 16, in which case the conversion of 12
to 16 may involve 15 as an intermediate. It is proposed that 16
can undergo an EshB-mediated cyclization to 17, which is the
2′R epimer of 11. This on-pathway intermediate is then
subjected to further desaturation by EshB to give 18, followed
by a second C−N bond formation to complete tricyclo-
[3.31,2.2.2]-piperazinedione formation.
Lastly, to test substrate promiscuity of EshB and EshC, we
synthesized diastereomers of cRL {cyclo-L-Leu-D-Arg (S3),
cyclo-D-Leu-L-Arg (S4), and cyclo-D-Leu-D-Arg (S5)}, as well
as cyclo-L-Leu-L-Orn (S6). None of these compounds were
substrates to EshB and EshC as no new products were formed
(Figure S16).
■CONCLUSIONS
In summary, genome mining of RCDPS-containing BGCs led
to the discovery of novel aza-polycyclic compounds synthe-
sized via oxidative cyclization of the arginine side chain in
cyclo-Arg-Xaa DKPs. In the pno pathway, the P450 PnoB
catalyzed a new mode of arginine side chain cyclization in
which the α-amide nitrogen is involved. This resulted in the
formation of a guanidino-proline moiety that becomes a part of
the pentacyclic ring system in the product 5. Separately,
mining of BGCs containing α-KG/Fe(II)-dependent oxygen-
ase and RCDPS led to spirocyclic products as a result of
intramolecular cyclization between the α-carbon and the
guanidino group. The cascade of reactions unveiled for the
esh pathway is especially interesting, as the unprecedented
guanidino-bridged tricyclo-[3.31,2.2.2]-piperazinedione in 13
was generated via two oxidative C−N bond forming steps
separated by a required epimerization step. Collectively, this
work demonstrates the potential to discover new aza-polycyclic
natural products from fungi using arginine-specific RCDPS as
the starting point in genome mining.
■EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
General Experimental Procedures. NMR spectra were
measured by a Bruker Avance 500 spectrometer with a 5 mm dual
cryoprobe and Avance Neo 600 with a 5 mm prodigy broadband
cryoprobe at the UCLA Molecular Instrumentation Center (1H NMR
600 or 500 MHz, 13C NMR 125 MHz). The resonances of residual
DMSO (δH 2.49 and δC 39.7), pyridine (δH 8.71 and δC 123.5),
methanol (δH 3.30 and δC 49.0), HOD (δH 4.75), and acetonitrile (δH
1.93) were used as internal references for the 1H and 13C NMR
spectra. High resolution mass spectra were obtained from an Agilent
Quadrupole Time of Flight LC/MS instrument (6545 LC/Q-TOF)
at the UCLA Molecular Instrumentation Center. UV spectra were
recorded on a Shimadzu SPD-M20A photodiode array detector.
Optical rotations were measured on a Rudolph Research Analytical
Autopol III Automatic Polarimeter. LCMS analyses were performed
on an Agilent 6545 LC/Q-TOF with a reverse-phase column (Agilent
Poroshell, 120 EC-C18, 2.7 μm, 3.0 × 50 mm2) using positive-mode
electrospray ionization with a linear gradient of 1−95% acetonitrile−
water (containing 0.1% formic acid) in 9 min, followed by 95%
acetonitrile for 3 min with a flow rate of 0.8 mL min−1. In selected
samples, LCMS analyses were also performed on a Shimadzu 2020
EVLC−MS with a reverse-phase column (Waters, ACQUITY
Premier HSS T3, 1.8 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm2) using positive-mode
electrospray ionization with an isocratic flow of 1.5% acetonitrile−
water (containing 0.1% formic acid) with a flow rate of 0.3 mL min−1.
Control strains carrying empty vectors were similarly analyzed.
Medium pressure liquid chromatography (MPLC) was carried out on
a RediSep Gold C18 (Teledyne Isco Inc., Lincoln, USA). HPLC was
performed on COSMOSIL PBr (5 μm, ϕ10 × 250 mm2, Nacalai
Tesque, Inc., Japan), COSMOSIL πnap (5 μm, ϕ10 × 250 mm2), and
Waters Atlantis T3 100 Å (5 μm, ϕ10 × 250 mm2, Waters Co.,
Milford, USA).
Strains and Culture Conditions. Aspergillus pseudonomiae
NRRL 3353, A. carlsbadensis NRRL 66343, and Eupenicillium shearii
were obtained from NRRL and NBRC, respectively. The A. nidulans
A1145 ΔEMΔST host was previously developed in our lab.16 All
fungal strains except for A. nidulans were maintained on potato
dextrose agar (PDA, BD) at 28 °C for cell proliferation or in liquid
PDB medium (PDA medium without agar) for the isolation of
genomic DNA. A. nidulans A1145 was grown at 28 °C in CD media
(1 L:10 g of glucose, 50 mL of 20× nitrate salts, 1 mL of trace
elements, pH 6.5, and 20 g/L agar for solid cultivation) for
sporulation or in CD-ST media (1 L:20 g of starch, 20 g of casamino
acids, 50 mL of 20× nitrate salts, 1 mL of trace elements, pH 6.5) for
heterologous expression of gene cluster, compound production, and
RNA extraction. All E. coli strains were cultured in LB media at 37 °C.

study were conducted according to the manufacturer’s protocol. E. coli
TOP10 was used for cloning, following standard recombinant DNA
techniques. DNA restriction enzymes were used as recommended by

Preparation of Protoplast of A. nidulans. The general fungal
transformation method is described in detail elsewhere.33 Briefly, for
the transformation of A. nidulans A1145 ΔEMΔST, spores were
inoculated into 50 mL of liquid CD media in a 125 mL flask and
germinated at 30 °C, shaking at 250 rpm for ∼9 h. The 20× nitrate
salts solution was prepared by dissolving 120 g of NaNO3, 10.4 g of
KCl, 10.4 g of MgSO4·7H2O, and 30.4 g of KH2PO4 in 1 L of
deionized water. The trace elements solution (100 mL) contained
2.20 g of ZnSO4·7H2O, 1.10 g of H3BO3, 0.50 g of MnCl2·4H2O, 0.16
g of FeSO4·7H2O, 0.16 g of CoCl2·5H2O, 0.16 g of CuSO4·5H2O, and
0.11 g of (NH4)6Mo7O24·4H2O. The dropout components for
selection for the three expression vectors were uracil/uridine,
pyridoxine and riboflavin. A. nidulans A1145 ΔEMΔST was initially
grown on CD agar plates containing 10 mM uridine, 5 mM uracil, 0.5
μg/mL pyridoxine HCl and 2.5 μg/mL riboflavin at 37 °C for 5 days.
The germinated spores were harvested by centrifugation at 3,500g for
10 min and washed with osmotic buffer (10 mL, 1.2 M MgSO4, 10
mM sodium phosphate buffer, pH 5.8). The mycelia were then mixed
with osmotic buffer (10 mL, 30 mg of lysing enzymes from
Trichoderma, 20 mg of Yatalase) in a 125 mL flask. Protoplasts
were prepared by incubating the mixture overnight at 30 °C with
gentle shaking at 80 rpm. Cells were collected in a 30 mL Corex tube
and overlaid gently by 10 mL of trapping buffer (0.6 M sorbitol, 0.1 M
Tris−HCI, pH 7.0). Centrifugation at 3,500g for 15 min at 4 °C
layered the protoplasts at the interface of the two buffers. The
protoplasts were then pipetted to a sterile 15 mL falcon tube and

Heterologous Expression of pno, car, and esh Gene Clusters
in A. nidulans. All plasmids for heterologous expression in A.
nidulans were built using the shuttle vectors pYTU, pYTP, and pYTR
with auxotrophic markers for uracil (pyrG), pyridoxine (pyroA), and
riboflavin (riboB), respectively. Genes from the pno, car, and esh
BGCs from A. pseudonomiae, A. carlsbadensis, and E. shearii and their
native terminators were amplified by PCR with overhang primers
using the gDNA from the native hosts as the template. The glaA
promoters from A. niger were amplified by PCR. The overlapping
DNA fragments and their corresponding PacI/NotI-digested pYTU
or pYTP expression vector were transformed into yeast for
homologous recombination. Plasmids were extracted from yeast
using the Zymoprep Yeast Plasmid Miniprep I Kit (Zymo Inc. USA).
The correct colonies checked by PCR were combined and subjected
to a yeast Miniprep. Plasmids were introduced into E. coli TOP 10 by
electroporation. After plasmid extraction from E. coli using a Zyppy
Plasmid Miniprep Kit (Zymo Research, USA), plasmids were
sequenced to confirm identities. The resulting recombinant plasmids
are listed in Table S2. For A. nidulans transformation, necessary
plasmids were added to 60 μL of A. nidulans A1145 protoplast
suspension prepared above, and the mixture was incubated on ice for
60 min. After incubating on ice, 600 μL of PEG solution (60% PEG,
50 mM calcium chloride and 50 mM Tris−HCl, pH 7.5) was added
to the protoplast mixture, followed by additional incubation at room
temperature for 20 min. The mixture was spread on the regeneration
medium (CD solid medium with 1.2 M sorbitol and appropriate
supplements including 10 mM uridine, 5 mM uracil and/or 0.5 μg/
mL pyridoxine HCl and/or 2.5 μg/mL riboflavin depending on the
plasmids being transformed) and incubated at 30 °C for 2−3 days
until single colonies appear. Isolated transformants were grown in CD
agar (1 L:10 g of glucose, 20 g of agar, 50 mL of 20× nitrate salts, 1
mL of trace elements, pH 6.5) for the metabolite analysis by LCMS.
Small Scale Metabolite Analysis. A. nidulans ΔEMΔST
transformants were selected for CD sorbitol agar (2% glucose as
carbon source) appropriately supplemented with riboflavin, uracil,
and/or pyridoxine for the set of plasmids introduced. The CD agar
was inoculated with spores and incubated at 28 °C for 3 days. Two
agar plugs (diameter ∼1 cm) were cut from the plates and extracted
with acetone and methanol (each 200 μL) for 30 min with sonication.
After centrifugation, the supernatant (200 μL) was dried by Speedvac
and the residue resuspended in 50% methanol (100 μL). The organic
phases were subjected to LCMS analysis.
Protein Expression and Purification. The genes encoding carB
and eshB-D were amplified by PCR from cDNA. The sequences of
primers are presented in Table S4. The PCR product of target genes
and pET28a(+) vector are assembled using the NEBuilder HiFi DNA
Assembly kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The
resulting recombinant plasmids were confirmed by sequencing. The
recombinant plasmids harboring C-(His)6 tagged CarB and EshB-D
listed in Table S2 were transferred into E. coli BL21(DE3) for
overexpression. Cultures (4 × 1 L) in LB medium supplemented with

and protein expression was induced by the addition of isopropyl-β-D-
thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) to a final concentration of 80 μM,
followed by further incubation for 16 h at 18 °C. The cultures were
centrifuged for 10 min at 5,000 rpm at 4 °C, and then the cell pellet
was resuspended in 100 mL of lysis buffer (50 mM Tris−HCl, 500
mM NaCl, 10 mM imidazole, 10% glycerol, pH 8.0). The enzymes
were purified with Ni-NTA affinity resin according to the
manufacturer’s manual (Qiagen, Valencia, CA). The target protein
was desalted using a PD-10 Desalting Column (GE Healthcare, USA),
and the purified protein was concentrated using an Amicon Ultra-4
(10 or 40K, GE Healthcare) and stored at −80 °C in buffer (50 mM
Tris−HCl, 200 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, pH 8.0). Bradford Protein
Assay (Bio-Rad) was used to calculate the protein concentration. For
enzymatic assays, see the Supporting Information.

13C NMR Calculations. NMR calculations of (2R, 2′S)-7a, (2S,
2′S)-7b, (2R, 2′S)-8a, and (2S, 2′S)-8b were subjected to the
chemical shift calculation protocol with a default setting on Spartan
20. This protocol automatically performs conformational search with
MMFF94x, conformer narrowing employing Hartree−Fock/3-21G,
energy calculation using ωB97X-D/6-31G(d), structural reoptimiza-
tion with ωB97X-D/6-31G(d), energy calculation applying ωB97X-
V/6-311+G(2df, 2p) [6-311G(d)], and chemical shift calculation
using ωB97X-D/6-31G(d).
Conformational searches and DFT calculations were carried out on
the Spartan 20 program (Wavefunction Inc., Irvine, CA, USA) and
Gaussian 09 program,34 respectively. Conformational searches of (2R,
2′S)-11a, (2S, 2′S)-11b, (2R, 2′S)-12a, and (2S, 2′S)-12b were
conducted at Molecular Mechanics with MMFF to give initial
conformers, which were optimized by the DFT method at the
B3LYP/6-31G(d) level in the presence of MeOH with a polarizable
continuum model (PCM). The stable conformers with Boltzmann
distributions over 1% of the (2R, 2′S)-11a, (2S, 2′S)-11b, (2R, 2′S)-
12a, and (2S, 2′S)-12b were subjected to DFT calculations at the
mpw1pw91/6-31G(d,p) level with PCM (MeOH) to afford the
magnetic shielding values. Then, the 13C NMR chemical shifts were
obtained using a multistandard approach.35 The calculated 13C NMR
chemical shifts were constructed after corrections based on the
Boltzmann distributions of the stable conformers.
