Discovery of the Lipopeptides Albubactins A−H from Streptomyces
albidoflavus RKJM0023 via Chemical Elicitation with Rhamnolipids
and Synthesis of Albubactin A

ABSTRACT: The marine tunicate-derived Streptomyces albidoflavus RKJM0023 was cultured in the presence of a rhamnolipid
mixture in an effort to elicit the production of silent natural products. MS/MS-based molecular networking analysis enhanced with
nonparametric statistics highlighted the upregulation of a molecular cluster (Kruskal−Wallis p = 1.6 e−6 for 1) in which no MS/MS
features had library matches. Targeted isolation of these features resulted in the discovery of nine new N-acylated lipopeptides,
albubactins A−H (1−8) each containing a unique glutamine tripeptide and a C-terminal ethyl ester moiety. Three related albubactin
acids A−C (9−11) lacking the ethyl ester were also identified. NMR spectroscopy and UPLC-HR-ESI-MS/MS demonstrated that
the albubactins were obtained as mixtures that shared a common m/z and differed only in their acylated terminal groups. Due to the
complex spectroscopic elucidation with many overlapping shifts, a total synthesis of albubactin A (1) was completed and used to
determine the absolute configuration of the new albubactins.
T
he discovery of new natural product families holds
immense potential for the development of new drugs,
agrochemicals, and cosmeceuticals.1 Among bacterial sources
of natural products, Gram-positive filamentous bacteria
belonging to the Streptomyces genus stand out as prolific
producers of bioactive metabolites. This is especially apparent
when considering antimicrobial metabolites, as streptomycetes
are the source of two-thirds of all clinically used antibiotics1
due to the rise of antibiotic-resistant infections and emerging
diseases.2,3 Natural products isolated from streptomycetes
display a remarkable array of structural diversity and
complexity, spanning many chemical classes and exhibiting a
wide range of bioactivities.1,4 While terrestrial streptomycetes
have been extensively explored as a source of new natural
products, their marine relatives remain comparatively under-
explored.5 Bioprospecting from under explored habitats
increases the probability of isolating rare streptomycetes
strains, which have the potential of harboring the genetic
information to produce new or novel bioactive natural
products. For example, a study analyzing the BGCs of five
Streptomyces albidoflavus genomes found that each of the five
genomes had one or two biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs)
exclusive to each specific isolate.6
A continuing challenge in natural product discovery is
accessing the full complement of metabolites encoded within
the genomes of microorganisms isolated from diverse habitats.
Even in extensively studied model organisms such as
Streptomyces coelicolor A3(2), there remains a noticeable
discrepancy between the number of natural products produced
under laboratory culture conditions and the number of BGCs
identified in the genome.7,8 These BGCs are referred to as
silent or cryptic. Silent BGCs are those that are not expressed
under typical culture conditions, while cryptic BGCs are
expressed but their products are produced below the limit of
detection. This phenomenon is even more pronounced in
nonmodel strains where the majority of streptomycete BGCs

remain silent or cryptic, preventing biological evaluation of the
natural products encoded by these BGCs.9 Therefore,
innovative techniques are required to gain access to these
cryptic natural products and maximize the discovery potential
of bioprospecting efforts.
Several methods have been employed to upregulate or
induce the biosynthesis of cryptic and silent BGCs in
streptomycetes. These methods include targeted methods
such as the refactoring of BGC regulatory genes,10
heterologous expression of the BGC in production host,
genomic engineering to induce pathway expression by adding a
transcriptional promoter or activating an existing pro-
moter,11,12 or disabling repressors.13 There are also untargeted
methods such as One Strain Many Compounds (OSMAC)
approaches,6 coculture using live or inactivated microogran-
isms14−17 and the exposure to chemical elicitors13,18 or
sublethal concentrations of antibiotics.19,20 Chemical elicitors
have emerged as an attractive method for the activation of
cryptic/silent BGCs in streptomycetes, primarily due to their
relatively simple application across a large number of strains
compared to other activation methods.13,21,22 However, a
major bottleneck in this strategy is the identification of novel
chemical elicitors and determining the application conditions
(e.g., concentration, timing, etc.) that will induce a detectable
metabolic change in the exposed bacteria.
Surfactants are ubiquitous in bacteria and fungi and play
critical roles in motility,23,24 competition,25 morphological
differentiation,26−28 and nutrient acquisition.29,30 There are
several key classes of microbial biosurfactants: lipopeptides as
exemplified by surfactin,31,32 glycolipids as exemplified by
sophorolipids,33 rhamnolipids,34,35 and mannosylerythritol
lipids,36 fatty acids such as phospholipids, β-hydroxydecano-
yl-β-hydroxydecanoate,23 and polymeric surfactants like
emulsan.37,38 Exposure of S. coelicolor to biosurfactants has
been shown to interfere with morphological differentiation39
which would consequently have impacts on downstream
secondary metabolite biosynthesis.40−42 To the best of our
knowledge there is no research exploring the utility of
surfactants as chemical elicitors and their impacts on secondary
metabolism in streptomycetes.
We selected rhamnolipids as a biosurfactant to evaluate as an
elicitor of silent/cryptic BGCs. Rhamnolipids (Rha) are
glycolipid biosurfactants most frequently comprised of a
mono or dirhamnose moiety linked via an O-glycosidic bond
to a hydroxy terminus of a rhamnose sugar. Additionally, they
are linked to one to three 3-hydroxy fatty acids, with chain
lengths varying between 8 and 12 carbons.34,43 Pseudomonas
aeruginosa makes abundant quantities of Rhas largely
dominated by mono or dirhamnose linked to two hydrox-
ydecanoic acids (C-10).43 Rhamnolipids were originally
discovered from P. aeruginosa44 and later identified to be
widespread in Pseudomonadota of the classes Betaproteobac-
teria, Deltaproteobacteria, Gammaproteobacteria, and can be
found in several strains of Actinomycetota and Bacillota.34 As a
result of this wide taxonomic distribution, it is likely that Rha
are found in a broad geographic area and likely to be
encountered by streptomycetes residing in marine and
terrestrial habitats. Consequently, we reasoned that Rha

represented a logical starting point for the evaluation of
biosurfactants as chemical elicitors of silent/cryptic Strepto-
myces BGCs. A S. albidoflavus strain (RKJM0023) isolated
from a marine tunicate was exposed to Rha incorporated in
agar medium. Metabolomic analysis of agar culture organic
extracts revealed a remarkable upregulation of multiple
metabolite families including surugamides, antimycins, and
an unidentified family of metabolites with the most abundant
member exhibiting a mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) of 655.43956
[M + H]+. Induction of the unidentified metabolites by Rha
was reproducible and dose-dependent, illustrating the potential
utility of Rha as chemical elicitors. Here we present the
discovery, isolation, and characterization of these metabolites
as a new lipopeptide family, named albubactins A−H (1−8)
and albubactin acids A−C (9−11) obtained through the use of
Rha as a chemical elicitor. The total synthesis of albubactin A
(1) is also reported.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Upregulation of Albubactins from S. albidoflavus
RKJM0023. To assess the effect of Rha on the metabolome of
a marine streptomycete, S. albidoflavus RKJM0023 was
cultured on ISP2m agar supplemented with 7.70 mg/L of a
commercially available Rha mixture, comprised primarily of
monorhamno-dilipidic and dirhamno-dilipidic Rha. The
resulting extracts were analyzed by UPLC-ESI-HRMS/MS,
and a molecular network was generated using the Global
Natural Product Social Molecular Networking platform
(GNPS)45 (Figure 1). The initial molecular network of S.
albidoflavus RKJM0023 treated with Rha contained 256 ions
with 10 clusters (≥3 nodes).
Several natural product clusters contained precursor ions
with matches in the GNPS MS2 fragmentation databases,46
notably the surugamides47 and antimycins48 (Figure 1). The
identification of surugamides and antimycins production was
corroborated by the presence of complete BGCs of both
antimycin and surugamide in the draft genome of S.
albidoflavus RKJM0023 (Acc. no. NZ_CP133227.1) as well
as supported by the isolation of a surugamide A analogue, acyl-
surugamide A2.49 Exposure to Rha led to the significant
upregulation of a precursor ion with m/z 655.4378 (Dunn’s
test control p-value 0.011, vehicle control p-value 0.022)
(Figure S1) within the unknown cluster m/z 655.439, in which
m/z 655.439 and 669.463 are the most abundant precursor
ions (Figure 1).
Three nodes in the cluster containing m/z 655.439 matched
known cyclic polyethers in the AntiBase 201750 or
NPAtlas51,52 databases, namely 26-deoxymonensin A, 26-
deoxymonensin B, and macrotetrolide B.53 To determine if
the cluster consisted of polyether analogs, a standard of 26-
deoxymonensin A was analyzed by UPLC-ESI-HRMS. The
retention time (RT) was compared to that of the metabolites
from RKJM0023. The RT of 26-deoxymonensin A was (5.36
min) significantly different than the RKJM0023 metabolites
(3.92 min), indicating that the Rha-induced metabolites were
likely not cyclic polyethers (Figure S2), and as a result, the
compounds corresponding to the upregulated cluster were
pursued for purification and given the trivial name albubactins.

chemical elicitor on the secondary metabolism of S.
albidoflavus RKJM0023, the strain was exposed to a 2-fold
dilution series of Rha, starting from the critical micelle
concentration (61.6 mg/L, CMC)54 down to 1/32 of the
CMC (1.9 mg/L, Rha32), and the resulting fermentation
extracts were analyzed by UPLC-ESI-HRMS/MS. The CMC
was chosen as the maximum concentration of Rha, as any
concentration above the CMC would result in the formation of

the effect of inoculation type on the production of the

To identify the metabolites that were upregulated or
downregulated in response to the increasing amount of Rha,
we constructed an ion identity feature-based molecular
network (IIFBMN).55 Nodes present in the media controls
and Rha controls were filtered. Then nonparametric statistical
(Dunn’s test)56 calculations were combined with calculations
of fold change for each ion and visualized as a volcano plot
using R Statistical Software (v4.3.1)57 (Figure 2). The resulting
statistically upregulated and downregulated feature list was
used to enhance the IIFBMN in Cytoscape (v3.10.1).58 The
resulting network contained three clusters with multiple
upregulated ions: the antimycin, surugamide, and albubactin
clusters (Figure 2). Additionally, features that were not
detected in any control or in RKJM0023 control cultures
were colored purple (Figure 2). The purple features were
exclusively detected in Rha-treated RKJM0023 cultures,
suggesting that these features are either biotransformations
or de novo induced cryptic natural products. We hypothesize
that the purple nodes present in the Rha cluster are
biotransformations of Rha done by RKJM0023 while the
unclustered nodes may be de novo induced natural products or
further degradations (Figure 2).
The IIFBMN (Figure 2) revealed that the surugamides,
antimycins, and unknown cluster (albubactins) each contain
multiple features that were upregulated (red) in the Rha
treatments. We selected the most abundant features (as
determined by peak area) from each cluster and performed the
nonparametric Kruskal-Wilcox test to determine if there were
significant differences between groups, followed by Dunn’s test
(p-value < 0.05) to determine which treatments were
significantly different from the vehicle control (Figure S3).
The level of surugamide A (m/z 912.6261) was significantly
decreased in the vehicle control compared to the untreated
control, and treatment with increasing concentration of Rha
increased the production of this cyclic peptide. Production of
antimycin A (m/z 521.2678) was significantly increased by
treatment with Rha beginning at a concentration of 7.7 mg/L
(Rha8) and increasing to a maximum at 30.8 mg/L (Rha2).
The log peak area for unknown feature m/z 655.439 was
significantly increased at all concentrations of Rha greater than
1.9 mg/L (Rha32) with a maximum increase achieved at 30.8
mg/L (Rha2) (Figure S3). Production of both unknown
feature m/z 655.439 and antimycin A peaked at 30.8 mg/L
(Rha2), while accumulation of surugamide A continued to
increase up to the maximum treatment dose (Rha1). A feature
at m/z 225.1499 had a GNPS database match to MKN-
003A;59 though this was one of the most abundant metabolites
produced in the absence of Rha, mean peak area for this
feature decreased as Rha concentration increased (Figure S3).
These results strongly suggests that the specific addition of Rha
to the fermentations is driving changes in RKJM0023
secondary metabolism.
For the spore-inoculated plates, several key differences were
observed in the Rha titration experiment: the spores were
completely inhibited at Rha concentrations greater than 15.4
mg/L (Rha4) (Figure S4); a higher sensitivity to added vehicle
(ethanol) was observed, and the mean peak areas measured for
most features had a higher average standard deviation (SD),
indicating increased variability in the production of NPs (e.g.,
unknown m/z 655.439 log SD mycelium 0.47, spore 0.60;
surugamide A log SD: mycelium 0.78, spore 1.23). Overall,
fewer ions were significantly upregulated in the spore-
inoculated plates (mycelium 28 ions, spore 2 ions) while
many ions were instead observed to be downregulated
(mycelium 10 ions, spore 13 ions) (Figure S5). Looking at
the same indicator ions, no significant change was observed for
surugamide A, and for antimycin A and unknown m/z 655.439,
both vehicle controls were significantly upregulated compared
to the untreated controls while m/z 655.439 was exclusively
upregulated at the Rha4 treatment condition (Figure S6). For
MKN-003A,59 upregulation was observed in response to
ethanol while down regulation was observed in the Rha
treatment similar to the trend observed in the mycelium
inoculation group (Figure S6).
The inoculation using spore stocks was far more sensitive to
both the vehicle control (ethanol) and elevated Rha
concentrations compared to the mycelium inoculum. This
difference in Rha sensitivity may be attributed to the higher
cell density of the mycelium inoculum, or Rha may have
prevented germination of the spores at higher concentra-
tions.60 The Rha potentially inhibited the spores by denaturing
spore-coat proteins due to hydrophilic or hydrophobic
interactions with Rha. The elevated spore sensitivity to ethanol
can be attributed to potential interference of outer membrane
of the spores and cell membrane of developing colonies,
especially at lower spore densities.61
For mycelium cultures the Rha did cause changes in the
colony morphology at 30.8 mg/L (Rha2, Figure S4) where the
colonies are more dispersed and have a different sporulation
pattern, while Rha at 61.6 mg/L (Rha1) showed a notable
increase in aerial mycelium formation but no sporulation
(Figure S4). These morphological changes co-occurred with
the greatest changes in metabolomics, suggesting that changes
in colony morphology can be a telling indicator of changes
secondary metabolism BGC expression in solid streptomycetes
fermentations26−28 and that surfactants can cause changes to
streptomycetes colony morphology.39
Isolation and Structure Elucidation of Albubactin A.
The Rha chemical elicitor fermentations of RKJM0023 were
scaled up to 20 L on ISP2m agar containing 7.70 mg/L of Rha
to increase the accumulation of the target metabolites. RP-
HPLC purification yielded four peaks containing ions seen in
unknown cluster m/z 655.439 (m/z 627.4083, 641.4238,
655.4396, and 669.4549 [M + H]+). This includes an
albubactins A−C (1−3) peak at 15 min (1.71 mg) with m/z
655.4396, albubactins D−E (4−5) at 18 min (0.95 mg) with
m/z 669.4549, albubactins F−H (6−8) at 12 min (1.17 mg)
with m/z 641.4238, and albubactin acids A−C (9−11) at 9.5
min (1.00 mg) with m/z 627.4083.

Albubactins A−C (1−3) were obtained as a mixture in an
amorphous solid and had a formula of C32H58N6O8 with seven
degrees of unsaturation as supported by HR-ESI-MS (m/z
655.4396 [M + H]+). The
1H NMR (Table 1) revealed
distinctive signals for three secondary amide protons (δH 7.94,
δH 7.96, and δH 8.31) and a fatty chain region (δH 1.2−1.4),
while the 13C DEPTQ NMR revealed the presence of seven
carbonyls (δC 171.48, δC 171.50, δC 171.7, δC 172.55, δC
173.34, δC 173.88, and δC 173.98), one deshielded methylene
(δC 60.5), and three deshielded methines (δC 51.7, δC 51.9,
and δC 52.3) corresponding to the amino acid α-carbons.
Analysis of 1H−1H TOCSY revealed four distinct spin systems
H3-1/H2-2, NH-4/H-4/H2-5/H2-6, NH-9/H-9/H2-10/H2-11,
and NH-14/H-14/H2-15/H2-16 consistent with the presence
of an ethyl ester and three amino acid residues (Figure 3). A
fifth spin system was observed and was consistent with an acyl
fatty acid as correlations were observed from H2-19/H2-20 to
the overlapping methylene envelope (δH 1.23) and to an
additional one or two methyl groups (δH 0.83 and δH 0.82 or
δH 0.86 or δH 0.84) corresponding to the presence of both
branched and unbranched fatty acid side chains. The first
amino acid was confirmed to be glutamine through key HMBC
correlations from NH-4 (δH 7.94) to C-4 (δC 51.9) and C-5
(δC 28.0) and from H2-5 (δH 1.88, 1.71) to C-3 (δC 171.5), C-
4 (δC 51.9), C-6 (δC 31.6), and C-7 (δC 173.9) (Figure 3). A
TOCSY spin system from 7-NH2a (δH 6.7) to 7-NH2b (δH 7.2)
and
1H−15N HSQC (δH 7.2 and δH 6.7, δN 109) were
consistent with the primary amide in glutamine. An HMBC
correlation from NH2-7a (δH 6.7) to C-6 (δC 31.6) confirmed
the residue as glutamine. The presence of two additional
glutamine residues was confirmed using COSY, TOCSY, and
HMBC correlations and were linked as a tripeptide by HMBC
correlations from NH-4 (δH 7.94) to C-8 (δC 171.7) and NH-9
(δH 8.31) to C-13 (δC 171.5) (Figure 3). The presence of an
ethyl ester at the C-terminus of this tripeptide was confirmed
by COSY correlations between H3-1 (δH 1.17) and H2-2 (δH
4.07) and HMBC correlations from H2-2 (δH 4.07) to C-1 (δC
14.0) and C-3 (δC 171.5). The fatty acid was confirmed to be
pentadecanoic acid based on the remaining molecular formula
(C15H29O) and confirmed later by MS/MS analysis (Figure
S7). The pentadecanoic acid moiety was confirmed to be
linked at the N-terminus of the tripeptide through HMBC
correlations from NH-14 (δH 7.96) to C-18 (δC 172.6).
Careful analysis of the 1D and 2D NMR spectra (Figures S8−
S18) revealed the presence of three analogs containing anteiso,
iso, or straight chain pentadecanoic acid which were given the
trivial names albubactin A, B, and C, respectively (Figure 3).
Albubactins D−E (0.95 mg) (4−5) were obtained as an
amorphous solid with a chemical formula of C33H60N6O8 as
determined by HR-ESI-MS (m/z 669.4549 [M + H]+).
Analysis of HRMS/MS revealed that 4−5 differed from 1 by
the presence of an additional CH2 unit in the fatty acyl-chain
(Figure S18). The 1H and HSQC NMR spectra of 4−5 shared
similarities to 1−2 suggesting they were analogs, containing
anteiso and iso fatty acid termini (Figures S19 and S20).
Albubactins F−H (1.17 mg) (6−8) were obtained as an
amorphous solid with a chemical formula of C31H56N6O8
supported by HR-ESI-MS (m/z 641.4238 [M + H]+). The
HRMS/MS analysis showed that 6−8 differed from 1−3 by
the absence of a CH2 group in the fatty acyl-chain (Figure
S21). The
1H and HSQC NMR spectra of 6−8 shared
similarities to 1−3, suggesting they were analogs (Figures S22
and S23).
Albubactin acids A−C (1.00 mg) (9−11) were obtained as
an amorphous solid with a chemical formula of C30H54N6O8
determined by HR-ESI-MS (m/z 627.4083 [M + H]+). The
1H and HSQC NMR spectra of 9−11 shared similarities to 1−
3, suggesting they were analogs. The 1H NMR showed a
prominent broad signal characteristic of a carboxylic acid
proton (δH 6.90) and lacked the prominent triplet at δH 1.17 of
C-1 of 1−3. The HRMS/MS analysis showed that all fatty acid
containing fragments were identical to 1−3, suggesting the loss
of the ethyl-ester group on the C-terminus of Gln-1 leaving a
free carboxylic acid. The structures of albubactin acids A−C
(9−11) were determined through HRMS/MS analysis
comparing fragments to 1−3 combined with NMR analysis
(Figures S24−S26).
Amino acid configurations of 1−3 were determined by
Marfey’s analysis62 (Figure S27). A mixture of 1−3 was
hydrolyzed with 6 M HCl followed by the addition of N-(5-
fluoro-2,4-dinitrophenyl-5)-L-alanineamide (L-FDAA). The
derivatized hydrolysate was analyzed by UPLC-HRMS and
compared to L-DAA-derivatized standards of glutamic acid.
Standards of glutamic acid were used for Marfey’s in place of
glutamine due to deamination of the amide functional group to
a carboxylic acid during the hydrolysis of 1−3. Derivatized 1−
3 has a retention time consistent with L-Glu (Figure S27).
Therefore, albubactins A−C 1−3 are composed of an L-Gln
tripeptide.
To the best of our knowledge this is the first example of a
Gln tripeptide-containing natural product. The presence of the
acid forms of albubactins 9−11 potentially suggests that the
ethyl ester forms could be artifacts from extraction. However,
both MeOH and EtOAc extractions showed the presence of
each analogue when analyzed via UPLC-ESI-HRMS/MS. This
rules out the possibility of ethyl ester extraction artifacts. Two
sequential Gln residues are commonly found in fungal NRP
antibiotics including the peptaibiotics and the peptaibols,
produced by Trichoderma/Hypocrea.63,64 The composition of
variable chain-length acyl groups with differential terminal
groups including iso, anteiso, and unbranched fatty acyl chains
in albubactins (1−3) is similar to those found in the marine
siderophore saccharochelins, from the rare marine-derived
actinomycete Saccharothrix sp.65 The saccharochelins contain a
tetrapeptide core with similar N-terminal acyl chains to the
albubactins. They were shown to have potent cytotoxicity
against several human tumor cell lines, with IC50 values ranging
from 2.3 to 17 μM.65 Interestingly, the specific N-terminal fatty
acid side chains affected cytotoxicity of the saccharochelins.
The biosynthetic origins of the N-terminal groups of the
saccharochelins are thought to originate from primary
metabolism as degradation products of the amino acids
isoleucine, leucine, and valine.65 Branched-chain fatty acids
with terminal iso and anteiso groups are the primary acyl
constituents of bacterial membranes.66
Given the novelty of the structures, the significant
overlapping signals in the NMR, and the fact that the
albubactins were isolated as mixtures of iso, anteiso and
unbranched fatty acids even after extensive HPLC, we
undertook a total synthesis of 1 to unambiguously establish
the core structure of this new family of lipopeptides. The
anteiso-acyl-chain albubactin A (1) was selected to assign the
absolute configuration of the anteiso-acyl-chain.

■CONCLUSIONS
In conclusion, front-end molecular network-based metabolo-
mics can be an effective method for natural product isolation
prioritization and can be further enhanced with nonparametric
statistics. This study demonstrates that rhamnolipids are
effective at significantly increasing the secondary metabolite
production of the new lipopeptides albubactins A−H (1−8),
albubactin acids A−C (9−11), surugamides, and antimycins,
when added to a solid media fermentation of the marine-
derived streptomycete, S. albidoflavus RKJM0023. Total
synthesis of 1 confirmed the structure of a difficult to separate
family of natural products. Further investigations using
surfactants as chemical elicitors can be an effective method

General Experimental Procedures. Optical rotations were
measured on a Rudolph Autopol III polarimeter using a 50 mm
microcell (1.2 mL). NMR spectra were obtained on a Bruker Avance
III NMR spectrometer (1H: 600 MHz, 13C: 151 MHz) equipped with
a 5 mm cryoprobe or on a Bruker Avance III NMR spectrometer (1H:
400 MHz, 13C: 100 MHz) equipped with a 5 mm cryoprobe where
indicated. All chemical shifts (δ) are referenced to the DMSO-d6
residual solvent peaks [1H (DMSO-d6): 2.50 ppm; 13C (DMSO-d6):
39.51 ppm]. A Thermo Scientific Vanquish UPLC chromatograph
equipped with HRMS-CAD-UV detection, which included a Thermo
Scientific ID-X Tribrid mass spectrometer fitted with a heated
electrospray ionization (H-ESI) source, a Thermo Scientific charged
aerosol detector VF-D20-A, and a Thermo Scientific diode array
detector (DAD) VF-D11-A-01 scanning 200−600 nm, were used.
The solvents A = 0.1% formic acid in water and B = 0.1% formic acid
in acetonitrile were used at a 0.5 mL/min flow rate with a Kinetex 1.7
μm C18 100 Å (50 × 2.1 mm) with the following gradient: 0 min =
5% B, 0.2 min = 5% B (isocratic), 4.8 min = 98% B, 8 min = 98% B
(isocratic), 8.5 min = 5% B, 9.8 min = 5% B (isocratic). The MS
parameters include positive ion scans performed from 150 to 2000
amu at an ion transfer tube temperature of 300 °C and a vaporizer
temperature of 275 °C and set on data-dependent MS/MS scanning.
Reagents and starting materials were from commercial suppliers. All
media were prepared using 18 mOhm diH2O. Rha (AGAE
Technologies, R90, catalogue #7901) used in chemical elicitor
challenges, the major constituents were dirhamno-dilipidic m/z
651.3945 [M + H]+ (Rha-Rha-C10-C10) and monorhamno-dilipidic
m/z 505.3364 [M + H]+ (Rha-C10-C10). Anhydrous ethanol was
prepared by drying over 4 Å molecular sieves for 24 h. All glassware
used was washed with acetone and dried with N2 or in a vacuum oven.
Isolation of RKJM0023. RKJM0023 was isolated from the tissue
of a tunicate Halocynthia papillosa collected in the Mediterranean Sea
off the coast of Turkey (36.591415, 30.600488) at a depth of 18 m via
SCUBA in April 2013. The sample was collected under a permit
issued to Prof. Dr. Belma Konuklugil from Ankara University, Faculty
of Pharmacy, Department of Pharmacognosy, 06100 Tandoğan
Ankara (granted 02.01.2012 by the Ministry of Food, Agriculture
and Animal Husbandry, Directorate General on Agricultural
Researches and Policies; Issue: B.12.0.TAG.0.04.03.730.10-2457).
The tunicate tissue was homogenized, and serial dilutions were plated
on raffinose histidine agar plates (raffinose 10 g/L, histidine 1 g/L,
KH2PO4 1 g/L, FeSO4·7H2O 0.01 g/L, noble agar 12 g, 1 L diH2O
with the pH = 7.5) supplemented with Instant Ocean marine salts (18
g/L),69 cycloheximide (50.0 μg/mL), and nalidixic acid (15.0 μg/
mL).70 The plates were incubated at 22.5 ± 2.5 °C and strain
RKJM0023 was purified by serial subculturing. The RKJM0023

collected from the surface of the culture in 20% (v/v) glycerol by
gentle scraping and then filtered through nonabsorbent cotton to
remove mycelia. The spore suspension was stored at −80 °C until
needed. A serial dilution of the spore stock was then employed to
determine the dilution factor where 10−15 spores were added to the
well and the entire surface of the well covered. Diluted (diH2O) spore
stock was used to inoculate each well of the 24 well plates containing
2 mL ISP2m. For the mycelia inoculum, 50 μL of glycerol stock was
used to inoculate 7 mL of BSM1m medium (dextrose 10 g/L, yeast

beads and incubated at 30 °C and 200 rpm. After 24 h, 1 mL of the
first-stage seed was transferred to 50 mL of fresh BSM1m broth and
incubated under the same conditions for 24 h. For the mycelia
inoculum, 10 μL of second stage seed culture was pipetted onto the
surface of the 2 mL agar plugs. Rha (AGAE Technologies, R90,
catalogue no. 7901) was added to the medium by dissolving the
surfactant in 95% ethanol (Thermo Scientific). The total volume of
the vehicle with surfactant was equal to 1% v/v of the total media
volume in each well. To add the surfactant-ethanol solution to the
agar, the warm agar was mixed with the surfactant solution in a sterile
conical tube before being dispensed into the well plates using a
serological pipette. For the Rha titration, a 2-fold serial dilution was
conducted, descending from the reported CMC of Rha (61.6 mg/
L).54 The Rha concentrations used were: 61.6 mg/L (Rha1), 30.8
mg/L (Rha2), 15.4 mg/L (Rha4), 7.7 mg/L (Rha8), 3.85 mg/L

10 μL of the spore suspension or mycelium from vegetative culture
and incubated for 14 days at 30 °C. Small scale fermentations were
extracted by transferring each individual agar plug into a scintillation
vial with 10 mL of ethyl acetate. The agar plug was removed from the
vial after 24 h, and the solvent was then dried under air. The dried
extract was then dissolved in MeOH and split into two equal fractions
for UPLC-HRMS/MS analysis and subsequent metabolomic analysis.

MZmine HRMS/MS Data Processing, GNPS Network
Generation, and Statistical Analysis in R. Small-scale fermenta-
tion samples were analyzed by UPLC-ESI-HRMS/MS in positive
mode with a scanning window from m/z 190−2000 that was set for
data-dependent MS/MS scanning. The raw data files were converted
to .MZml files using MSConvert (http://proteowizard.sourceforge.
net/). The chromatograms were processed in MZmine 372 following
recommended ion identity feature-based molecular networking
processing.55 In brief, MS1 and MS2 masses were detected, the
ADAP chromatogram builder73 assembled (minimum intensity 1.0E6,
minimum absolute height 5.0E6, and a m/z tolerance of 35 ppm),
smoothing, feature resolved, isotope patterns detected and filtered,
peaks aligned, filtered and gap filled before doing the ion identity
detection. The resulting MGF, quant table, and metadata were used to
generate a feature-based molecular network with GNPS.45,46 The
quant table was imported from MZmine 372 to the RStudio
(v2023.12.1.402) running R (v4.3.1). Metabolomic feature filtering
and statistical analysis were performed using the following packages:
tidyverse (v2.0.0),74 reshape2 (v1.4.4),75 broom (v1.0.5),76 rstatix
(v0.7.2),77 and dunn.test (v1.3.5).78 Ions present in the media blanks
and Rha controls were removed (Rha control at concentrations of
61.6 and 30.8 mg/L) and the mean fold change of the peak area for
each ion to the vehicle control calculated. Subsequently, non-
parametric statistics (Kruskal−Wallis and Dunn test) were used to
evaluate significant variation of each ion and were plotted in a volcano
plot as well as generated box plots. Calculated upregulated ions and
downregulated ions were used to enhance the molecular network
visualization in Cytoscape (v3.10.1).58
