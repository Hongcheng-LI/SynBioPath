ABSTRACT: Four new p-terphenyl derivatives, talaroterphenyls
A−D (1−4), together with three biosynthetically related known
ones (5−7), were obtained from the mangrove sediment-derived
Talaromyces sp. SCSIO 41412. Compounds 1−3 are rare p-
terphenyls, which are completely substituted on the central
benzene ring by oxygen atoms; this is the first report of their
isolation from natural sources. Their structures were elucidated
through NMR spectroscopy, HRESIMS, and X-ray diffraction.
Genome sequence analysis revealed that 1−7 were biosynthesized
from tyrosine and phenylalanine, involving four key biosynthetic
genes (ttpB−ttpE). These p-terphenyls (1−7) and 36 marine-
derived terphenyl analogues (8−43) were screened for phospho-
diesterase 4 (PDE4) inhibitory activities, and 1−5, 14, 17, 23, and
26 showed notable activities with IC50 values of 0.40−16 μM. The binding pattern of p-terphenyl inhibitors 1−3 with PDE4 were
explored by molecular docking analysis. Talaroterphenyl A (1), with a low cytotoxicity, showed obvious anti-inflammatory activity in
LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 cells. Furthermore, in the TGF-β1-induced medical research council cell strain-5 (MRC-5) pulmonary
fibrosis model, 1 could down-regulate the expression levels of FN1, COL1, and α-SMA significantly at concentrations of 5−20 μM.
This study suggests that the oxidized p-terphenyl 1, as a marine-derived PDE4 inhibitor, could be used as a promising antifibrotic
agent.
P
hosphodiesterase 4 (PDE4) plays a crucial role in
regulating intracellular levels of cyclic adenosine mono-
phosphate (cAMP) and is considered an important drug target.
cAMP is a vital signaling molecule involved in various cellular
processes, including inflammation, immune responses, and
smooth muscle contraction.1 PDE4 inhibitors are a class of
drugs that block the activity of the PDE4 enzyme, leading to an
increase in the cAMP levels. In recent years, a series of
competitive PDE4 inhibitors, such as roflumilast,2 crisaborole,3
apremilast,1 ibudilast,4 and difamilast,5 have been developed
for treatment of various inflammatory diseases, including
chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), atopic
dermatitis, psoriasis, asthma, and others. More than 60
cocrystal structures of PDE4 complexed with substrate
cAMP and various inhibitors were reported in the PDB
database and revealed that the substrate cAMP and the
marketed inhibitors bound in the same catalytic pocket.
Although PDE4 inhibitors show promising therapeutic
potential, their clinical development is limited due to the
common occurrence of adverse reactions. Therefore, the
development of novel PDE4 inhibitors holds significant clinical
and scientific importance.6,7
As important sources of drug molecules, natural products
have been extensively studied.8 In our previous research, we
conducted screening of natural products and discovered several
novel PDE4 inhibitors.9 Among them, α-mangostin (IC50 =
1.31 μM) and mangostanin (IC50 = 0.42 μM), which are
natural products belonging to the anthraquinone derivative
class with two conjugated benzene rings, demonstrated
remarkable PDE4 inhibitory activities. The oxygenated
anthraquinone derivative MG73, which contains three benzene
rings, exhibits even stronger PDE4 inhibitory activity with an
IC50 value of 4.2 nM and significant efficacy in combating
pulmonary fibrosis.10,11
There have been limited reports on the PDE4 inhibitory
activity of terphenyls. The p-terphenyls, consisting of three
conjugated benzene rings, are the most common natural
form.12 The most notable characteristic of p-terphenyl
compounds is their broad range of biological activities,

In our ongoing pursuit of PDE4 inhibitors,6,18−21 a screen
was performed on 46 terphenyls, including seven p-terphenyls
(1−7) from the mangrove sediment-derived Talaromyces sp.
SCSIO 41412 and 36 published terphenyl derivatives (8−43,
Figure S33) from two marine-derived fungi.13,14 The selected
compounds with significant PDE4 inhibitory activity and less
toxicity were evaluated for their anti-inflammatory and
antipulmonary fibrosis activities in vitro.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Talaroterphenyl A (1), which appears as colorless crystals, had
its molecular formula determined as C22H20O5 by HRESIMS.
The 1H, 13C, and HSQC NMR (Table 1) of 1 revealed the
presence of three methyls, including two oxygenated (δH/C
3.31/60.3, 3.26/60.6), 10 aromatic/olefinic methines (δH/C
7.47/128.0, 7.47/128.0, 7.44/130.0, 7.44/130.0, 7.42/127.8,
7.42/127.8, 7.40/127.5, 7.35/127.3, 7.27/129.9, 7.27/129.9),
eight nonprotonated sp2 carbons (δC 144.0, 143.2, 141.7,
137.9, 133.1, 133.1, 128.6, 123.3), and one ester carbon (δC
168.8). 1D NMR of 1 indicated the presence of a p-terphenyl
skeleton comprising two monosubstituted and a hexasubsti-
tuted benzene, which was supported by the 1H−1H COSY and
HMBC correlations (Figure 1). The HMBC correlations of 1
provided evidence for the assignment of the two methoxy
groups at the C-3′ and C-5′ positions. The existence of an
acetoxy group was determined by the HMBC correlations from
H3-10′ to C-9′ and the chemical shifts of CH3-10′ (δH/C 1.97/
20.1). The HMBC correlations from H-2 and H-6 to C-1′ and
from H-2″ and H-6″ to C-4′ indicated that two monosub-
stituted benzenes were located at C-1′ and C-4′. The locations
of the remaining hydroxy and the acetoxy group were difficult
to determine by 2D NMR. Fortunately, high quality crystals of
1 were obtained and analyzed by the X-ray diffraction (Figure
2, CCDC 2324777).
Talaroterphenyl B (2) was obtained as colorless crystals and
its molecular formula was established as C20H18O4 based on
HRESIMS. The NMR (Table 1) showed that 2 shared the
same p-terphenyl skeleton as 1. The absence of one ester
carbon and a methyl group suggested the presence of another
hydroxy group in place of the acetoxy group in 2, which was
confirmed by HMBC (Figure 1). Accordingly, 2 was assigned
as talaroterphenyl B and was further confirmed by X-ray
diffraction (Figure 2, CCDC 2323418).

Talaroterphenyls C (3) and D (4) were both obtained as
yellow crystals, respectively. Comparison of the NMR data
(Table 1) of 3, 4, and 2 revealed that they share the same p-
terphenyl skeleton. The HRESIMS of 3 gave a molecular
formula of C20H18O5, consistent with one more hydroxy group
than that of 2. The major difference between 3 and 2 was the
presence of an additional hydroxy at C-4″ in 3 rather than the
hydrogen atom in 2, which was verified by the chemical shift of
C-4″ (δC 156.5) and the disappearance of the 1H NMR signal
of an aromatic methine. The molecular formula of 4 was
C19H17O4, consistent with one less methoxy group than 3. It
was deduced that there was a hydrogen atom at C-5′ in 4 as
opposed to a methoxy. The deductions above were
unequivocally confirmed by the X-ray diffraction of 3 and 4
(Figure 2, CCDC 2324783 and 2324786, respectively).

The three known p-terphenyls from Talaromyces sp. SCSIO
41412 were elucidated as terferol (5),22 strepantibin D (6),23
and strepantibin E (7)23 by comparison of their 1D NMR data
with the literature.
Based on the genomic information, we deduced that the
biosynthetic pathway of 1−7 involved four key biosynthetic
genes (ttpB−ttpE) (Figure 3). First, extracellular enzymes
catalyzed the conversion of L-tyrosine and L-phenylalanine to
form 4-hydroxyphenylpyruvate (4-HPP) and phenylpyruvate
(PP), respectively. TtpC (NRPS-like enzyme) catalyzed the
condensation of 4-HPP and PP, or two PP units, to form
quinone, which was subsequently reduced and dehydrated by
TtpB (NADH-dependent oxidoreductase). Conversion to 2
and 3 was catalyzed by TtpE (cytochrome P450 mono-
oxygenase) and TtpD (O-methyltransferase), while 1 was
derived from acetylation of one hydroxy group of 2.
Compounds 4 and 5 are directly methylated by TtpD, while
6 and 7 are the result of the combined catalysis of TtpD and a
glycosyltransferase. At present, the biosynthetic pathways of p-
terphenyls derived from L-tyrosine have been reported,24 while
p-terphenyls derived from both L-tyrosine and L-phenylalanine
have not been described.
Compounds 1−7, along with 36 terphenyl derivatives (8−
43), were evaluated for inhibitory activity against PDE4 (Table
S1). Among them, nine compounds (1−5, 14, 17, 23, and 26)
were identified as PDE4 inhibitors, with IC50 values ranging
from 0.40 to 7.8 μM (Table 2). Rolipram, a well-known PDE4
inhibitor, was used as the control, with an IC50 value of 0.59
μM (Figure S34). Compounds 1−3, with the central benzene
ring highly oxidized, exhibited the strongest activity. This
suggests that the high degree of oxidation in the central
benzene ring plays a crucial role in their pharmacological
activity.
Furthermore, molecular docking analysis of 1−3 was used to
explore the possible binding pattern of p-terphenyl inhibitors
with PDE4. As shown in Figure 4, the terphenyl moiety formed
strong π−π stacking interactions with the hydrophobic clamp
of PDE4 consisting of Phe372 and Phe340/336. Additionally,
the oxygen atom in the central ring could form an H-bond with
residue Gln369. These two interactions were characteristic of
PDE inhibitors. Furthermore, the binding of 2 seemed to be
sufficiently flexible to allow both of the methoxy groups to
rotate and form an H-bond with Gln369, whereas the acetyl
group in 1 was sterically hindered by the nearby Met357,
which might partially explain the higher activity of 2. The
docking study also suggested that the hydroxy group of 3
might be sterically excluded from the interior of the PDE4
pocket, causing the p-terphenyl scaffold to rotate compared
with that of 2 (Figure 4C).
Since 1−3 showed significant PDE inhibitory activity, we
further explored their antifibrotic potential. The transforming
growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1)-induced NIH-3T3 cell model was
used for preliminary screening, and 1 exhibited low
cytotoxicity and visible inhibitory activity of fibrogenic action
in cells prompting further evaluation (Figure S35−S36).
The antifibrotic effect and anti-inflammatory activity of
PDE4 inhibitors are known to be closely linked. Therefore, we
used lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-stimulated RAW264.7 cells to
test the anti-inflammatory activity of 1. It also did not show
significant cytotoxicity to the RAW264.7 cells at concen-
trations of 10−160 μM (Figure 5A). As shown in Figures
5B−5D, the up-regulated mRNA levels of pro-inflammatory
cytokines IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β in LPS-stimulated
RAW264.7 cells were significantly decreased after treating
with 1 (10 and 20 μM).
In order to further explore the inhibitory activity and
medicinal potential of 1 on pulmonary fibrosis, a TGF-β1-
induced medical research council cell strain-5 (MRC-5)
pulmonary fibrosis model was established. After the
stimulation of TGF-β1 for 48 h, the mRNA and protein levels
of FN1, COL1, and α-SMA, whose production is a main
hallmark of fibrosis, were upregulated significantly in MRC-5
cells. As shown in Figures 6B−6D, the mRNA levels of FN1,
COL1, and α-SMA were visibly decreased after treating with 1
(5, 10, or 20 μM) or pirfenidone (PFD, 10 μM). Meanwhile,
the up-regulated protein expression levels of FN1, COL1, and
α-SMA were also reduced by 1 (5, 10, or 20 μM) treatment in
a dose-dependent manner (Figures 6E−6H). The immuno-
fluorescence staining results were consistent with Western
blotting results (Figure 6I). These results indicated that the
PDE4 inhibitor 1 could prevent TGF-β1-induced pulmonary
fibrosis.
In conclusion, the marine-derived PDE4 inhibitor talar-
oterphenyl A (1) exhibited significant efficacy against TGF-β1-
induced pulmonary fibrosis in MRC-5 cells and down-
regulated the expression levels of FN1, COL1, and α-SMA

■EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
General Experimental Procedures. Melting points were
recorded on an SGW X-5 melting point apparatus. Optical rotations
were determined using an Anton Paar MPC 500 polarimeter. UV and
IR spectra were recorded by using a Shimadzu UV-2600 PC
spectrometer and an IR Affinity-1 spectrometer, respectively. NMR
spectra were recorded on a Bruker Avance spectrometer operating at
500 MHz for 1H NMR and 125 MHz for 13C NMR. HRESIMS
images were acquired on a Bruker maXis Q-TOF mass spectrometer.
HPLC was performed on the Hitachi Primaide with a DAD detector,
using an ODS column (YMC-pack ODS-A, 10 × 250 mm, 5 μm). X-
ray diffraction was performed on an XtalLAB PRO diffractometer with
Cu Kα radiation.
Fungal Material. The fungal strain Talaromyces sp. SCSIO 41412
was isolated from a sediment sample obtained from Gaoqiao
Mangrove in Zhanjiang City, Guangdong Province in August 2021.
The fungus was identified by analyzing the sequence data of the
internally transcribed spacer (ITS) region of rDNA. The correspond-
ing sequence has been submitted to GenBank and assigned the
accession number PP001498. The strain was stored on MB agar (15 g
of malt extract, 18 g of agar, 10 g of sea salt, and 1 L of water), slants
at 4 °C, and deposited in the CAS Key Laboratory of Tropical Marine
Bioresources and Ecology, South China Sea Institute of Oceanology,
Chinese Academy of Sciences, Guangzhou, China.
Fermentation, Extraction, and Isolation. Talaromyces sp.
SCSIO 41412 was cultured in 200 mL of seed medium (15 g of
malt extract, 10 g of sea salt, and 1 L of water) on a rotary shaker (180
rpm) at 28 °C for 3 days. A large-scale fermentation was incubated
statically at 26 °C for 28 days using a rice medium (200 g of rice, 2%
sea salt, and 230 mL of water) in 47 Erlenmeyer flasks. The whole
fermented culture was extracted with EtOAc more than three times to
afford an extract (326.1 g), which was chromatographed over a silica
gel column eluted with CH2Cl2/petroleum ether (0−100%) and
MeOH/CH2Cl2 (0−100%) in a gradient to yield 10 fractions (frs. 1−
10). Fr. 2 was divided into 12 subfractions by ODS silica gel
chromatography eluting with MeOH/H2O (10−100%). Frs. 2−8
were separated by semipreparative HPLC (67% MeOH/H2O, 3.0
mL/min) to obtain 5 (4.0 mg, tR = 7.5 min), 2 (80.0 mg, tR = 8.5
min), and 1 (18.3 mg, tR = 13.0 min). Fr. 4 was divided into 14
subfractions by ODS silica gel chromatography eluting with MeOH/
H2O (5−100%). Frs. 4 and 3 were separated by semipreparative
HPLC (50% MeCN/H2O, 3.0 mL/min) to afford 3 (46.1 mg, tR =
15.8 min), and a subfraction which was further purified by
semipreparative HPLC (68% MeOH/H2O, 2.5 mL/min) to yield 4
(21.7 mg, tR = 6.7 min). Fr. 6 was divided into 10 subfractions by
ODS silica gel chromatography eluting with MeOH/H2O (5−100%).
Frs. 6−10 were purified by semipreparative HPLC (43% MeCN/
H2O, 3.0 mL/min) to afford 7 (43.7 mg, tR = 14.5 min) and 6 (8.8
mg, tR = 15.3 min).

X-ray Crystallographic Analysis. The crystallographic data of
1−4, obtained in MeOH by slow evaporation, were collected by using
a XtaLAB PRO single-crystal diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation.
Their X-ray crystal structures were solved using SHELXS97, expanded
by difference Fourier techniques, and finally refined by full-matrix
least-squares calculations. The non-hydrogen atoms were refined
anisotropically, and hydrogen atoms were fixed at calculated positions.
The crystallographic data for these compounds have been deposited
in the Cambridge Crystallographic Data Centre.

Genome Sequencing. Whole genome scanning of Talaromyces
sp. SCSIO 41412 was conducted using the second generation
Illumina sequencing platforms (Shanghai Biozeron Biotechnology)
and assembled by ABySS (https://www.bcgsc.ca/platform/bioinfo/
software/abyss) and GapCloser software (https://sourceforge.net/
projects/soapdenovo2/files/GapCloser).
Bioinformatic Analysis. BGCs were analyzed by antiSMASH
7.0.0.25 ORFs were assigned, and their functions were predicted using
secondFind (https://biosyn.nih.go.jp/2ndfind) and the BLAST
program (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi).
PDE4 Inhibitory Assay. The activity of PDE4 was assessed using
the phosphodiesterase scintillation proximity assay, as described
previously.26 In short, the enzymatic activity of the catalytic domain
was assessed using 3H-cAMP or 3H-cGMP as substrates. The reaction
was conducted with a protein concentration of 2 nM in an assay
buffer containing Tris-HCl, MgCl2 or MnCl2, DTT, and 3H-cAMP or
3H- cGMP. After a 15 min incubation, the reaction was terminated
and the reaction product was precipitated, while the unreacted
substrates remained in the supernatant. The radioactivity of the
supernatant was measured using a liquid scintillation counter, and
IC50 values were determined based on multiple tests with various
compound concentrations.
Cell Viability Assay. Mononuclear macrophage mouse
RAW264.7 and MRC-5 cells were obtained from Procell Life Science
& Technology Co., Ltd. (catalog nos. CL-0190 and CL-0161).
RAW264.7 cells were cultured in DMEM complete growth medium
with 10% FBS (Gibco) and 1% penicillin−streptomycin (Gibco).
MRC-5 cells were cultured in MEM medium (contain NEAA) with
10% FBS and 1% penicillin−streptomycin. Cells were seeded at a
density of 5 × 106 cells/mL in a 96-well plate and cultured for 24 h
and subsequently treated with different concentrations of 1 for 48 h.
Then, 10 μL of cck8 reagent was added and the absorbance measured
at 450 nm.
qPCR Assay of Inflammation Related Gene Expression. The
protocols for the real-time qPCR assay of inflammation related gene
expression were similar to previous work.27 RAW264.7 cells were
seeded at 5 × 105 cells/well into 6-well plates and incubated at 37 °C
for 24 h, then 1 was added (20 and 10 μM, three replicate wells).
After being administered for 4 h, RAW264.7 cells were stimulated
with 10 μL of LPS (Sigma, final concentration 1 μg/mL) for 24 h.
The RNAs were extracted using the Trizol method (RNAiso plus,
Takara), and the detection of the target gene expression followed the
universal SYBR green qPCR protocol (Tli RNaseH Plus, Takara).
Pharmacodynamic Effects of 1 on the TGF-β1-Induced
MRC-5 Cell Model. MRC-5 cells were seeded overnight at 5 × 105
cells/mL. The cells were divided into three groups: the blank control
group, the model group, and the drug administration group. Except
for the blank group, 10 ng/mL TGF-β1 was added to induce the
model group and drug administration group. In the drug
administration group, 1 was added at final concentrations of 20, 10,
and 5 μM. After 48 h of incubation, the cells were subjected to qPCR
analysis, Western blotting, and immunofluorescence staining. For
Western blotting, total protein extraction was performed using a RIPA
buffer, and the protein concentration was quantified using the BCA
method. The proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate−
polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to a
polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. After being blocked with
5% skim milk, the membrane was incubated with primary antibodies
against FN1 (Cell Signaling Technology, 26836), COL1 (Cell
Signaling Technology, 72026), α-SMA (Abcam, ab184705), and
GAPDH (Proteintech, 60004-1-Ig) at 4 °C for 12 h. Following three
washes with TBST, the membranes were incubated with secondary
antibodies for 1 h and then detected using BeyoECL Moon reagents
(Beyotime) with the Amersham ImageQuant 800 (Cytiva) imaging
system.
Immunofluorescence Staining. After treatment with or without
TGF-β1 and 1, MRC-5 cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde
and permeabilized by 0.5% Triton X-100. The cell dishes were
incubated with 10% goat serum at room temperature for 0.5 h prior to
incubation with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. After washing
with PBST three times, the dishes were incubated with the Alexa
Fluor 488 goat antimouse IgG (green, Abcam) and Cy3 antirabbit
IgG (red, ThermoFisher) for 60 min and DAPI solution (blue,
Boster) for 15 min. Immunolabeling was examined using a laser
scanning confocal microscope (Olympus, FV3000).
Molecular Modeling. Docking studies utilized the crystal of
human PDE4D with roflumilast (PDB code: 1XOQ). The water was
removed from the structure, except for those coordinated with the
two metal ions Mg2+ and Zn2+. Hydrogen atoms and charges were
added to the systems using the CHARMM force field and the
Momany−Rone partial charge method, which were implemented in
Accelrys Discovery Studio 2.5.5. The ionizable residues were assigned
their protonation states based on a neutral pH. To define the active
site of PDE4, the bound roflumilast was used as a control. The active
compounds were then docked into the active site by using
CDOCKER. The radius of the input site sphere was set to 10 Å
from the center of the binding site. During the docking process, 10
random conformations were generated for each ligand. Default values
were used for the other parameters.
