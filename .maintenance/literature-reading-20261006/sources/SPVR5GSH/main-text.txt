Construction and the Elucidation of Key Amino Acids

ABSTRACT: Cytochrome P450 monooxygenases (CYP450s) play an
important role in the biosynthesis of natural products by activating inert
C−H bonds and inserting hydroxyl groups. However, the activities of most
plant-derived CYP450s are extremely low, limiting the heterologous
biosynthesis of natural products. Traditional enzyme engineering methods,
either rational or screening-based, are not suitable for CYP450s because of
the lack of crystal structures and high-throughput screening methods for
this class of enzymes. CYP725A4 is the first hydroxylase involved in the
biosynthesis pathway of Taxol. Its low activity, promiscuity, and
multispecificity make it a bottleneck in Taxol biosynthesis. Here, we
identified key amino acids that affect the in vivo activity of CYP725A4 by
constructing the ancestral enzymes of CYP725A4. We obtained positive mutants that showed an improved yield of hydroxylated
products based on the key amino acids identified, providing guidance for the modification of other CYP450s involved in the
biosynthesis of natural products.
1. INTRODUCTION
Cytochrome P450 monooxygenases (CYP450s) functionalize
unactivated C−H bonds by inserting oxygen atoms to generate
hydroxyl groups. Their high regio- and stereoselectivities are
impressive compared with the methods available to synthetic
organic chemistry.1 CYP450s contribute to the diversity of
natural products; the hydroxyl or oxygen-containing functional
groups they install are essential for pharmacological activity.2
However, most plant-derived CYP450s are membrane-bound
proteins with poor stability, have low expression levels, and are
challenging to heterologously express and purify.3 Traditional
enzyme engineering methods are not suitable for the
modification of these CYP450s; challenges in purification
and heterologous expression make it difficult to obtain crystal
structures, further hindering the rational design and
modification. In addition, screening high-capacity mutant
libraries is also barely feasible due to the absence of efficient
screening platforms.
Taxol (Paclitaxel), a diterpenoid originally extracted from
the bark of the Pacific yew tree (Taxus brevifolia), is a
blockbuster anticancer drug that has a positive therapeutic
effect on multiple human malignant tumors.4−6 However, due
to supply shortages, the increasing clinical demand for the
compound cannot be met. Traditional extraction from the
plant is unsustainable, total chemical synthesis is too complex,
the yield is too low, and the alternative semisynthetic methods
starting from baccatin III are still based on plant resources.7−9
With progress in synthetic biology, many natural products of
high medicinal value can now be synthesized in easily
cultivated microbial hosts, suggesting new options for the
overproduction of Taxol.10 However, the biosynthetic pathway
of Taxol is extremely complex. taxadiene synthase cyclizes the
universal diterpene precursor geranylgeranyl pyrophosphate
(GGPP) into taxadiene, and the subsequent enzymatic
modification utilizes at least eight CYP450-mediated hydrox-
ylation steps, of which some CYP450s remain uncharacterized.
The known taxane hydroxylases have low activity and poor
heterologous expression properties, and their catalytic
mechanisms are still unclear.9,11 The process of taxadiene
modification, therefore, represents a key bottleneck that limits
the overproduction of Taxol in microbial hosts.
taxadiene-5α-hydroxylase (T5αOH), CYP725A4, is the first
hydroxylase involved in the skeletal modification pathway of
Taxol; it hydroxylates taxadiene to produce taxadiene-5α-ol
and several other oxidation products, of which only taxadiene-
5α-ol can enter the following metabolic pathway to produce
the final product Taxol.12 The low activity and the poor
specificity of CYP725A4 lead to a low level of taxadiene-5α-ol
both in synthetic systems in microbial hosts and the natural
biosynthetic pathway.13 Enhancing the activity or the product
fidelities of CYP725A4 is therefore critical to the realization of
Taxol production in microbial hosts. However, only two

It is widely accepted that extant enzymes originate from
biocatalysts in response to changing environments.16 Com-
puter algorithms, with a phylogenetic tree of existing sequences
and statistical models of evolution, can be used to infer amino
acid sequences of evolutionary precursors.17 Many examples
have shown that reconstructed ancestral enzymes typically
exhibit activity against the substrates of extant enzymes, but
differences exist in their catalytic activity.18,19 Current research
is mostly focused on obtaining biocatalysts with improved
stability and expression through ancestral sequence recon-
struction (ASR)this is because the ancestral enzymes are
usually more robust.20,21 There is a lack of methods for
locating key catalytic residues of enzymes, where the residues
are difficult to predict based on ASR. We hypothesized that by
comparing the difference between ancestral and extant
sequences, key amino acids that affect the catalytic perform-
ance of these enzymes may be identified. A small mutant
library can then be constructed to improve the activity of
enzymes that are difficult to screen from a large library.
In this study, we combined bioinformatics and experimental
methods to enhance the in vivo activity of CYP725A4. Key
catalytic residues affecting the in vivo activity of CYP725A4
were identified by ASR. By replacing the amino acids at these
sites with the corresponding counterparts of the ancestors, a
“smart” mutant library was constructed, from which mutants
with significantly higher hydroxylation yields were selected.
These results provide a feasible solution to the modification of
other enzymes that lack structure and high-throughput
screening methods and also provide a strategy for the synthesis
of other rare terpenoids.
2. MATERIALS AND METHODS
2.1. ASR of the CYP725A Subfamily. A total of 21
CYP725A subfamily sequences and the CYP726B sequence
were collected from UniProt and NCBI. These sequences were
aligned using MAFFT.36 The alignment was fine-tuned
manually to improve the reliability at the gap positions.
Phylogenetic relationships of these CYP725A sequences were
reconstructed using the maximum likelihood method under
the Jones−Taylor−Thornton (JTT) substitution model with
MEGA7.37 The default parameters were used, and the initial
tree was determined by neighbor-joining (BIONJ).38 Boot-
strapping analysis with 1000 replicates was performed to
evaluate the obtained tree. The tree was rooted using the
branch of CYP716 as an outgroup, and the ancestors of the
CYP725A subfamily were inferred using PAML.39 The codon-
optimized nucleotide sequence was synthesized by GenScript
for better expression in Escherichia coli, in which the first 24
amino acids of enzymes were replaced with MALLLAVFL, an
eight-residue synthetic peptide from bovine, as previously
shown to facilitate the expression of P450s in E. coli
BL21(DE3).40
2.2. Plasmid Construction and DNA Manipulation. All
strains, plasmids, and DNA sequences used in this study are
given in Table S1. All plasmids were constructed by a
ClonExpress Ultra One-Step Cloning Kit. This is because
isopentenyl diphosphate isomerase (IDI) and 1-deoxy-
xylulose-5-phosphate synthase (DXS) are the most important
rate-limiting enzymes in the endogenous MEP pathway of E.
coli.41 The corresponding idi and dxs genes were constructed
on plasmid pET28a(+) (plasmid1). The genes of GGPPS, TS,
and hydroxylases (ancestors or T5αOH) were constructed on
the plasmid pRSFDuet-1 (plasmid2). The GSTGS peptide was
used as a linker between P450 and its cognate redox partner,
NADPH-cytochrome P450 reductase (CPR). The N-terminal
74 amino acids of CPR were truncated to increase the
solubility during intracellular expression. hydroxylases can use
taxadiene as a substrate to produce the corresponding
hydroxylated products when these plasmids are coexpressed
in heterologous host E. coli BL21(DE3).
2.3. Culture Medium and Reagents. The LB medium
contains 10 g L−1 NaCl, 10 g L−1 peptone, and 5 g L−1 yeast
extract; the TB medium contains 30 g L−1 glycerol, 24 g L−1
yeast extract, 12 g L−1 peptone, 16.4 g L−1 K2HPO4, and 2.3 g
L−1 KH2PO4. peptone and yeast extract were purchased from
OXOID; glycerol and anhydrous sodium sulfate were
purchased from Titan; n-octadecane (>98%) and n-dodecane
(>98%) were from Meryer; NaCl, K2HPO4, KH2PO4, and 5-
aminolevulinic acid hydrochloride (ALA, >99%) were
purchased from Aladdin; antibiotics (kanamycin, K+; chlor-
amphenicol, C+; ampicillin, A+) and IPTG were purchased
from Shanghai Shenggong Bioengineering Company; a
ClonExpress Ultra One-Step Cloning Kit was purchased
from Vazyme.
2.4. Shake Flask Cultivation. First, single colonies of the
constructed strains were inoculated into 4 mL of the LB
medium supplemented with appropriate antibiotics and were
cultured in a rotary shaker at 200 rpm, 37 °C for 12 h. Second,
1 mL of the overnight seed culture was transferred to 50 mL of
the TB medium (supplemented with the corresponding
antibiotics when necessary: 100 μg mL−1 for ampicillin and
50 μg mL−1 for chloramphenicol). The cultivation was
performed at 37 °C and 200 rpm until the optical density
(OD600) of the culture reached around 1.2, and then, the
temperature was reduced to 22 °C for precooling around 20
min. Third, IPTG and 5-amino levulinic acid were added to
each shake flask with final concentrations of 0.1 and 0.2 mM,
respectively. Additionally, 10% (v/v) n-dodecane was added to
each shaking flask with the purpose of extracting the products
during fermentation, and product concentrations mentioned in
this article were those in n-dodecane phase.
2.5. Scale-Up Fermentation. Benchtop scale-up fermen-
tation was carried out in a 3-L fermentor. The constructed
strain was first removed from −80 °C and streaked onto an LB
agar plate containing appropriate antibiotics, and after
incubation at 37 °C for 12 h, a single colony was picked and
then inoculated into a 4 mL LB medium in a 10 mL tube
containing appropriate antibiotics and grown at 37 °C
overnight. The overnight culture was then transferred to a
shake flask culture medium with 1% inoculation and cultivated
for 8 h. Until 1% of the inoculation amount was transferred to
a 300 mL shake flask, we continued to cultivate until we

2.6. Gas Chromatography−Mass Spectrometry (GC-
MS) Analysis. The samples were collected from the n-
dodecane overlayer and analyzed by GC-MS with full-scan
mode (m/z 50−350) and Rtx-5MS column (0.25 × 30 m, 0.25
μm film thickness) on a GC-MS QP2010 SE (Shimadzu,
Kyoto, Japan), and helium was used as carrier gas. The mass
spectrum was collected by electron ionization. The oven
program was as follows: 80 °C (1 min hold), 80−220 °C (15
°C min−1), and 220−250 °C (20 °C min−1, 1 min hold). The
solvent delay was set at 8 min. Samples were normalized by
using the n-octadecane internal standard. The product
recognition was judged by the characteristic ion fragment
size and molecular weight of the mass spectrum peak, and the
peak areas of the product and the internal standard were
integrated. The same GC protocol was followed for FID.
2.7. Homology Modeling. Homology modeling of
T5αOH and its four ancestors was performed using
AlphaFold2 (AF2).42 Heme was added using multiple template
modeling in Modeler 9.11.43 CYP102A1 (UniProtKB:
Q59990, PDB: 2ve3, residues 391−400), which has the
highest sequence identity (35.25%) with T5αOH in the PDB
database, was selected as the second template for multiple
template modeling to provide a reference coordinate for heme
binding in the model.
3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
3.1. Reconstruction and In Vivo Characterization of
CYP725A4 Ancestors. The amino acid sequences of putative
ancestors can be calculated and inferred from the phylogenetic
tree of existing sequences and statistical models of evolution.17
T5αOH was used as the probe for a homology search
(BLAST) in the NCBI database. All enzymes belonging to the
CYP725A subfamily in the search results were selected,
together with the reported six extant taxane hydroxylases
derived from Taxus species (T5αOH, T10βOH, T2αOH,
T13αOH, T7βOH, and T14βOH), for a total of 21 sequences.
Additionally, T9αOH from Ginkgo biloba,22 which belongs to
the CYP716B subfamily, was also selected to serve as an
outgroup for the construction of the evolutionary tree; its
sequence identity with the existing six natural taxane
hydroxylases is between 40 and 47%. In total, 22 sequences
were obtained for ancestral sequence reconstruction. The
phylogenetic tree is shown in Figure 1.
To investigate the feasibility of identifying the key catalytic
amino acids of T5αOH, we turned our attention to the branch
that leads to T5αOH. Ancestors of this branch include four
nodes, A1−A4. The sequence consistency between ancestors
and T5αOH decreases with increasing evolutionary distance,
and the putative ancestral sequences differ from the T5αOH
by 11.9% (A4), 13.3% (A3), 19.8% (A2), and 21.3% (A1).
Next, we investigated the hydroxylation activities of these
four ancestors toward taxadiene. Due to the difficulty of
obtaining purified enzymes, we did not carry out in vitro assays;
instead, we used intracellular fermentation. First, T5αOH and
its ancestors were modified at the N-terminus and then fused
with the NADPH-cytochrome P450 reductase (CPR). Next,
these fused proteins were expressed in engineered E. coli with
engineered metabolic pathways containing taxadiene syn-
thase,23 in which the substrate, taxa-4(5),11(12)-diene,
accumulates to a high level (∼100 mg L−1).
All four ancestral enzymes had taxadiene hydroxylation
activity. GC-MS analysis of the solvent overlay showed that
products generated by the ancestors were the same as those
generated by T5αOH. Four monooxygenated compounds
dominated the total fermentation products. The GC and mass
spectra (Figure S1) of 5(13)-oxa-3(11)-cyclotaxane (iso-OCT,
1), 5(12)-oxa-3(11)-cyclotaxane (OCT, 3), and taxadiene-5α-
ol (4) were fully consistent with those reported the

3.2. Exploring the Key Catalytic Amino Acids of
T5αOH from an Evolutionary Perspective. Though
T5αOH and the ancestors A1−A4 can all hydroxylate
taxadiene, their product distributions are different. We believe
that by comparing and analyzing the differences in the amino
acid sequences of these five enzymes, we can identify the key
catalytic amino acids of T5αOH. The sequence identities
among T5αOH and its four ancestors were 79−88% (Table
S1). Around 60 amino acids (out of 474) differ between
ancestor A4 and T5αOH, despite A4 having the highest
similarity to T5αOH, and more than 100 amino acids differ
between A1 and T5αOH. To quickly identify the key amino
acids from these numerous mutation sites and avoid the
construction of large-scale mutant libraries, structure-based
multisequence alignment was employed. We envisioned that
identifying the mutant regions and mapping them onto the
structures could reveal the important catalytic amino acids of
T5αOH.

These results indicate that the amino acids in this mutation
“hotspot” have a significant impact on the yield of
monooxygenated products. Additionally, due to the short
distance between these two mutation sites, we speculate that
there may be a synergistic effect between them.
To further increase the yield of the total oxidation products
and taxadiene-5α-ol, these two positive variants were iterated
with the hope of obtaining mutants with a higher yield. After
30 h of flask fermentation (50 mL), the specific yields of
monooxygenated taxanes and taxadiene-5α-ol of double-point
mutant T5αOH (V124I−S127A) further increased to 14.4 ±
1.7 and 2.8 ± 0.3 mg gDCW−1, respectively (Figure 4A).
In a 3-L fermenter, the level of these four monooxygenated
products increased with fermentation time, and the ratio of
products did not change significantly. After 48 h of
fermentation, the titer of total monooxygenated products was
110.3 mg L−1 (space-time yield, 55.2 mg L−1 day−1), of which
taxadien-5α-ol was 22.5 mg L−1 (Figure 5). Additionally, no
iso-OCT was detected at a fermentation time of 12 h, probably
because the yield of iso-OCT was too low during the early
fermentation phase to be detected, or the formation of this
product occurred in a later period than the three other
monooxygenated diterpenoids.

Several studies have reported the construction of metabolic
pathways in E. coli to generate oxygenated taxanes. Wu et al.
constructed an E. coli cell factory with optimized mevalonate
(MVA) and 2-C-methyl-D-erythritol-4-phosphate (MEP) path-
ways; the biosynthetic genes related to taxadien-5α-ol were
integrated into two plasmids, and total titers of 27 mg L−1 for
oxygenated taxanes and 7 mg L−1 for taxadien-5α-ol were
obtained in a 50 mL shake flask fermentation.26 To reduce the
number of plasmids, Biggs et al. integrated precursor genes
into the genome and optimized the promoter strength. After 9
days of fermentation in a 2-L bioreactor, a total titer of 570 mg
L−1 (space-time yield, 63.3 mg L−1 day−1) for oxygenated
taxanes was achieved.24 Modifications aimed at the chassis cells
have been carried out; however, wild-type T5αOH was used in
all of the above studies. The mutant described here can be
applied to other fermentation systems with the expectation of
an increased production yield, thereby providing guidance for
improving the yield of terpenoids in heterologous biosynthesis.
3.3. Discussion. When the activity of biocatalysts is
insufficient to meet the production demand, traditional
enzyme engineering methods are often applied to improve
catalytic performance. There are two main conventional
engineering strategies. One strategy is the creation of a
small-scale mutant library guided by the crystal structure or
highly reliable homologous models. The other strategy is the
screening of a large-scale random mutant library to obtain
positive mutants. However, for most CYP450s involved in
natural product biosynthesis, especially those derived from
fungi or plants, neither of the traditional modification strategies
is feasible. The heterologous expression level and stability of
CYP450s are low, making it challenging to obtain crystal
structures.29,30 In addition, the sequence identity of these
biocatalysts with enzymes that have crystal structures is fairly
low (usually less than 40%); this results in poorly reliable
homology modeling.31 Furthermore, the catalytic mechanism
of these P450s is still unclear, making the establishment of
relatively small-scale mutant libraries unsuitable, and effective
high-throughput screening methods do not exist, rendering the
establishment and screening of a large-scale mutant library
time- and labor-consuming.32 As a result, the modification of
these enzymes is still very difficult, which markedly limits the
heterologous biosynthesis of natural products.
The biosynthesis pathway of specific natural products
involves multiple CYP450s, which usually belong to the
same CYP450 family or subfamily. For example, hydroxylases
involved in the biosynthesis of Taxol all belong to the
CYP725A subfamily.31 The CYP72A subfamily was found to
be capable of biosynthesizing multiple kinds of β-amyrin
skeleton triterpenoids, such as glycyrrhetinic acid, gypsogenic
acid, and hederagenin.33,34 The CYP97 family, including
CYP97A3, CYP97A4, CYP97B29, CYP97C1, and CYP97C2,
contributes to the biosynthesis of tetraterpenoid xantho-
phylls;35 their high sequence identities and their ability to
accept structurally similar substrates indicate that they may
originate from the same ancestor. In these cases, ASR can be
used as an enzyme engineering method to screen positive
mutants that are difficult to obtain using traditional methods.
Another critical issue in the CYP725A4-mediated biosyn-
thesis is the generation of multiple products. In ancestors A1
and A2, significantly more iso-OCT was produced than
diterpenoid1; however, in A3, A4, and T5αOH, this ratio
was reversed. In addition, the product ratios of the single-point
and multipoint CYP725A4 mutants were similar to those of
wild-type CYP725A4, indicating that too few mutations are
present to change the production ratios. Therefore, to study
the selectivity, we need to expand the scope of the screening
and investigate even older ancestors.
