ABSTRACT: Six new thiazole-containing cyclic peptides, the cyclotheonella-
zoles D−I (1−6), were isolated from the Australian marine sponge Theonella sp.
(2131) with their structures assigned by comprehensive 1D and 2D NMR
spectroscopic and MS spectrometric analyses, Marfey’s derivatization studies,
and comparison with time-dependent density functional theory (TDDFT)
calculated ECD data. The Type 2 azole-homologated peptides herein comprise
up to five nonproteinogenic amino acids, including the protease transition state
mimic α-keto-β-amino acid residue 3-amino-4-methyl-2-oxohexanoic acid
(Amoha), while 1−3 also contain a terminal hydantoin residue not previously
found in cyclotheonellazoles. The keramamides A (7) and L (8) were reisolated
affording expanded exploration of their biological activities. The peptides were
examined for protease inhibitory activities against two mammalian serine proteases (elastase and chymotrypsin) and SARS-CoV-2 3-
chymotrypsin-like protease (3CLpro), a validated antiviral therapeutic target for COVID-19. Peptides 1−6 and keramamide A (7)
displayed potent nanomolar inhibition of elastase (IC50 16.0 to 61.8 nM), while 7 also contained modest inhibition of chymotrypsin
and SARS-CoV-2 3CLpro (IC50 0.73 and 1.1 μM, respectively). The cyclotheonellazoles D−E (1−3) do not affect the viability of
human breast, ovarian, and colon cancer cells (>100 μM), with the cytotoxicity previously reported for keramamide L (8) not
replicated (inactive >20 μM).
T
he marine environment continues to provide an excellent
source of drugs with 17 marine natural product derived
or inspired therapeutics currently approved by regulatory
bodies worldwide.1 Recently, the clinically approved marine
depsipeptide, plitidepsin (Aplidin), was reported with potent
in vitro antiviral activity against SARS-CoV-2 more than 25
times greater than the currently prescribed antiviral treatment
remdesivir.2 Proteases, proteolytic enzymes that catalyze the
breakdown of proteins, are involved in highly regulated
signaling pathways controlling important physiological systems
making them potential therapeutic targets for the treatment of
cancers and disease caused by inflammation and infections.3
Elastase, a serine protease concentrated in the lungs (among
other organs), upregulates neutrophil production during
inflammatory events caused by viral infections such as
COVID-19 which can lead to acute respiratory distress
syndrome (ARDS) and acute lung injury (ALI).4−6 Moreover,
3-chymotrypsin-like cysteine protease (3CLpro), also referred
to as the main protease, is a validated viral protein target for
inhibiting the replication of severe acute respiratory syndrome
coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), the virus responsible for the
COVID-19 pandemic.7 For the foreseeable future, small
molecule antiviral treatments for COVID-19 will continue to
be in demand due to both, the unpredictability of new variants
and the likelihood of drug resistance emerging for current FDA
approved drugs, Paxolid (nirmatrelvir in combination with
ritonavir), Lagevrio (molnupiravir), and Veklury (remdesi-
vir).8,9
Marine sponges, particularly those belonging to the genus
Theonella, are a proven source of peptide-containing natural
products often with diverse biological activities. Of 292 marine
natural products reported from Theonella species positioned
within the MarinLit database (2023), nearly 57% (165)
contain amide functionality.10 Furthermore, Theonella peptides
are often cyclic with modified nonproteinogenic amino acids
and are biosynthesised by nonribosomal synthetase/polyketide
synthases (NRPS-PKS).11 Azole-containing (oxazole and
thiazole) cyclic peptides are also characteristic of NRPS-PKS
biosynthesis, pathways of which are proposed (both genetically
and chemically) to originate from symbiotic γ-proteobacterium
Entotheonella sp.12−14 The macrocycles of azole-homologated
peptides (AHPs) contain tetrads of conserved amino acid
residues and can be broadly classified as Type 1 or 2
depending on the presence of either, ornithine (Type 1) or

The biological evaluations of Type 2 AHPs has mainly

exception of keramamide K reported with modest cytotoxicity
against KB and murine leukemia cancer cell lines.19 However,
of most interest for Type 2 AHP bioactivity is the potent
nanomolar inhibition of the mammalian serine proteases,

alleviate ALI and lung deterioration in bleomycin (BLM)-
induced mouse models outlining the therapeutic potential of
Type-2 AHP elastase inhibitors for the potential treatment of
ARDS/ALI induced by viruses such as COVID-19.5,20
Herein we report six new Type 2 AHPs, the cyclo-
theonellazoles D−I (1−6), and the known anabaenopeptin-
type peptides, keramamides A (7) and L (8), from Theonella
sp. (2131). Their potential protease activities were examined
against both mammalian and viral ones, including chymo-
trypsin, elastase and SARS-CoV-2 3-chymotrypsin-like pro-
tease (3CLPro), in addition to their effects on the cell viability
of three human cancer cell lines (ovarian, breast, and prostate).

A sample of Theonella sp. (2131) collected by SCUBA from
Osprey Reef, an isolated underwater seamount located in the
Coral Sea located more than 300 km east of the North
Queensland coastline in North Eastern Australia, was
exhaustively extracted in MeOH and partitioned between
EtOAc, hexanes, n-butanol and H2O.

Cyclotheonellazole E (2) was isolated as a deep brown oil
displaying a sodiated [M + Na]+ molecule in the (+)
HRESIMS at m/z 891.2350 establishing the molecular formula
C36H45N8Na2O12S2 and 18 degrees of unsaturation. The 1H
NMR data for 2 (Table 2) clearly indicated a shared structure
with 1, however differences in the shielded regions of the
spectra suggested that the 2 contained a different amino acid
residue coinciding with a molecular weight 14 amu less from
the MS. The methyl doublets and methine proton resonances
representing Leu in 1 were not present in 2, and instead a
methyl triplet at δH 0.92 (Apa-H5) and methylene proton
resonances at δH 1.53 and 1.32 (Apa-H4) were observed.
Analysis of 2’s HMBC, COSY and TOCSY spectra enabled the
assignment of the nonproteinogenic amino acid 2-amino-
propanoic acid (Apa) an analogue 14 amu less than the Leu-
containing 1.
Cyclotheonellazole F (3) was also isolated as a deep brown
oil and was represented by a sodiated [M + Na]+ molecule at
m/z 877.2237 in the HRESIMS consistent with the molecular
formula C35H43N8Na2O12S2 and 18 degrees of unsaturation.
The NMR data for 3 (Table 2) were almost identical with 2
and 1, however it displayed a mass 14 amu less than 2
suggesting the loss of a methylene group from its structure. 2D
NMR analysis corroborated observations made from the MS
establishing the modified amino acid homoalanine (hAla) in 3
instead of Leu and Apa residues in 1 and 2, respectively.
Cyclotheonellazole G (4) was isolated as a brown oil and with
a sodiated [M + Na]+ molecule in the HRESIMS spectrum at
m/z 879.2759 consistent with the molecular formula
C36H49N8Na2O11S2 and 16 degrees of unsaturation. The 1H
NMR data for 4 (Table 3) shared similarities with the Leu-
containing cyclotheonellazole D (1), however, the methyl
doublet proton resonance at δH 1.23 (Hyd-H3) of hydantoin
in 1 was slightly more deshielded in 4 at δH 1.30 (Ala-H3).
Further, the
1H NMR data for 4 displayed an extra
exchangeable proton resonance at δH 8.06 (Ala-NH2) not
present in 1, while 4’s 13C NMR spectrum also contained one
less carbonyl resonance. An Ala residue was ascribed to 4
instead of the hydantoin residue in structures 1−3, with
HMBC correlations observed from a doublet methyl resonance
at δH 1.30 (Ala-H3) to an α-methine carbon signal at δC 48.2
(Ala-C2) and an amide carbonyl carbon resonance at δC 168.7
(Ala-C1). Further HMBC correlations from both, the α-NH
proton signal of Dap at δH 8.53 (Dap-α-NH) and from the α-

carbonyl resonance of Ala at δC 168.7 (Ala-C1) confirmed the
condensation of Ala with the macrocycle at the Dap residue
establishing 4 as an Ala homologue of 1.
The HRESIMS spectrum for cyclotheonellazole H (5)
contained a sodiated [M + Na]+ molecule at m/z 865.2600
consistent with the molecular formula C35H47N8Na2O11S2 and
16 degrees of unsaturation. The NMR spectroscopic data for 5
(Table 3) contained similarities with both, 2 and 4. A terminal
Ala residue was assigned to the α-NH of Dap consistent with
that in 4 while an Apa residue the same as 2 finalized the
planar structure of 5. The last compound we report in this
series, cyclotheonellazole I (6), was isolated as a brown oil and
with a sodiated [M + Na]+ molecule in the HRESIMS
spectrum at m/z 851.2441 consistent with the molecular

The NMR (Table 3) and MS data for 6 established it as a hAla
analogue of peptides 4 and 5. Due to the low isolation yield of
6 (0.4 mg), our previously reported NMR mass quantification
method for DMSO-d6 was used to accurately quantify the mass
of the submilligram sample.22
The macrocycles present within the cyclotheonellazoles D−I
(1−6) are consistent with those reported for the cyclo-
theonellazoles A-C12 consisting of tetrads of conserved
nonproteinogenic amino acid residues, including 4-propeno-
yl-2-tyrosylthiazole (Ptt), cysteic acid (CysO3), 3-amino-4-
methyl-2-oxohexanoic acid (Amoha), and 2,3-diaminopropa-
noic acid (Dap), alongside either leucine (Leu, 1 and 4), 2-
aminopropanoic acid (Apa, 2 and 5) or homoalanine (hAla, 3
and 6). The cyclotheonellazoles D−F (1−3) and G−I (4−6)
are terminal hydantoin and alanine analogues respectively, of
the previously reported thiazole-homologated peptides,
oriamide17 and the cyclotheonellazoles A-C.12 The terminal
hydantoin residue contained in the cyclotheonellazoles D-F
(1−3) is relatively uncommon and features in only 78 of
39 906 marine natural products deposited within the MarinLit
database (2023).10

ascribed using orthogonal methods including Marfey’s LCMS
derivatization studies,23 comparative NMR data analyses (with
NMR data reported for synthetic cyclotheonellazole A),12,20,21

theory (TDDFT) calculated ECD data. The absolute

and 4 were established by their hydrolysis and subsequent

30% H2O2/0.1 M NaOH before acid hydrolysis (6 M HCl,
110 °C, 24 h), while a separate sample of 4 was subjected to
acid hydrolysis only to provide a baseline for the potential
epimerization of the peroxy-hydrolyzed residues. The D and L

IC50 0.73 μM. Interestingly, 1−6 displayed only micromolar

activities reported for the cyclotheonellazoles A−C12 suggest-
ing that the terminal tripeptide is important for this activity.
The peptides 1−5 and 7−8 were also examined against
SARS-CoV-2 3CLPro, a viral protease containing a unique
cleavage site compared with human ones that hydrolyzes viral
polyproteins into functional ones important for viral
replication.7,29,30 To measure inhibition of SARS-CoV-2
3CLPro the cleavage of two fluorescent proteins by the viral
protease were measured in the presence and absence of the
reducing reagent dithiothreitol (DTT).7 In the absence of
DTT keramamide A (7) displayed modest inhibition of 3CLPro
at IC50 1.1 μM, while keramamide L (8, IC50 4.6 μM) and the
cyclotheonellazoles G (4, IC50 6.1 μM) and H (5, IC50 16.1
μM) contained only weak activities (Table 4). Interestingly, all
of the peptides examined against SARS-CoV-2 3CLPro lost
activity in the presence of dithiothreitol (DTT, 4 mM), a result
suggested to indicate nonspecific binding activity of com-
pounds containing thiol reactivities with cysteine proteases.29
To examine this hypothesis further, we took two aliquots
(sample A and B) of cyclotheonellazole D (1) and acquired 1H
NMR data on both, one treated with DTT (4 mM, sample A),
and the other without (sample B, see S54−55). The NMR
data obtained for both samples were virtually identical (except
for the presence of proton signals that could be assigned to
DTT in sample A) and contained no visible changes in proton
chemical shifts suggesting that no reaction had occurred
between 1 and DTT. Additionally, keramamides A (7) and L
(8), both of which contain chemical structures without obvious
sites for thiol reactivity, were also significantly less active in the
presence of DTT. Our findings suggest that the 3CLPro results
obtained for the peptides reported herein in the presence of
DTT be interpreted with some caution since reducing agents
can be expected to disrupt the formation of a tetrahedral
hemiketal covalent bond by α-keto amides with serine
proteases.12,31,32 Moreover, the addition of reducing agents
such as DTT could also dissuade the potential discovery of
other compounds without thiol reactivities, particularly those
that bind allosterically. Despite this, the cyclotheonellazoles
displayed a clear SAR in-line with the known specificities of
3CLPro.29 There was a preference for peptides containing a
terminal Ala over Hyd residue (4, 5 > 1) while Leu was
preferred over Apa and hAla. The preference for Leu at P2 and
Ala at P3 is consistent with the substrate specificity of SARS-
CoV-2 3CLPro indicating a strong preference for Leu at P2,
while hydrophobic amino acids at P3 are generally considered
more favorable.29
The peptides 1−3 and 8 were examined for cytotoxicity
against human ovarian (OVCAR), colon (HCT-116) and
breast (MDA-231) cancer cell lines with the cyclotheonella-
zoles D−F (1−3) showing no effect on cell viability up to 100
μM, while keramamide L (8) only displayed cytotoxic effects at
concentration greater than 20 μM. The toxicity profiles
obtained for the cyclotheonellazoles D−F (1−3) are consistent
with the cellular safety reported for synthetic cyclotheonella-
zole A which displayed no cytotoxic effects up to 100 μM
against two mammalian cell lines, human lung epithelial
(BEAS-2B) and ACE2 overexpression (ACE2+-293T) cells.20
Interestingly, keramamide L (8) was reported with moderate
cytotoxicity against L1210 murine leukemia and KB
epidermoid carcinoma cells (IC50 0.50 and 0.97 μM,
respectively),19 however, this activity was not replicated in
the three cancer cell lines examined in this study.
The potent elastase inhibition ascribed to the cyclo-
theonellazoles D−I (1−6) further supports the potential of
these and other Type-2 AHP analogues for inhibiting elastase-
related neutrophil overexpression caused by inflammatory
responses to respiratory viruses such as COVID-19. It was
found that cyclotheonellazole A relieved alveolar and
histopathological damage in the lungs of bleomycin-induced
ALI mouse models while a significant reduction in
inflammatory granulocytes and monocytes was also observed.20
Additionally, the cellular safety of the cyclotheonellazole series
has been further demonstrated with no cytotoxic effects
observed against three human cancer cell lines tested in this
study. Moreover, the reisolation of keramamides A and L (7
and 8) afforded excellent opportunity to expand their
biological testing diversity with 7 found to contain potent
elastase inhibition and modest activities against chymotrypsin
and SARS-CoV-2 3CLPro. Recently, our group has published
two comprehensive meta-analyses exploring marine natural
product biological activities and trends. The first study found
that 68% of new compounds reported between 2016 and 2020
were inactive for cytotoxicity, antimicrobial and anti-inflam-
matory activities,33 while our second meta-analysis exploring
marine indole alkaloid diversity and bioactivity found that
most were inactive for cytotoxicity and antimicrobial activities
despite the high amount of testing in these areas and that a
large proportion of marine indole chemical space was likely
nontoxic.34 The bioactivities reported herein support our
suggestion for more thoughtful approaches to the biological
evaluation of marine natural products and an increasing
emphasis on exploring disease and infection targets amenable
to compounds with nontoxic activities.

General Experimental Procedures. UV spectra were recorded
on a Shimadzu UV-1800 spectrophotometer while optical rotations
were acquired on a JASCO P-1020 polarimeter with [α] D values
given in 10−1 deg cm2 g−1. One and two-dimensional NMR spectra

to the solvent peak for DMSO-d6 at δH 2.50 and δC 39.52,
respectively. A Bruker Shigemi NMR tube (5 mm) matched for
DMSO-d6 was used to acquire a 13C NMR spectrum for 6 on a Bruker
Avance III HDX 800 MHz NMR spectrometer. Positive mode high
resolution electrospray ionization mass measurements and LCMS
data were acquired using ACN (0.1% formic acid) and water (0.1%
formic acid) as the mobile phase on an Agilent Technologies 6530
Accurate-Mass Q-TOF LC/MS with a 1200 Series autosampler and

7000 interface, were used for MPLC and HPLC purification. MPLC
and HPLC fractions were collected by a Gilson 215 liquid handler
and with solvents evarporated using a GeneVac HT-12 Series II
evaporation system. The solvents used for chromatography were
Scharlau HPLC grade, and H2O was Millipore Milli-QPF filtered.

derivatization analyses were carried out using an Ultimate 3000 RS
UHPLC coupled to a Thermo Fisher ISQ EC single quadrupole ESI
mass spectrometer.

has a membranous surface encrusted with microrhabds (8−12 μm).
The skeleton is composed of confused arrangement of oxeas (185−
380 μm) deep within the choanosome, from which emerge rising
paucispicular tracts of the same oxeas extending to the ectosome,

Extraction and Isolation. The freeze-dried and ground Theonella
sp. sample (1.3 kg) was extracted exhaustively in MeOH and
concentrated to dryness affording an orange/brown crude extract
(204.5 g). The crude extract was partitioned between EtOAc and
H2O (3 × 300 mL EtOAc) with the organic phase collected and
concentrated before subsequent partitioning between 90% aqueous
MeOH (250 mL) and hexane (3 × 250 mL) yielding fraction 1 (7.60
g). The aqueous fraction was partitioned between n-butanol (3 × 200
mL) and H2O with the organic phase affording fraction 2 (36.6 g).
Fraction 1 (7.60 g) was adsorbed to C18 silica gel and loaded onto an
MPLC column (40 mm × 300 mm) filled with C18 bonded silica and
eluted at 9.0 mL/min in 100% H2O for 30 min, a gradient to 100%
MeOH over 120 min, followed by elution at 100% MeOH for a
further 40 min (fractions collected each min to afford 190 fractions).

Computational Methods. Using an energy window of 21.0 kJ/
mol, a thorough conformer search (>30 000 steps) was undertaken for
cyclotheonellazole F (3) within the Schrödinger Macromodel 2016
software suite using the Monte Carlo Minimum method (MCMM)
and the OPLS3 force field. The 670 conformers obtained for 3 were
filtered based on the torsion angles for key
3JHH proton NMR
coupling constants (J = 11.2 and 3.0 Hz) observed between L-Dap
residue’s α-methine (Dap-H2) and β-methylene protons (Dap-H3)
within a range of ±2.0 Hz.24 The remaining 77 conformers were
subjected to gas phase density functional theory (DFT) geometry
optimization (GO) and single point energy calculations at the
B3LYP/6-31g (d) level of theory with GD3 empirical dispersion using
Gaussian 16.37 Conformers >5.0 kcal/mol above the energy minima
were removed with the remainder undergoing a second GO at the
B3LYP/6-311G(d,p) level of theory using the polarizable continuum
model (PCM) for methanol.38 Duplicate and high-energy conformers
(above 3.0 kcal/mol) were filtered from the second GO conforma-
tional set with 32 conformers subjected to electronic transition and
rotational strength calculations using the time-dependent density
functional (TDDFT) method at the CAM-B3LYP/6-311G(d,p) level
of theory including GD3 empirical dispersion and the PCM solvent
effect in methanol. Boltzmann-weighted TDDFT UV and ECD
spectra were generated and matched with experimental spectra using
Gaussian band shapes and a sigma gamma value (eV) of 0.28 (UV
correction of −16) using the freely available SpecDis software
(version 1.71).39 The Automation processes with the “Gowonda”
High-performance computing cluster (HPC) were carried out using a
Windows 10 PC and customized Python scripts originally based upon
the amended Willoughby protocol.40
