 19    Abstract: Bisbenzylisoquinoline alkaloids (bisBIAs) are pharmacologically valuable plant
 20    metabolites with complex stereochemical architectures, yet the catalytic principles governing
 21    their assembly have remained largely unclear. Here, we elucidate the enzymatic pathway to
 22    cyclic bisBIAs and uncover a non-canonical redox-mediated mechanism for post-assembly
 23    stereochemical control. We identify cytochrome P450 enzymes that catalyze regioselective
 24    oxidative dimerization and macrocyclization of benzylisoquinoline monomers, establishing the
 25    macrocyclic scaffold. Subsequent stereochemical specification is achieved by a paired oxidase-
 26    reductase module that selectively epimerizes a single stereocenter through a transient imine
 27    formation, converting (R,S)-configured intermediates to (S,S)-products. Reconstitution of the
 28   pathway in yeast enabled production of both native bisBIAs and non-natural analogs,
 29    demonstrating pathway modularity and engineering potential. These results establish the
 30    biochemical  principle  underlying  bisBIA  biosynthesis  and  provide  a  framework  for
 31   programmable biosynthesis of these complex natural products.

 32   Main Text: Benzylisoquinoline alkaloids (BIAs) constitute a chemically coherent group of
 33    plant specialized metabolites that includes widely used drugs such as morphine and noscapine1.
 34   Among more than 2,500 structurally distinct BIAs identified to date2, bisbenzylisoquinoline
 35    alkaloids (bisBIAs) represent a particularly elaborate subclass, generated through oxidative
 36    phenol coupling between two 1-benzylisoquinoline alkaloids (1-BIA) units (Fig. 1a)3. Members
 37    of this family have played prominent roles in medicine and pharmacology, ranging from early
 38    neuromuscular blockers such as tubocurarine to widely used therapeutic agents including
 39    cepharanthine and semisynthetic derivatives such as cisatracurium4,5. In addition, several
 40   bisBIAs have demonstrated clinical or preclinical utility, including tetrandrine, which inhibits
 41   Ebola  virus  replication by  targeting lysosomal  integral membrane protein-2  to  disrupt
 42    sphingosine-mediated calcium signaling6,7; cepharanthine, which interferes with viral entry,
 43    positioning  it as a promising therapeutic lead against SARS-CoV-28,9; and methylated
 44    derivatives of daurisoline, which modulate GLP-1-mediated glucose metabolism10. It is thus
 45    evident that the bisBIA scaffold holds high promise for therapeutic applications by targeting
 46    diverse pathologies ranging from viral infections to metabolic disorders.

 47    Structural diversity within the bisBIA class arises from a limited set of 1-BIA precursors3,11.
 48    Oxidative phenol coupling generates dimeric  scaffolds  that  differ  in linkage  position,
 49    macrocyclization, and stereochemical configuration (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Fig. 1), producing
 50    multiple chiral centers and conformationally flexible biaryl frameworks3,12,13. These features
 51    generate discrete three-dimensional geometries with measurable differences in aromatic
 52    stacking,  electronic  delocalization, and  solvent  accessibility, which  directly  influence
 53    physicochemical behavior and ligand-binding  potential across  the  subclass14,15. Despite
 54    extensive structural characterization, the biosynthetic logic governing the assembly of bisBIAs,

 55    particularly the origin and control of stereochemistry, has remained poorly understood16–18.
 56    Current models propose that biosynthesis begins with cytochrome P450 (CYP)-mediated
 57    intermolecular oxidative phenol coupling of two benzylisoquinoline monomers, followed by a
 58    series of downstream transformations, including intramolecular cyclization, isomerization, and
 59    regioselective methylation (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Fig. 1)16,19. Although CYP80 enzymes have
 60   been shown to catalyze the  initial dimerization16–18, the enzymatic basis of macrocycle
 61    formation and subsequent stereochemical diversification remains elusive. In particular, the
 62   mechanisms governing configurational isomerization and methylation, the key drivers of
 63   bisBIA diversity-represent major gaps in the pathway. This contrasts sharply with other BIA
 64    classes, such as morphinan20, phthalideisoquinoline21,22, protoberberine23,24, and aporphine25,26,
 65    for which biosynthetic pathways have been elucidated and successfully reconstructed in
 66    microbial systems.

 67   BisBIAs occur predominantly in plants of the Menispermaceae, Berberidaceae, Lauraceae, and
 68   Ranunculaceae families3. The herbaceous perennial vine Stephania tetrandra (Menispermaceae)
 69    stands out as a representative species in  this group, synthesizing both (S,S)-configured
 70    tetrandrine  and  (R,S)-configured  bisBIAs  such  as  isotetrandrine  and  cepharanthine
 71    (Supplementary Fig. 2)27. However, access to these compounds at scale is constrained by slow-
 72   growing plant sources, e.g., Menispermaceae species require several years of cultivation before
 73    harvest27; and the difficulty of chemical synthesis28. These constraints have hindered systematic
 74    exploration of their therapeutic potential and strongly motivated the need for biosynthetic
 75    elucidation and heterologous production. In this work, we elucidate the biosynthetic pathway
 76    of bisBIAs  in  S.  tetrandra,  identifying CYP enzymes  responsible  for  regioselective
 77    dimerization and macrocyclization, and revealing a redox-mediated mechanism that controls
 78    stereochemical configuration of the macrocyclized products. We further achieve de novo
 79    biosynthesis of these complex alkaloids in a heterologous host, establishing a scalable platform
 80    for their production and diversification.

101   CYP80Q4 is an intermolecular C-O phenol-coupling enzyme for linear (R,S)-bisBIA
102   formation

103   CYP80  family  enzymes  have  been  reported  to  catalyze  phenol  coupling  of  the
104    benzylisoquinoline monomers (R)- and (S)-N-methylcoclaurine (5) to form (R,R)- and (R,S)-
105    configured linear bisBIAs (Supplementary Fig. 1)16. However, given the confirmed presence of
106   bisBIA structures lacking N-methyl groups in S. tetrandra such as 2′-N-norfangchinoline
107    (Supplementary Fig. 2)27, we hypothesized that the N-nonmethyl 1-benzylisoquinoline alkaloid
108    (1-BIA) monomers 3 and 4 may also serve as precursors towards linear bisBIA coupling in this
109    species. Since linear bisBIAs are biosynthesized by phenol coupling of two phenol monomers16,
110   we focused on genes encoding CYPs in the co-expression dataset. A total of 28 candidates (Fig.
111    1d) were first expressed in WAT11 yeast strain, and the microsomes were extracted for in vitro
112    assays33. Both the (R)- and (S)- configured 3, 4, and 5 were tested as potential substrates in the
113    assays. LC-MS analysis revealed that two of the 28 screened CYPs yielded detectable products.
114   These two enzymes share 99% amino acid sequence identity and are encoded by tandemly
115    duplicated loci St07G1598 and St07G1600, on chromosome 7 (Supplementary Fig. 5). The
116   enzyme encoded by St07G1598 was subsequently designated as CYP80Q4.

117   CYP80Q4 catalyzed the coupling of (R)-5/(S)-5 pairs to yield C3′-O-C4′ phenol-coupled
118    product (R,S)-berbamunine ((R,S)-6), as assigned by comparison with the product of the well-
119    characterized CYP80A1 from Berberis stolonifera16 (Fig. 2, Supplementary Figs. 6 and 7a-c).
120    This reaction also produced a minor amount of (R,R)-guattegaumerine ((R,R)-6), a product
121    resulting from the analogous coupling of two (R)-5 monomers (Supplementary Figs. 6 and 7a-
122     c)16. While (R)-5/(S)-5 has been the most extensively studied substrate pair in previous
123     reports16,18, our results reveal that (S)-4/(R)-4 also serves as a highly robust substrate pair for
124   CYP80Q4-mediated coupling (Fig. 2, Supplementary Fig. 6 and 7d-f). Upon utilizing (S)-4/(R)-

143   To compare the activity of CYP80Q4 against the previously reported bisBIA-dimerizing
144   enzymes, we synthesized BsCYP80A116, NnCYP80Q217, and Bs-MaCYP80A118 and assayed
145    these CYP80 homologs under identical conditions. Analysis of a comprehensive substrate panel
146    revealed that all enzymes accept a broader range of 1-BIA substrates than previously recognized
147    (Supplementary Fig.  6)16–18. CYP80Q4 predominantly generated (R,S)-configured dimers,
148   whereas the other CYP80s exhibited a marked preference for coupling two (R)-enantiomers to
149   form (R,R)-configured products (Supplementary Fig. 6 and 9). Notably, none of the tested
150   CYP80 enzymes catalyzed detectable (S,S)-configured bisBIAs. This observation aligns with
151    the presence of (R,S)-configured isotetrandrine and cepharanthine in S. tetrandra, yet it fails to
152    explain the accumulation of the more abundant (S,S)-configured tetrandrine.

153   CYP82BCs catalyze intramolecular phenol coupling to form cyclic bisBIA scaffolds

155    cepharanthine, necessitates the cyclization of linear bisBIA precursors, which likely occurs via
156   enzyme-mediated intramolecular phenol coupling (Supplementary Fig. 1)3. Based on the
157    structures of bisBIAs known to accumulate in S. tetrandra, we inferred that there are at least
158   two  types  of  intramolecular  coupling  reactions, C7-O-C8′  phenol  coupling  (towards
159    cepharanthine) and C7′-O-C8 phenol coupling (towards tetrandrine and isotetrandrine), for the

165    using microsomal cascade reactions (Fig. 1d). Microsomes expressing each candidate were co-
166    incubated with microsomes expressing CYP80Q4, which served to generate the requisite linear
167    substrates. The reactions were initiated by the addition of 1-BIA monomer pairs ((R)-4/(S)-4,

170    This screening identified four active enzymes belonging to the CYP82 family, designated as
171   CYP82BC1-4. In combination with CYP80Q4, these enzymes generated new products with a
172    characteristic mass shift of –2 Da relative to the linear intermediates, consistent with the loss
173    of two protons during intramolecular phenol coupling (Fig. 3, Supplementary Fig. 10).
174   CYP82BC1 and CYP82BC4, which share 92.3% (Supplementary Fig. 11a) amino acid
175    sequence identity and are encoded by tandemly duplicated loci St10G1685 and St10G1684 on
176   chromosome 10 (Supplementary  Fig.  5),  displayed  dual  regioselectivity  in  mediating
177    intramolecular phenol coupling. Large-scale purification and NMR analysis confirmed that
178    both enzymes catalyzed C7-O-C8′ coupling and C7′-O-C8 coupling of (R,S)-8 and (R,S)-7 to
179    yield cepharanthine-type scaffolds bisnoraromoline ((R,S)-16) and racemosinine A ((R,S)-18),
180    as well as isotetrandrine-type scaffolds bisnorobamegine ((R,S)-17) and 2′-norobamegine
181    ((R,S)-19) (Fig. 3, Supplementary Fig. 10a-h, NMR data for (R,S)-16, (R,S)-17, (R,S)-18, (R,S)-
182   19 in Supplementary Table 4–7). In contrast, CYP82BC2 and CYP82BC3, which share 80.6%
183    (Supplementary Fig. 11b) amino acid sequence identity and are encoded by tandem loci
184   St13G1440 and St13G1445 on chromosome 13 (Supplementary Fig.  5), showed strong
185    regioselectivity. These enzymes predominantly catalyzed C7′-O-C8-coupling, converting
186    (R,S)-8 and (R,S)-7 into the corresponding isotetrandrine-type macrocycles (R,S)-17 and (R,S)-
187    19, with only trace amounts of the C7-O-C8′ isomers detected (Fig. 3, Supplementary Fig. 10a-
188     h). Notably, the cyclization efficiency was substrate-dependent, with only trace amounts of the

194    specificity. No cyclized products were detected in these reactions, indicating that CYP82BC
195   enzymes are highly selective for (R,S)-configured linear bisBIA substrates.

201    Five methyltransferases catalyze heteroatom methylation in bisBIA biosynthesis

202   To identify the enzymes bridging the gap between the cyclized scaffolds and methyl-decorated
203    bisBIAs, we screened 30 candidate MTs from the co-expression dataset (Fig. 1d). The N-
204   nonmethyl monomers (R)-4 and (S)-4 were used to initiate the cascade containing CYP80Q4,
205   CYP82BC1 and each candidate MT. LC-MS analysis revealed that reactions containing five
206    candidates yielded new peaks exhibiting a +14 Da mass shift relative to the cyclic scaffolds
207    (R,S)-16, (R,S)-17, consistent with single methylation events (Fig. 3, Supplementary Fig. 12).
208   Based on chromatographic retention time shifts reflecting changes in polarity, these MTs were
209    provisionally classified as three N-methyltransferases (NMTs) and two O-methyltransferases
210   (OMTs).

211  We performed large-scale reactions and purified the resulting compounds for NMR analysis,
212    enabling   precise  assignment   of NMT   activities.  One  enzyme,   designated   as
213    bisbenzylisoquinoline 2-N-methyltransferase (B2NMT), catalyzed methylation  at the N2
214    position to yield (R,S)-18 and (R,S)-19, as confirmed by comparison with the cyclized products
215    generated from (R)-5 and  (S)-4  via  the CYP80Q4 and CYP82BC1  catalytic cascade.
216    (Supplementary Fig. 12a, b). Similarly, NMR characterization of (R,S)-35 and (R,S)-36 defined
217   two paralogous enzymes, B2′NMT1 and B2′NMT2 (71.6% shared amino acid sequence

249  A dual-enzyme redox system catalyzes stereochemical inversion of (R,S)- to (S,S)-bisBIAs

250   Although the (S,S)-configured tetrandrine  is the most abundant bisBIA in S. tetrandra,
251   CYP80Q4 does not generate (S,S)-configured linear bisBIAs, and CYP82BCs do not catalyze
252    their subsequent cyclization. This indicates that cyclic (S,S)-bisBIAs are likely formed through
253    the epimerization of cyclic (R,S)-bisBIAs. Mechanistically, such a stereochemical inversion
254    typically requires a two-step redox process involving an initial oxidative desaturation to a
255    planar intermediate, followed by a stereoselective reduction. A notable parallel exists in opium
256    poppy, where reticuline epimerase (REPI, a CYP-aldo-keto reductase fusion protein) drives a
257    redox-mediated configurational reset via a transient 1,2-dehydroreticuline intermediate37,38.
258   However, no REPI-like fusion protein homologs were identified in the S. tetrandra genome,
259   and assays of discrete CYP and aldo-keto reductase homologs using cyclic (R,S)-17 as a
260    substrate yielded no detectable products. This suggests that a different class of enzymes is
261    responsible for this conversion.

263   which are known to catalyze C-N bond desaturation in BIA biosynthesis24,39. This led to the
264    identification of four candidate enzymes (Fig. 1d), including St13G1442, which is genomically

276   To identify the reductase responsible for the stereospecific reduction of intermediate 24, we
277    screened 15 SDRs from the co-expression dataset (Fig. 1d). These candidates were tested in
278    coupled assays with BBOX using (R,S)-17 as substrate. One candidate, an atypical SDR
279    (encoded by St13G827), converted the planar imine intermediate 24 to a product with a +2 Da
280   mass shift (Fig. 4a, Supplementary Fig. 20). Although this product shared identical mass and
281   MS/MS fragmentation with (R,S)-17, it exhibited a distinct retention time (Supplementary Fig.
282    20). Electronic circular dichroism (ECD) spectroscopy confirmed this product as the (S,S)-
283    configured diastereomer, confirming inversion at the C1 stereocenter (Fig. 4b). We designated
284     this enzyme as bisbenzylisoquinoline reductase (BBR). Notably, BBR is also encoded on
285   chromosome 13, although outside the BBOX/CYP82BC cluster (Supplementary Fig.  5),
286    suggesting coordinated but spatially distributed genetic control.

287    In contrast to fused epimerization enzymes, this two-enzyme architecture creates a temporal
288   window in which the planar iminium intermediate (24) can be intercepted by tailoring MTs.
289    Indeed, incubation of the BBOX reaction mixture with B2′NMT2, B7OMT and B12OMT -
290    either individually or in combination - generated mono- and trimethylated oxidized products,
291    reflected by +14 Da and +42 Da mass shifts, respectively. MS/MS analysis confirmed that each
292  MT maintained its regioselectivity on the oxidized scaffold (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 21;
293    see Supplementary Information for  structural elucidation). Subsequent reduction of the
294    trimethylated intermediate (25) by BBR successfully yielded the fully elaborated (S,S)-
295    configured product (S,S)-26 (Fig. 4, Supplementary Fig. 22). This oxidation–methylation–
296    reduction sequence defines a redox-mediated “stereochemical editing” mechanism that couples
297    epimerization with late-stage methylation, thereby expanding the accessible chemical space of
298    (S,S)-configured bisBIAs.

299   De novo biosynthesis of canonical and novel bisBIAs in yeast reveals modular methylation
300    control

301   To evaluate whether the pathway logic elucidated in S. tetrandra could be transferred to a
302    heterologous host, we reconstituted the bisBIA pathway in Saccharomyces cerevisiae to
303    establish a microbial production platform. The pathway was assembled stepwise by introducing
304    discrete enzymatic modules for monomer supply, oxidative coupling, macrocyclization,
305    methylation, and stereochemical editing. As a starting chassis, we used strain XJ0636, which
306    produces over 300 mg/L (S)-3 in shake-flask cultures24. Genotypes of all constructed strains are
307   shown in Supplementary Table 10.

308   Chromosomal integration of 6OMT from P. somniferum (Ps6OMT) yielded strain XJ06360,
309   which produced (S)-4 (Fig. 5a, Supplementary Table 10)32. Given that bisBIA formation
310    requires both (R)- and (S)-configured monomers, we next introduced the DRS-DRR enzyme
311   module (PrDCS from P. rhoeas and PbDCR from P. bracteatum), previously shown to catalyze
312    the epimerization of (S)-5 to (R)-5 and (S)-reticuline to (R)-reticuline18. Since no homologs
313   have been  identified, whether the PrDCS-PbDCR module could  similarly mediate the
314    epimerization of N-nonmethyl 1-BIA monomers (S)-4 to (R)-4 within our engineered context
315   was thus explored. Integration of PrDCS and PbDCR, together with CYP80Q4 in XJ06360
316    yielded strain XJ06388 (XJ06360 + PrDCS + CYP80Q4 + PbDCR), which produced the linear
317   bisBIA (R,S)-8, indicating successful in vivo epimerization of (S)-4 to (R)-4 and confirming
318    that both enantiomers act as necessary substrates for CYP80Q4-mediated coupling (Fig. 5b,
319   Supplementary Fig. 23). Increasing the copy number of PrDCS, PbDCR, and CYP80Q4 further
320   improved production, yielding strain XJ06399 that efficiently produced the linear (R,S)-bisBIA
321    precursor (Fig. 5b).

322    Subsequent introduction of CYP82BC enzymes (CYP82BC1-4) together with four bisBIA
323   MTs (B2NMT, B2′NMT2, B7OMT and B12OMT) in strain XJ06399 enabled conversion of the
324    linear (R,S)-configured intermediate into cyclic bisBIAs, demonstrating that both oxidative
325    macrocyclization and downstream tailoring reactions are functional in yeast and preserve the
326    stereochemical constraints observed in vitro (Supplementary Fig. 24). Across the resulting
327     strains, bisBIA production was readily detected, including the fully methylated cyclic bisBIAs
328    (R,S)-22 and (R,S)-23. Among these strains, XJ06325 expressing CYP82BC3 in combination
329    with the four MTs yielded the highest accumulation (3.6 mg/L) of the fully methylated cyclic
330   bisBIA (R,S)-23 (Fig. 5c, Supplementary Fig. 24).

331   To investigate how methylation contributes to structural diversification, we systematically
332   examined combinations of MTs using strain XJ06322, which produces both (R,S)-16 and (R,S)-
333   17 (Supplementary Fig. 23). Expression of individual MTs revealed distinct regioselectivity:

341    a versatile platform for generating structurally and potentially functionally diverse bisBIA
342    derivatives (Supplementary Fig. 28).

343   To reconstitute stereochemical editing in yeast, we introduced the BBOX-BBR redox module
344    into strain XJ06019 (XJ06321 + B2′NMT2 + B7OMT + B12OMT), a cyclic (R,S)-bisBIA-
345    producing strain lacking B2NMT. Expression of BBOX alone (strain XJ06020, Supplementary
346    Table 10) produced an oxidized intermediate with m/z 607.0 [M + H]+, while co-expression of
347  BBOX and BBR  (strain XJ06021, Supplementary Table 10) yielded cyclic (S,S)-26, as
348    confirmed by MS/MS spectral matching with (R,S)-26 (Fig. 5d, Supplementary Fig. 29).

349    Collectively, these results establish a modular yeast platform for bisBIAs biosynthesis, enabling
350    production of both native and novel compounds with diverse methylation patterns and
351    stereochemistries. Starting from (S)-4, the engineered strains produced at least 21 cyclic (R,S)-
352    configured bisBIAs, with further diversification achievable via combinatorial methylation.

353   Conclusions

354    Here, using genome and transcriptome data from S. tetrandra, we identified and characterized
355    five CYPs, one BBE-like oxidase, one SDR, and five methyltransferases involved in bisBIA
356    biosynthesis, successfully elucidating the complete pathways to multiple (R,S)- and (S,S)-
357    configured bisBIAs. This establishes the biochemical logic underlying cyclic bisBIA formation,
358    revealing how post-cyclization redox reactions control stereochemistry within a dimeric
359    scaffold. By defining the mechanism that converts (R,S) to (S,S)-configured bisBIA and
360    demonstrating  its functional integration in a reconstructed pathway, we unify previously
361    discrete aspects of bisBIA biosynthesis - oxidative coupling, redox inversion, and methylation
362    diversification - into a single coherent framework. These findings expand the known strategies
363   by which plant alkaloid pathways generate molecular complexity and create new opportunities
364    to explore stereochemical control, enzymatic modularity, and metabolite diversification in
365    engineered systems.

366   The successful reconstitution of this pathway in a heterologous yeast host marks a transition
367   from strictly descriptive biochemistry to synthetic biology. By integrating the CYP82BCs, the
368    promiscuous methyltransferase network, and the BBOX-BBR editing module, we have a new

370   enzymes to generate not only the naturally occurring (S,S)- and (R,S)-bisBIAs but also a library

372    configurations. Given the potent antiviral and anti-inflammatory properties of tetrandrine and

374    harvesting slow-growing wild plants. Moreover, it provides immediate access to a diverse array
375    of "new-to-nature"  derivatives, serving as a  sustainable approach  for  structure-activity
376    relationship studies and the development of next-generation therapeutics.

502   Methods

503    Plant Materials

504    Cultivated Stephania tetrandra plants (1 year old) were collected from the traditional Chinese
505    medicinal herb cultivation base in Yichun City, Jiangxi Province, China. For genome
506    sequencing, fresh young leaves were collected for DNA extraction. For transcriptome profiling,
507    root cortex, root stele, stem, and leaf tissues were collected with three biological replicates per
508     tissue, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80 °C until RNA extraction. High quality
509  DNA and RNA samples were sent to Wuhan Benagen Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China)
510    for genome sequencing.

511   BIA standards

512    Authentic standards of (R)- and (S)-norcoclaurine (3),  (R)- and (S)-coclaurine (4), and (R)-
513   and (S)-N-methylcoclaurine (5) were obtained from WuXi AppTec. The (R,S)-, (S,R)-, (R,R)-,
514   and (S,S)-stereoisomers of lindoldhamine (8) were synthesized as detailed in the Supplementary
515    Information. All other authentic standards were purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-
516   Technology Co., Ltd.

517   Genome sequencing, assembly, and chromosome-scale scaffolding

518    Short-read libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq platform. After filtering, 68.93
519   Gb clean Illumina data were obtained, with a Q30 value of 93.49%. K-mer analysis estimated
520    a genome size of 927.61 Mb. Long-read sequencing was performed on an Oxford Nanopore
521   PromethION platform. A total of 65.12 Gb filtered Nanopore reads were generated, about 70x
522    coverage based on the estimated genome size. The final genome assembly was 924.44 Mb in
523   923 contigs (GC 37.25%), with a contig N50 of 2.07 Mb and a maximum contig length of 8.74
524   Mb. Hi-C scaffolding (ALLHiC)41 grouped sequences into 13 pseudochromosomes (chr01-
525    chr13), with an additional unanchored set (chrUnK). Assembly accuracy was supported by an
526    Illumina read mapping rate of 99.82%, with an average depth of 73.97x and genome coverage
527    of 92.38%. To improve transcript evidence for genome annotation and downstream expression
528    analyses, PacBio Iso-Seq full-length transcriptome sequencing was performed on a SMRTbell
529    library sequenced using the PacBio Sequel platform42. Protein-coding genes were predicted
530    using an evidence-guided approach (BRAKER integrating GeneMark-ET43 and AUGUSTUS44)
531    trained with transcriptome evidence, yielding 22,226 predicted protein-coding genes. Illumina
532   RNA-seq reads from each sample were aligned to the reference genome using STAR45. Gene-
533    level read counts were obtained with HTSeq46, and expression levels were estimated using
534   RSEM. Expression values were normalized (e.g., FPKM/TPM as appropriate) for downstream
535    analyses. Differential expression analysis across tissues was performed using DESeq247.

536   Gene selection

537    Co-expression analysis of the entire S. tetrandra gene set was performed using the scipy library
538    (v1.5.3) in Python. Genes exhibiting a Pearson correlation coefficient (r) > 0.7 with the bait
539   gene St6OMT1 (locus St09G337) were defined as the primary co-expression pool. This dataset
540   was subsequently filtered based on functional annotations to retain candidates annotated as
541   "cytochrome  P450",  "methyltransferase",  "berberine  bridge  enzyme",  or  "short-chain
542    dehydrogenase/reductase". Candidates were further curated by excluding sequences with
543    truncated or aberrant open reading frames (ORFs) to generate the final primary candidate list.
544   Upon functional validation of hits from this primary screen, an iterative homology-based
545   mining strategy was employed: validated enzymes served as queries for local BLAST searches
546    to retrieve paralogs sharing >70% amino acid sequence identity, which constituted a secondary
547    candidate pool for subsequent functional characterization.

582    Yeast microsomes were prepared according to established protocols48, which have been
583    successfully employed for the characterization of plant CYPs49–51. Following induction, yeast
584    cells were harvested by centrifugation (6,000 × g, 5 min) and conditioned by resuspension in
585   TEK buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, 1 mM EDTA, 100 mM KCl, pH 7.5; 0.5 g wet cells/mL) for 5

587  mM Tris-HCl, 1 mM EDTA, 600 mM sorbitol, pH 7.5). Cell lysis was achieved using a high-
588    pressure homogenizer (10 cycles at 1,000 psi, 2-4 °C). The lysate was centrifuged at 20,000 ×
589   g for 20 min at 4 °C to remove cell debris. Microsomes were precipitated from the supernatant
590   by the addition of NaCl (150 mM final) and PEG-4000 (0.1 g/mL final). After incubation on
591    ice for 1 h with periodic mixing, microsomal fractions were pelleted (20,000 × g, 20 min, 4 °C)
592   and resuspended in 1 mL TEG buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, 1 mM EDTA, 20% glycerol, pH 7.5).

594    Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 0.5 mg microsomal protein, 0.2 mM substrate, 500 μM NADPH, and an

596    dehydrogenase, 5 μM FAD, 5 μM FMN). Reactions were incubated at 30 °C for 3 h with
597    shaking (180 rpm). To quench the reaction and facilitate alkaloid extraction, the pH was
598    adjusted to ~10 by the addition of 10 μL ammonium hydroxide, followed by extraction with
599   500 μL ethyl acetate. For coupled assays involving multiple enzymes, the total reaction volume
600   was maintained constant using Tris-HCl buffer. The organic phase was evaporated to dryness,
601    reconstituted in 150 μL methanol, and centrifuged (20,000 × g, 15 min) prior to LC-MS analysis.

667   Computational electron circular dichroism analysis

668    Conformational analyses were  carried out via Monte Carlo searching using molecular
669   mechanics with MMFF force field in the Spartan′18 program. The obtained conformers were
670    reoptimized using DFT at the B3LYP/6-31G(d) level in gas phase using the Gaussian 09
671    program, and selected relative Gibbs free energies in the range of 0-2 kcal/mol were chosen for
672    subsequent calculations. The B3LYP/6-31G(d) harmonic vibrational frequencies were further
673    calculated to conﬁrm their stability. The energies, oscillator strengths, and rotational strengths
674    of the first 60 electronic excitations were calculated using the TDDFT methodology at the
675   M062X/TZVP level. The ECD spectra were simulated by the overlapping Gaussian function53.
