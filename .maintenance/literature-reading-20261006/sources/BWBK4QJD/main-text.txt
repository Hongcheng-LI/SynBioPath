Supporting Information
ABSTRACT: The marine sponge-derived fungus Stachylidium
bicolor 293 K04 is a prolific producer of specialized metabolites,
including certain cyclic tetrapeptides called endolides, which are
characterized by the presence of the unusual amino acid N-methyl-
3-(3-furyl)-alanine. This rare feature can be used as bait to detect
new endolide-like analogs through customized fragment pattern
searches of tandem mass spectrometry data using the Mass Spec
Query Language (MassQL). Here, we integrate endolide-specific
MassQL queries with molecular networking to obtain substructural
information guiding the targeted isolation and structure elucidation
of the new proline-containing endolides E (1) and F (2). We
showed that endolide F (but not E) is a moderate antagonist of the
arginine vasopressin V1A receptor, a member of the G protein-
coupled receptor superfamily.
M
arine fungi are a rich but still understudied source of
specialized metabolites (SMs, traditionally referred to as
“secondary metabolites” or “natural products”) with distinct
structures and bioactivities,1 including antibacterial,2 antiviral,3
and antineoplastic4 agents, among others.
One such marine sponge-derived-fungus is Stachylidium
bicolor 293 K04, which harbors a remarkably diverse
metabolome that has yielded SMs from different chemical
classes. These include the cyclic tetrapeptide endolides,5 the
phthalide-derived marilones,6 the phthalimidine-derived mari-
lines,7 the tyrosine-derived stachylines,8 thiodiketopipera-
zines,9 and several volatile compounds.10
The increasing global availability of liquid chromatography
tandem mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS/MS) instruments
coupled with the widespread adoption of open science
initiatives has enabled the development of community-driven
and publicly accessible repositories for LC-MS/MS data such
as GNPS-MassIVE11 and MassBank.12 As manual analysis of
the overwhelming amount of LC-MS/MS data derived even
from a single microorganism extract is simply not feasible, new
computational strategies are being developed to overcome
common bottlenecks such as prioritization and dereplication,
i.e., recognizing and eliminating the active substances already
studied in the early stage of the screening process that hinder
the discovery of new specialized metabolites.13−20 The GNPS
framework13 allows for fast and scalable dereplication of
known compounds and detection of putatively new analogs by
clustering features with similar MS/MS spectra together in
molecular networks.11 In this way, it is possible to dereplicate
the annotated substances (annotation occurs against publicly
available or in-house MS/MS spectral libraries) within the
molecular clusters and obtain information about the remaining
unannotated metabolites that have been grouped in the
respective cluster by manual analysis21,22 or in-silico annotation
tools.23−26 An alternative strategy toward new SMs is to
investigate molecular clusters for which none of the nodes have
been annotated yet.27−29
The recently developed Mass Spec Query Language
(MassQL) enables LC-MS/MS data sets to be searched for
specific user-defined features, leveraging all the associated data
acquisition parameters and variables, including MS1/MS2
precursor m/z, MS/MS fragments, retention time, intensity,
and mass tolerance.15 MassQL is an SQL (Structured Query
Language) inspired tool explicitly designed for noncomputer
scientists to write their own queries in a flexible, precise, and
scalable fashion.
In this study we developed an integrative approach in which
MassQL queries are used to add substructural information to
all nodes of a molecular cluster, allowing for the targeted
isolation and structure elucidation of new endolides E (1) and

■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
To dereplicate known and annotate putatively new endolide
analogs from an ethyl acetate extract of S. bicolor, the workflow
shown in Scheme 1 was developed and applied. First, an
OSMAC (one strain many compounds)30 approach was used
to determine cultivation conditions for production of diverse
endolides. Extracts were analyzed using molecular networking.
Endolide yields and their structural diversity peaked after 30-
day cultivation on rice medium (Figures S1, S2).
The common feature for all endolides is the rare amino acid
N-methyl-3-(3-furyl)-alanine (NMefAla), which can be used as
a metabolomics hook to find known as well as putatively new
endolide analogs. Iminium ions occurring in the low-m/z
region of MS/MS spectra have been demonstrated to be useful
for fishing peptides from metabolomics data.15,31 To make the
query specific for endolides while also obtaining substructural
information to permit annotation of putative amino acid
building blocks for all nodes of the endolide molecular family,
we thought of a two-step query. First, we designed a MassQL
command to search the metabolome of the fungus S. bicolor for
NMefAla-containing peptides. The query command was
designed to search for signals within the MS/MS spectra
with the exact mass of the corresponding iminium ion with an
m/z tolerance of 0.001 and a minimum intensity of 5%
compared to the base peak in high-resolution MS/MS data.
We included both methylated and unmethylated iminium ions,
as demethylation can also occur via in-source fragmentation.
Second, as the endolides contain multiple amino acids, we
expected multiple iminium ion fragments in the MS/MS
pattern. Thus, we hyphenated the aforementioned query with a
second query command searching for other, unspecified
iminium ions from all possible methylated and unmethylated
iminium adducts of proteinogenic amino acids and NMefAla/
fAla (to account for endolides with multiple units of this amino
acid, Figure 1A).
Given by the “CARDINALITY”-function, at least two MS/
MS fragments of the listed iminium ions must be present in the
second part of the query to fulfill the entire query. This was
added to improve the specificity of the results, because
compounds consisting of MS/MS fragments with the same
exact m/z as described in the first part of the query (m/z
124.0757, C7H10NO+ or m/z 110.0600, C6H8NO+, respec-
tively), but happen to be constitutional isomers of NMefAla/
fAla of nonpeptidic origin will be discarded unless they contain
at least another false positive fragment. Furthermore, the high
upper range CARDINALITY value allows for the detection of
putatively bigger NMefAla/fAla-containing peptides.
To extend the node annotation and thus obtain more
information about the amino acid composition, further specific
queries were created. For this purpose, the first part of the
query was adapted to each proteinogenic amino acid. The
exact masses of the methylated and unmethylated iminium
ions used for the queries are given in Table S1.
For visualization of the query, an example for the known
endolide B as well as the results for amino acids with
annotations in the endolide cluster are shown in Figure 1B,C.
After analyzing the query results for the respective amino
acids, we noticed that there are only five amino acids with a
significant number of hits (Table S2). As expected, most of the
annotated amino acids were 3-(3-furyl)-alanine, leucine/
isoleucine/valine, and phenylalanine, which occur in previously
described endolides, from S. bicolor.5,9 Surprisingly, a
significant number of annotations were also obtained for the
iminium ion of proline. Proline-containing endolides have not
been reported or predicted before.
In order to visualize the MassQL-predicted amino acid
composition within the endolide molecular family, the
MassQL query results were integrated as a feature table into
the molecular network (Figure 2). To ensure that the query
results are correctly assigned to each feature, the ion identity
molecular network file was inspected for the respective query
commands. Each feature has a specific scan number which can
be used as a link between the ion identity molecular network
and the MassQL results. Additionally, filters were applied to

for other proline-containing natural products).18,33 Altogether,
the CANOPUS and SIRIUS results supported the MassQL-
annotated molecular networking predicted amino acid
composition NMefAla, Pro, Leu, and Val for m/z 461.2754.

The multiplicity-edited
1H−13C HSQC data provided
evidence for the presence of five CH2 groups in the structure
of compound 1. In addition, five non-hydrogenated carbons,
nine CH, and five CH3 groups were identified based on the 13C
and 1H−13C HSQC data as well as the integrals of the 1H
NMR data. The occurrence of four amide carbonyl (δC 173.0,
172.4, 172.2, 169.7) and four alpha-CH groups (δH/δC 4.74/
50.0, 4.49/61.6, 4.47/54.8, 4.43/61.8) suggested a tetrapepti-
dic structure of 1. The individual component amino acids and
their sequence was confirmed based on the interpretation of
2D NMR data.
1H−1H COSY, 1H−13C HMBC, and 1H−1H NOESY data
allow clear assignment of the carbons and hydrogens in
NMefAla. HMBC correlations from aromatic hydrogens H-5,
H-6, and H-7 to C-4 and from H-5, H-6, H-3a, and H-3b to C-
7 together with the COSY correlation between H-5 and H-6
establish the furan moiety. The HMBC correlations from H-3a
and H-3b to C-4 and the COSY coupling from H-3a and H-3b
to the alpha H-2 allow for the assignment of 3-(3-furyl)-
alanine. H-2 in turn shows an HMBC correlation to C-8 of the
N-methyl group, allowing for the assignment of NMefAla.
The two methyl groups H3-12 and H3-13 exhibited COSY
correlations to H-11, suggesting a valine moiety. Furthermore,
the HMBC spectrum showed correlations from H-10 to C-11,
C-12, and C-13 and from H-10 to C-9.
The third residue was designated as proline, based on the
continuous COSY spin system beginning with the alpha H-15
and continuing through H-16a/16b, H-17a/17b, and H-18a/
18b. HMBC correlations were observed from H-15, H-16b,
and H-17a to C-14 as well as from H-15 and H-16a to C-18.
Interestingly, the large chemical shift difference between C-16
and C-17 (Δ = 9.8 ppm)34 implies a cis Leu-Pro amide bond,
which is a known feature for proline residues in cyclic
peptides.35 The cis amide bond is further supported by NOEs
between H-15 (α-Pro) and H-20 (α-Leu). An NOE between
H-18 (δ-Pro) and H-20 (α-Leu), which would be suggestive of
a trans amide bond, is absent. The last subunit was found to be
leucine on the basis of COSY couplings between the two
methyl groups H3-23 and H3-24 and H-22, suggesting an
isopropyl unit, and from H-21 to H-20 to NH-(2).
The amino acid sequence could be deduced from the
HMBC and NOESY data. The HMBC spectrum showed a
correlation from the NH-(2) of leucine to the carbonyl carbon
C-1 of NMefAla, and this linkage was further supported by an
NOE between NH-(2) of leucine and H-3 of NMefAla. Next, a
NOESY correlation connected the alpha H-20 of leucine to the
alpha H-15 of proline. In contrast to all other previously
reported endolides, which show a strictly alternating N-
methylation pattern, there is only one site of N-methylation in
1. The H3-8 of NMefAla couple to the carbonyl carbon C-9 of
valine in the HMBC spectrum. It follows that N-methyl-3-(3-
furyl)-alanine is flanked by valine and leucine. Proline must be
on the opposite site of N-methyl-3-(3-furyl)-alanine in the
cyclic tetrapeptide.
The structure of the proline-containing endolide F (2) was
also assigned based on LC-MS/MS (m/z 485.2389, [M + H]+,
C25H33O6N4
+, Figures S13, S14) and analysis of 1D and 2D
NMR data. The amino acid composition of this cyclic
tetrapeptide was assigned by comparing MS/MS data (Figure
S15) and the 1H NMR data of 2 with 1 (Figures S16, S17).
The signals for leucine were absent from the 1H NMR data
of 2. However, the analogous signals for proline, valine, and N-
methyl-3-(3-furyl)-alanine were present. In addition, a closer
look at the integrals of the aromatic hydrogen atoms of the
furan moiety showed that they are twice as large in 2 as in 1
(Figure S17). Together with the Δm/z of 23.9637, these data
suggested that 2 has a second, but unmethylated 3-(3-furyl)-
alanine moiety in place of the leucine in 1. The substitution of
leucine by 3-(3-furyl)-alanine and the resulting planar structure
of compound 2 were confirmed by analysis of 13C and 2D
NMR experiments (Figures 3, S16−S21). Similarly, as in 1, the
fAla-Pro amide bond possesses a cis configuration in 2 as
judged by the ΔCβCγ = 9.7 ppm chemical shift.

Based on the MassQL-annotated molecular network and
subsequent manual inspection of the MS/MS data, the amino
acid composition of additional cyclic tetrapeptides could be
annotated with high confidence. These included further
proline-containing analogs, the known endolides C and D,9
and other sequences of the same four amino acids (Figure
S27).
The scalability of MassQL queries makes them useful for
other purposes. S. bicolor harbors a bacterial endosymbiont,
Burkholderia contaminans, and there is evidence that the
endolides are produced by the bacterium and not the fungus.36
At present, only two other NMefAla-containing peptides,
bingchamide B and rhizonin A, are known,37,38 both
originating from bacteria. Only recently did Ehinger et al.
prove the production of furylalanine moieties in rhizonin A by
the endofungal symbiont Mycetohabitans endofungorum.39
Thus, it is likely that the endolides are also of bacterial origin,
but to date we have not been able to cultivate the
endosymbiont in axenic cultures (a commonly observed
phenomenon).40 To add further evidence for the bacterial
origin of the endolides, an extended repository-scale MassQL
search of all data sets deposited on MASSIVE from the same
mass analyzer (Q-Exactive Orbitrap) was carried out by
targeting the accurate masses for the iminium ion as well as the
neutral loss of NMefAla. Additionally, we also applied a
taxonomically informed mass spectrometry search tool for
microbial metabolomics data (microbeMASST41) with several
different endolides to find putatively cultivable endolide
producers that might allow for more detailed biosynthetic
studies of the endolides in the future. Unfortunately, both
attempts to detect other producer organisms of NMefAla-
containing natural products in the repositories were
unsuccessful and thus highlight the rarity of the NMefAla
building block.
Apart from the structure determination and the possible
bacterial origin of endolides, the question of their biological
activity arose. The already published endolides A and B are
known to bind to the vasopressin receptor 1A (V1A) and the
serotonin receptor 2B (5-HT2B),5 respectively, with low
micromolar affinities. However, only weak (5-HT2B) or no
(V1A) antagonistic activity could be determined for synthetic
endolides A and B in a cellular assay.42 To investigate whether
small structural modifications such as different amino acids and
amino acid sequence might translate to a functional effect, the
newly isolated endolides E (1) and F (2) were tested for V1A
antagonistic activity. While pretesting (data not shown)
confirmed that endolide A was inactive as well as 1, our
results suggested that 2 might indeed impact V1A receptor
signaling induced by the endogenous agonist arginine
vasopressin (AVP), causing us to focus on this ligand−
receptor system.
We employed label-free whole cell biosensing based on
dynamic mass redistribution (DMR) as functional readout for
V1A receptor activation. DMR technology provides phenotypic
measures of cellular activity in real time when cells are exposed
to pharmacologically active stimuli.43−45 In our experimental
DMR paradigm, AVP evoked concentration-dependent cellular
activity in naïve HEK293 cells that was significantly enhanced
when cells were transfected to transiently overexpress V1AR
(Figure 5A,B). In contrast, 2 on its own did not promote
activation of either endogenous or exogenous V1AR signaling
(Figure 5C,D).
We therefore investigated a possible inhibitory effect of 2 on
AVP-induced cell activation. Indeed, when cells were pre-
treated with various concentrations of 2, V1AR activation
promoted with AVP at its EC80 (10 nM) was significantly
diminished (Figure 5E,F). In contrast, carbachol (CCh)-
promoted cell activation (EC80 = 31.6 μM) via the
endogenously expressed muscarinic M3 receptor was not
affected (Figure 5E,F). Inhibition of AVP- but not of CCh-
mediated signaling in the two-digit micromolar concentration
range by 2 (∼22% at 31.6 μM) is consistent with bioactivity of
this natural product on V1AR and also with the low micromolar
affinity observed for endolide A on this receptor in an earlier
study.5,42
Combining molecular networking with substructural analysis
offers a promising strategy to subclassify and identify members
of a molecular family of interest, e.g., cyclic plant peptides.31
Recently, building blocks-based molecular networking
(BBMN)46 was developed to annotate specific substructures
in the Securinega alkaloid family to provide additional insights
for their biosynthesis as well as exploration of their chemical
space. BBMN even led to the unprecedented discovery of
alkaloids containing novel backbone scaffolds (8/5/6/5/6/6/
6/6-fused octacyclic scaffold with a unique cage-shaped 3-
azatricyclo[6.4.0.0]dodecane core),3,11 highlighting the useful-
ness of integrating molecular networking with annotation
strategies on a substructural (building block) level. MassQL-
annotated molecular networking goes one step further by
adding reliable substructural information to all previously
unannotated nodes in a molecular networking cluster of the
targeted endolide molecular family (Figure S27). It is possible
to identify putatively new compounds based on specific
structural features using simple search commands. The
application of MassQL thus enables rapid decision making to
prioritize candidates for subsequent isolation and hints toward
A-domain specificities of targeted nonribosomal peptides. In
addition, the m/z data of additional iminium ion fragments in
the MS/MS data can be used to facilitate and speed up the
structure elucidation process by assigning the available
iminium ion fragments to known (non-)proteinogenic amino
acids. However, this annotation remains only an aid to
structure elucidation, and the method has some other MS-
intrinsic limitations. (i) One limitation is the requirement of an
MS/MS capable high-resolution mass spectrometer with a low-
m/z cutoff in MS/MS spectral data to also detect iminium ions
from small amino acids such as glycine or alanine. (ii) A
second limitation is the occurrence of false positives in
metabolomics analysis due to analytes having the exact same
fragment masses as the respective amino acid iminium ions.
However, by combining different specific queries this effect can
be mitigated. Another limitation is the occurrence of false
positives due to selection of the query parameters; therefore,
the query parameters should be adapted to the quality of the
available data. (iii) A third limitation is the redundancy of
features due to in-source fragments and adducts. The redundant
adducts and in-source fragments such as water loss can be
removed by integrating filtering queries in future studies. (iv)
A fourth limitation is that MS2 signal quantification is not
possible: if there is a query hit for a specific iminium adduct, it
is not possible to determine how often the corresponding

■CONCLUSIONS
In conclusion, MassQL is a versatile tool for generating and
answering research questions from MS data, both broad and
specific, in different areas of natural products research,15 and
can be used to add useful substructural information to
molecular networks. Here we reported the guided isolation,
structure elucidation, and bioactivity evaluation of two new
proline-containing compounds, 1 and 2, by MassQL-annotated
molecular networking, as well as the putative annotation of the
amino acid constitution of seven additional endolides (Figure
S27). Future integration with other computational tools such
as MolNetEnhancer,23 NAP,24 or MolDiscovery25 can be
envisioned to guide the discovery of new natural products and
increase confidence in MS-based annotations of known and
new bioactive SMs.

Classic Molecular Networking. The molecular networks were
created using the online workflow on the GNPS Web site (http://
gnps.ucsd.edu). The data were filtered by removing all MS/MS
fragment ions within ±17 Da of the precursor m/z. MS/MS spectra
were window filtered by choosing only the top six fragment ions in the
±50 Da window throughout the spectrum. The precursor ion mass
tolerance was set to 0.02 Da with an MS/MS fragment ion tolerance
of 0.02 Da. A network was then created in which edges were filtered
to have a cosine score above 0.70 and more than six matched peaks.
Edges between two nodes were kept in the network if and only if each
of the nodes appeared in each other’s respective top 10 most similar
nodes. Finally, the maximum size of a molecular family was set to 100,
and the lowest scoring edges were removed from molecular families
until the molecular family size was below this threshold. The spectra
in the network were then searched against the GNPS spectral
libraries.12,13 The library spectra were filtered in the same manner as
the input data. All matches kept between network spectra and library
spectra were required to have a cosine score above 0.8 and at least six
matched peaks.
Feature-Based Molecular Networking. LC-MS/MS data was
preprocessed using MZMine348 employing the mass detection ADAP
chromatogram builder, local minimum feature resolver, 13C isotope
filter, isotopic peaks finder, join aligner, feature list rows filter, peak
finder, duplicate peak filter, meta-correlate, and ion identity molecular
networking (IIMN) capabilities. The processed data were uploaded to
GNPS using the IIMN workflow. The data were filtered by removing
all MS/MS fragment ions within ±17 Da of the precursor m/z. MS/
MS spectra were window filtered by choosing only the top six
fragment ions in the ±50 Da window throughout the spectrum. The
precursor ion mass tolerance was set to 0.02 Da, and the MS/MS
fragment ion tolerance to 0.02 Da. A molecular network was then
created in which edges were filtered to have a cosine score above 0.7
and more than six matched peaks. Further, edges between two nodes
were kept in the network if and only if each of the nodes appeared in
each other’s respective top 20 most similar nodes. Finally, the
maximum size of a molecular family was set to 400, and the lowest
scoring edges were removed from molecular families until the
molecular family size was below this threshold. The spectra in the
network were then searched against GNPS spectral libraries.12,13 The
library spectra were filtered in the same manner as the input data. All
matches kept between network spectra and library spectra were
required to have a cosine score above 0.7 and at least six matched
peaks.

Cell Culture. Native human embryonic kidney (HEK)-293 cells
were cultivated in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM)
with 5% CO2 at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere. The medium was
supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal calf serum, penicillin (100 U/ml),
and streptomycin (0.1 mg/mL). Cells were transfected 24 h before
the experiments using polyethylenimine (PEI, 1 mg/mL). Trans-
fection was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions
(DNA to PEI solution ratio 1:3). Approximately 2.8 × 106 cells were
seeded into 10 cm dishes and immediately transfected with 5 μg of
total cDNA per condition (with and without 3 μg of V1A receptor,
filled up to 5 μg with empty pcDNA3.1(+) vector).
Label-Free DMR Assays. DMR measurements were performed as
previously described43−45 using the Epic biosensor (Corning)
together with the CyBi-SELMA semiautomated electronic pipetting
system (Analytik Jena AG). In brief, 24 h after transfection, HEK293
cells were washed with PBS, detached with trypsin, taken up in
DMEM, counted, and seeded at a density of 18,000 cells per well on
384-well fibronectin-coated biosensor plates (Corning) and were then
cultivated overnight. After 22 to 24 h, cells were washed three times
with assay buffer (Hank’s buffered salt solution (HBSS) containing 20
mM Hepes and 0.25% DMSO). Cells were incubated in 20 μL of
assay buffer for 1 h at 37 °C in the Epic reader to equilibrate.
Endolides and the reference agonist were diluted in assay buffer (final
concentration of 0.25% DMSO for every condition). After
equilibration, the sensor plate was scanned to record a baseline
optical signature for 5 min, followed by buffer or endolide addition
(10 μL of 3× concentrated dilution). As a second addition, either
buffer or agonist dilutions were added after 16 min (10 μL of 4×
concentrated dilution). Data points were generated from an average of
five measurements per point. Single DMR traces are shown as buffer-
corrected signal (Δpm) by subtracting the mean of the buffer values
corresponding to each data point. Data were analyzed using
GraphPad Prism 10 software.
Computational Details. All quantum chemical calculations were
performed using the Turbomole software package (version 7.7).50 An
initial conformer search for the possible configurations was conducted
by employing the default iMTD-GC algorithm as implemented in the
CREST program.51 The resulting conformer candidate structures
(149 for R-configured NMefAla, 163 for S-configured NMefAla) were
energy optimized at BP86/def2-SVP level of theory with the D3
dispersion correction with damping (BJ) and the resolution of
identity method for the Coulomb part (RI-J) enabled on the m3
integration grid for an initial screening. The structures of the
energetically lowest lying conformers were further refined by
reoptimization at the M06-2X/def2-TZVP level of theory on the
m4 integration grid. The Boltzmann average weight of the different
conformers was calculated on the basis of their electronic energies at
the temperature T = 298.15 K. For the conformers with significant
weight (>0.8%), specific optical rotation at a wavelength of λ = 589.3
nm was calculated at the B3LYP/aug-cc-pVTZ level of theory. NMR
chemical shieldings were also calculated for the candidate conformers
and trimethylsilane at the PBE0/pcSseg-2 level of theory. To account
for effects due to the solvent acetone used in the experiments, its
behavior has been mimicked by employing the COSMO solvation
model with the corresponding relative dielectric constant (εr =
1.3592) in the above calculations.52
