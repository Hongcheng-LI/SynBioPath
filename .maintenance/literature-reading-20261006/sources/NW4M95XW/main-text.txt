A B S T R A C T   
Trans-aconitic acid (TAA) is a promising bio-based chemical with the structure of unsaturated tricarboxylic acid, 
and also has the potential to be a non-toxic nematicide as a potent inhibitor of aconitase. However, TAA has not 
been commercialized because the traditional production processes of plant extraction and chemical synthesis 
cannot achieve large-scale production at a low cost. The availability of TAA is a serious obstacle to its widespread 
application. In this study, we developed an efficient microbial synthesis and fermentation production process for 
TAA. An engineered Aspergillus terreus strain producing cis-aconitic acid and TAA was constructed by blocking 
itaconic acid biosynthesis in the industrial itaconic acid-producing strain. Through heterologous expression of 
exogenous aconitate isomerase, we further designed a more efficient cell factory to specifically produce TAA. 
Subsequently, the fermentation process was developed and scaled up step-by-step, achieving a TAA titer of 60 g 
L−1 at the demonstration scale of a 20 m3 fermenter. Finally, the field evaluation of the produced TAA for control 
of the root-knot nematodes was performed in a field trial, effectively reducing the damage of the root-knot 
nematode. Our work provides a commercially viable solution for the green manufacturing of TAA, which will 
significantly facilitate biopesticide development and promote its widespread application as a bio-based chemical.   
1. Introduction 
Aconitic acid (propene-1,2,3-tricarboxylic acid) is an unsaturated 
tricarboxylic acid present in plants (Burau and Stout, 1965) such as 
sugarcane and maize (Brauer and Teel, 1981; Montoya et al., 2014). 
Therefore, it is safe for consumption and has been approved as a food 
additive by the World Health Organization (WHO) and the United States 
Food and Drug Administration (US FDA). Moreover, the unsaturated 
double bond and abundant hydroxyl groups make aconitic acid a 
promising monomer chemical for polymeric materials (Michailidou 
et al., 2019; Montoya et al., 2021). The United States Department of 
Energy (US DOE) has selected it as one of the top 30 potential building 
blocks. However, it was excluded from the final list of the top 12 
promising bio-based chemicals because it has not been commercially 
produced (Werpy and Petersen, 2004). 
There are two isomers of aconitic acid, cis-aconitic acid (CAA, CAS: 
585-84-2) and trans-aconitic acid (TAA, CAS: 4023-65-8). CAA is the 
reaction intermediate of aconitase, one of the key enzymes in the 
tricarboxylic acid cycle. As a stereoisomer of CAA, TAA can act as a 
competitive inhibitor that interferes with aconitase and exhibits some

(Chandler et al., 2011). 
Traditionally, aconitic acid is mainly extracted from plants, such as 
sugarcane juice (Miller and Cantor, 1951; Russell and Mayland, 1987). 
Subsequently, a chemical synthesis method through dehydration of 
citric acid by concentrated sulfuric acid was developed (Umbdenstock 
and Bruins, 1945; Verduyckt and De Vos, 2017); however, this method 
suffers from serious adverse reactions under high-temperature condi­
tions (Wyrzykowski et al., 2011). It is industrially produced from 
propane-1,1,2,3-tetracarboxylic compound through saponification, 
dehydrochlorination, and acidification, but it is still limited by the for­
mation of unwanted by products of lactones (Cao et al., 2011). Notably, 
none of the above technologies have been successfully commercialized 
on a large scale because of the complex and inefficient process, low 
conversion rate, and unavoidable by-products. To date, only small 
quantities of aconitic acid products are available as expensive chemical 
reagents, severely limiting the development of trans-aconitic acid

Synthetic biotechnology can be a promising alternative method for 
TAA production because it has been successfully applied to the efficient 
production of various organic acids (Yin et al., 2015). The production of 
aconitic acid in Escherichia coli was significantly improved by simulta­
neously reducing the activities of pyruvate kinase (PK) and isocitrate 
dehydrogenase (IDH). However, the final titer of 0.62 g L−1 was still too 
low for commercial production (Li et al., 2020). This inefficiency may 
result from CAA specificity in the TCA cycle and the fact that CAA is the 
intermediate during the conversion of citric acid to isocitric acid cata­
lyzed by aconitase (Fig. 1A) (Chroumpi et al., 2020). Typically, CAA is 
not released from aconitase, and the proportion of citrate: CAA: iso­
citrate is about 90:4:6 in the reaction system at equilibrium (Lowenstein, 
1969). As a result, microorganisms rarely accumulate aconitic acid, with 
the possible exception of strains that produce itaconic acid (IA) (Pom­
raning et al., 2022; Steiger et al., 2019). Itaconic acid is an important 
biobased building block on the top 12 list proposed by US DOE (Medway 
and Sperry, 2014). Aconitic acid is the key precursor for itaconic acid 
biosynthesis (Chroumpi et al., 2020). In Aspergillus terreus, itaconic acid 
is produced from cis-aconitic acid via the decarboxylation catalyzed by 
cis-aconitate decarboxylase (CadA) (Deng et al., 2020; Kanamasa et al., 
2008; Tevz et al., 2010; van der Straat et al., 2014). In Ustilago maydis, 
itaconic acid is produced from trans-aconitic acid via the decarboxyl­
ation catalyzed by trans-aconitate decarboxylase (Tad1) (Geiser et al., 
2016). At present, itaconic acid was commercially produced through the 
submerged fermentation of A. terreus (Okabe et al., 2009). It suggests 
that a large amount of carbon sources goes to cis-aconitic acid in 
A. terreus rather than being trapped as citric acid in Aspergillus niger, the 
industrial species producing citric acid (Karaffa and Kubicek, 2019; 
Steiger et al., 2019). 
In this study, a commercially valuable cell factory of TAA was con­
structed using the industrial itaconic acid-producing A. terreus strain as a 
chassis cell. The fermentation process was developed and tested at the 
demonstration scale. Furthermore, the nematocidal activity of micro­
bially produced TAA was demonstrated by a cucumber field trial. 
2. Material and methods 
2.1. Strains and medium 
All strains used or constructed in this work are listed in Table 1. The 
A. terreus CICC40205 is an industrial strain of itaconic acid. Strain At- 
ΔpyrG is the ku80−/pyrG−double mutant of A. terreus CICC40205, an 
uracil auxotrophic mutant that grows on Aspergillus minimal medium 
(AMM) supplemented with 5-fluoroorotic acid (0.5 g L−1) and uracil (10 
mM) to generate spores. Spores of other normal A. terreus strains were 
harvested from 7-day-old potato dextrose agar plates (PDA). The codon- 
optimized aconitate isomerases, adi1 and tbrA, were synthesized and 
cloned into the pEASY vector. The IPM medium (110 g L−1 glucose, 3 g 
L−1 NH4NO3, 1 g L−1 corn steep liquor powder (Lanhe® Qiqihar Runxin 
corn Biotech. Co. LTD, China), 0.01 g L−1 FeSO4, 0.01 g L−1 
(NH4)2HPO4, 0.4 g L−1 MgSO4, and pH adjusted to 3.2) is the industrial 
production medium for itaconic acid. 
2.2. Deletion of cadA by gene-targeting in A. terreus 
The flanking regions of cadA with a length of about 1.5 kb were 
amplified from the genomic DNA of A. terreus CICC40205 using primer 
pairs U-cadA-F/U-cadA-R and D-cadA-F/D-cadA-R, respectively. The 
selectable marker pyrGAn was amplified from plasmid pXH106 and then 
fused with the flanking regions of cadA by fusion PCR (Huang et al., 
2016). The gene-targeting cassette was amplified using the nested 
primers C-cadA-F/C-cadA-R from the product of fusion PCR and further 
transformed into At-ΔpyrG using the protoplast-PEG method as 
described previously (Huang et al., 2014b, 2016).The transformants 
were selected on PDAS plates and confirmed by genomic PCR using the 
primer pair U-cadA-F/D-cadA-R and designed as At-ΔcadA. All primers 
used in this work are listed in Table S1. 
2.3. Heterologous expression of aconitate isomerases in A. terreus 
For the expression of aconitate isomerases using the promoter PcadA, 
the expression cassettes were integrated at the cadA site. The flanking 
regions of cadA, U1-cadA, and D1-cadA, were amplified from the 
genomic DNA of A. terreus CICC40205 using primer pairs U1-cadA-F/ 
U1-cadA-R and D1-cadA-F/D1-cadA-R. The fragments of TtrpC and 
pyrGAn were amplified from plasmid pXH2-1 and pXH106, respectively 
(Huang et al., 2014b, 2016). The isomerases tbrA and adi1 were 
amplified by PCR using primer pairs tbrA-F/tbrA-R (TtrpC) and 
adi1-F/adi1-R (TtrpC), respectively. These were then fused with three 
other fragments, U1-cadA, TtrpC, pyrGAn, and D1-cadA, by fusion PCR. 
The integration constructs were amplified from the products of fusion 
PCR using the primers C-cadA-F/C-cadA-R and then transferred into 
strain At-ΔpyrG. The transformants were selected on PDAS, confirmed 
by genomic PCR using the primers U-cadA-F/D-cadA-R, and designed as 
cadA::PcadA-adi1 and cadA::PcadA-tbrA, respectively. The strains 
expressing aconitate isomerases using the PcadA at ku80 site were 
constructed through the same strategy, and designed as ku80::Pca­
dA-adi1 and ku80::PcadA-tbrA, respectively. 
For the expression of aconitate isomerases using promoter PgpdAt, 
the expression cassettes were integrated at the site of ku80. The flanking 
regions of ku80 were amplified from the genomic DNA of A. terreus 
CICC40205 using primer pairs U-ku80-F/U-ku80-R (PgpdAt) and D- 
ku80-F (hph)/D-ku80-R respectively. The fragments of PgpdAt and 
TtrpC-hph were amplified from plasmid pXH2-1(Huang et al., 2014b). 
The isomerases adi1 and tbrA were amplified by PCR and fused with four 
other fragments, U-ku80, PgpdAt, TtrpC-hph, and D-ku80, by fusion PCR. 
The integration constructs were amplified from the products of fusion 
PCR using the primers C-ku80-F/C-ku80-R and then transferred into

2.5. Optimization of fermentation medium in the shake flask 
Optimization of fermentation medium was determined by L16 (4 ×
4) orthogonal design in shake flasks. The IPM medium was used as the 
basis for medium optimization. The influence of six factors, glucose, 
NH4NO3, corn steep liquor powder (CSL powder), FeSO4, (NH4)2HPO4, 
and MgSO4, on TAA production was investigated. A total of 16 experi­
ments were performed using the orthogonal design based on the basic 
medium of IPM (Table S2). Fermentation was carried out in 500-mL 
Erlenmeyer flasks containing 55 mL medium at 37 ◦C for 120 h. All 
experiments were performed in biological triplicate, and the means ±
standard deviations (SD) of the results were calculated. 
2.6. qRT-PCR analysis 
To determine the sampling time for qRT-PCR analysis, 2 × 108 spores 
of each strain were inoculated in 55 ml of IPM medium in 500-ml shake 
flasks for 12 hs of cultivation, then 5 ml fermentation liquid were 
inoculated into fresh 55 ml of IPM medium shake flasks. The growth 
curves were first determined by the biomass sampling at 4 h, 6 h, 9 h, 15 
h, 18 h, 24 h, 36 h, and 48 h. The biomass was calculated by the method 
of packed mycelial volume (p.m.v). The selected mycelium samples 
were frozen using liquid nitrogen. For RNA extraction, mycelium was 
ground via liquid nitrogen and extracted using EasyPure® RNA Kit 
(Trans Gen Biotech, China). PerfectStart® Uni RT&qPCR Kit (Trans Gen 
Biotech, China) was used to reverse-transcribe 100 ng of total RNA to 
cDNA. The amount of cDNA was determined using NanoPhotometer 
NP80 (IMPLEN, USA). The qRT-PCR analysis was performed on a Quant 
Studio 1 Real-Time PCR System (ABI, USA) with the primers listed in 
Table S2. PerfectStart® Green qPCR SuperMix (Trans Gen Biotech, 
China) was used for the reactions as the protocol. Gene transcription 
levels were calculated relative to the internal control gene, actin 
(ATEG_06973), using the cycle threshold (CT) 2−ΔCt method. The com­
parison of the samples of different engineered strains was calculated 
relative to the expression level of reference sample CT81-4 h using the 
2−ΔCtΔCt method. Three independent experiments were calculated, and 
transcription of the control (CT81-4 h) was set to 1. p-values were 
estimated using two-way-ANOVO analysis. 
2.7. Lab-scale and large-scale fermentation 
Lab-scale fermentation of a two-stage process was performed in a 
100 L bioreactor (Baoxing, Shanghai, China) with a working volume of 
75 L, aeration rate of 0.48 vvm, and a culture temperature of 37 ◦C. The 
influence of DO level (15%, 25%, 30%, 35% and 45%) on TAA pro­
duction was carried out by automatically adjusting the stirring rate 
(150–350 rpm). For the first and second seed cultures, a 30 L bioreactor 
with a working volume of 21 L was employed by controlling the DO level 
within 25%–30%. All experiments were performed with IPM medium 
except otherwise stated. 
For the pilot scale fermentation, the effect of aeration rates (0.14 
vvm, 0.33 vvm and 0.47 vvm) on TAA production was evaluated in a 5 
m3 bioreactor (Eastbio® Zhenjiang, China) with a constant DO value of 
25% obtained by adjusting the stirring rate. The 100 L/1 m3 bioreactors 
with a working volume of 70 L/0.5 m3 were used as the first and second 
seed cultures, respectively. Finally, the fermentation process was tested 
at a demonstration scale (20 m3 fermenter), and the 1st-generation 
strain of At-ΔcadA and 2nd-generation strain of cadA::PcadA-tbrA 
were evaluated. Fermentation was performed in an Eastbio® 20 m3 
fermenter with a working volume of 14 m3 and an aeration rate of 0.11 
vvm at 37 ◦C. Adjusting the stirring rate ensured a constant DO value 
(25%). All cultivations were performed in the optimized medium con­
taining glucose (136 g L−1), NH4NO3 (4 g L−1), FeSO4 (0.06 g L−1), 
(NH4)2HPO4 (0.01 g L−1), MgSO4 (0.4 g L−1), and corn steep liquor 
powder (2 g L−1). 
2.8. Field trial of TAA against cucumber root-knot nematodes 
TAA powder with a purity of 85% was extracted from the fermen­
tation supernatant and prepared by spray drying. All sites for the ex­
periments were selected due to the history of PPNs infestations. The 
suitability of the sites was determined according to the composition and 
population density of PPNs species. Planting was done manually at a 
10–15 cm depth using a hand-held cucumber planter and at a 25 cm 
within-row spacing. Fifty cucumber roots of each treatment were 
sampled for the statistical analysis of gall and disease index. Each cu­
cumber was irrigated with a 200 mL mixture at a determined concen­
tration when transplanting. 
The nematicide fosthiazate at a 30% concentration (Ishihara Indus­
trial Co., Ltd., Japan) was used as a positive control. The untreated 
control was irrigated with water. Each treatment had three replicates. 
All the treatments were performed twice, at the time of transplanting 
and 30 days after the first irrigation. Regular management was carried 
out during the experimental period. The gall index was scored on a 1- to 
7-point scale: 1 = no galls; 2 = 1–15% galled roots; 3 = 15–30% galled 
roots; 4 = 30–45% galled roots; 5 = 45–60% galled roots; 6 = 60–75% 
galled roots; and 7 = >75% galled roots (Bridge and Page, 1980). The 
disease index was calculated according to the following formula: Disease 
index = [Σ (Number of cucumbers in the gall index × Gall index)/(­
Number of total cucumbers × 7)] × 100. The effect of TAA against 
root-knot nematodes in the field was assessed with the formula: Man­
aging effect (%) = [(Disease index of control – Disease index of TAA 
treatment)/Disease index of control] × 100. All the statistical analysis 
was performed using the Duncan’s Multiple Range test (DMRT) in DPS 
software (Cheng et al., 2015; Sauder and DeMars, 2019; Tang and 
Zhang, 2013), with a significant difference determined by p < 0.05 and 
p < 0.01. 
3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Blocking the biosynthesis of itaconic acid in A. terreus resulted in the 
accumulation of aconitic acid 
The cis-aconitic acid decarboxylase CadA is the only essential 
enzyme required for the biosynthesis of itaconic acid from central car­
bon metabolism in A. terreus, which has been confirmed by in vitro 
enzymatic analysis, in vivo gene deletion, and heterologous expression in 
E. coli (Harder et al., 2016), yeast (Blazeck et al., 2015; Harder et al., 
2016; Sun et al., 2020), and A. niger (Blumhoff et al., 2013) (Fig. 1A). To 
achieve efficient microbial production of aconitic acid, A. terreus 
CICC40205, which is an industrial itaconic acid-producing strain with a 
final titer of 75 g L−1 was used as a chassis cell for the design of the cell 
factory (Huang et al., 2014a). The gene-targeting cassette of the cadA 
gene was transformed into the At-ΔpyrG, the uracil auxotrophy of 
A. terreus CICC40205 with high homologous recombination frequency, 
using pyrGAn as a selectable marker (Fig. 1B). The disruption of cadA in 
the transformants which could grow on the PDAS was confirmed by 
genomic PCR. The right one was designed as strain At-ΔcadA, and tested 
by cultivation in shake flasks using the fermentation condition of ita­
conic acid. Significant amounts of aconitic acid are present in the culture 
of At-ΔcadA mutant without any itaconic acid, while itaconic acid is the

that blocking the biosynthesis of itaconic acid successfully led to the 
accumulation of aconitic acid. However, both CAA and TAA are present 
in large amounts, not just CAA. 
The time-scale production of aconitic acid was analyzed to reveal the 
changes in the titer and ratio of two isomers during fermentation. The 
titer of aconitic acid gradually increased with time and reached 10.8 g 
L−1 at 5 days. It is significantly higher than that in the cadA deletion 
mutant of Aspergillus pseudoterreus ATCC32359, which was less than 4 g 
L−1 at 5 days and 10 g L−1 at 10 days (Deng et al., 2020). In the early 
stage, CAA was the main component, and only 10% was TAA on the first 
day. The proportion of TAA gradually increased with the increasing 
fermentation time and finally reached 80% at 120 h (Fig. 1C). This 
phenomenon may result from the spontaneous conversion between TAA 
and CAA. High temperature favors the conversion of CAA to TAA 
(Fig. S1A), while low temperature does the opposite. At equilibrium 
(room temperature), the CAA:TAA is maintained at approximately 1:9, 
means that the maximum TAA ratio is about 90% (Fig. S1B). These re­
sults indicated that CAA is indeed the precursor of itaconic acid 
biosynthesis in A. terreus, and deleting cadA led to the accumulation of 
CAA followed by spontaneous isomerization to form TAA. This infor­
mation was essential because it demonstrated that it is easier to produce 
TAA compared with CAA. The remaining CAA in the culture can be 
quickly converted into TAA with a simple heating process after 
fermentation. 
3.2. Introduction of exogenous aconitate isomerases significantly 
improved TAA production 
Aconitate isomerase catalyzes the reversible isomerization between 
CAA and TAA, which was initially of interest because of the assimilation 
of trans-aconitic acid in microorganisms and the biosynthesis of TAA in 
plants. In B. thuringiensis (Du et al., 2017), TAA is converted from CAA 
catalyzed by the aconitate isomerase TbrA and secreted extracellularly 
via the transporter TbrB. This demonstrated that CAA and aconitate 
isomerase are critical for TAA biosynthesis. In addition to TbrA, aconi­
tate isomerase Adi1 of Ustilago maydis was demonstrated to efficiently 
convert CAA to TAA in itaconic acid biosynthesis (Geiser et al., 2016). 
To enhance the production of TAA, Aid1 and TbrA were heterologously 
expressed in the aconitic acid-producing A. terreus At-ΔcadA. To get 
different types of strong promoters during the organic acid fermentation 
process, we screened the target genes from the time-series RNA-Seq 
transcriptomes of A. terreus under the industrial fermentation condition.

Three transformants of each engineered strain were selected for 
evaluation in shake flasks. Compared with strain At-ΔcadA, the ratio of 
TAA was significantly increased in all four engineered strains, suggest­
ing that aconitate isomerases were functionally expressed in A. terreus. 
Among these strains, significantly less aconitic acid was produced in the 
strain ku80::PgpdAt-tbrA. In contrast, the titer of TAA and total aconitic 
acid increased significantly in other engineered strains (Fig. 2B). For 
both isomerases, heterologous expression using the PcadA promoter was 
better compared with using PgpdAt. The best combination is expressing 
TbrA using the PcadA promoter, and the highest TAA titer reached 20 g 
L−1 in transformant CT81 with a TAA ratio of 90%. It is significantly 
higher compared with that in the At-ΔcadA strain, which produced 10 g 
L−1 TAA with a TAA ratio of 57% (Fig. 2B). This demonstrated that 
introducing aconitate isomerase is an efficient strategy for enhancing 
TAA production. The aconitate isomerase can rapidly convert CAA to 
TAA to prevent it from entering the TCA cycle and being consumed, 
effectively promoting the accumulation of TAA and increasing the 
glucose conversion rate. 
As shown in Fig. 2C, the titer of TAA was slightly lower in strain 
ku80::PcadA-tbrA than that in strain cadA::PcadA-tbrA (~10.7% lower), 
but not statistically different. Meanwhile, the TAA titer of strain ku80:: 
PcadA-tbrA is significantly higher than that of strain ku80::PgpdAt-tbrA. 
It indicated that integration sites of ku80 and cadA have no different 
effect on the expression of exogenous genes, and appropriate promoter 
was the critical factor. Because CAA is an essential metabolite for 
biochemical processes in life, a delicate balance between cell growth and 
TAA production needs to be achieved. In A. terreus CICC40205, the 
biosynthesis of itaconic acid begins after sufficient biomass growth after 
24 h of fermentation, and no aconitic acid was accumulated in the 
fermentation process. This indicated that the glycolytic pathway and 
TCA cycle are fully dedicated to cell growth. During biosynthesis, the 
decarboxylation of CAA catalyzed by CadA is highly coordinated with its 
synthesis. Therefore, we speculated that the transcriptional profiles of 
the promoter PcadA are consistent with CAA production in the industrial 
fermentation process. In contrast, PgpdAt is a strong constitutive pro­
moter of glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase, which is a 
housekeeping gene closely related to basic vital activities. It maintains 
high transcription of the target gene throughout the fermentation pro­
cess, from mycelial growth to itaconic acid production. 
This speculation was confirmed by growth curve and qRT-PCR 
analysis (Fig. 2D–E). The transcription level of tbrA in strain cadA:: 
PcadA-tbrA (CT81) is very low at the initial growth stage of fermentation 
and then increases sharply from 15 h to 24 h, which is consistent with 
the synthesis of itaconic acid and aconitic acid (Fig. 2E). Whereas, in 
strain ku80::PgpdAt-tbrA (GT71), the transcription level of tbrA from 4 h, 
9 h, 15 h–24 h is always at a high level that can only be reached at 24 h in 
strain CT81. Heterologously expressing aconitate isomerase with PgpdAt 
promoter may result in a deficiency of CAA in the early growth stage and 
further retarded biomass accumulation, which disturbed the coordina­
tion between cell growth and TAA production (Fig. 2D). Therefore, 
promoter PcadA is more favorable for TAA synthesis because its tran­
scriptional profile is consistent with CAA production in the fermentation 
process. In addition, A. terreus LYT10, a more efficient itaconic acid- 
producing strain derived from A. terreus CICC 40205, has been tested 
as an optional chassis strain. Unfortunately, it failed to construct a more 
efficient TAA-producing strain than strain CT81 (Fig. S3). 
3.3. Optimization of fermentation conditions in the shake flask and 100 L 
bioreactor 
Although the itaconic acid production process of A. terreus is well 
established, optimization of fermentation is still necessary for the 
engineered TAA-producing strains. The parent strain of A. terreus was 
originally sensitive to cultivation conditions, especially under nitrogen 
limitation, the strains were facilitated to excrete itaconic acids and 
decrease energy consumption (Vassilev et al., 1992), and high oxygen 
tension supply increased the itaconic acid accumulation (Moln´ar ´A et al., 
2018; Nemest´othy et al., 2019). On the other side, the fermentation pH 
for both TAA and itaconic acid are different because of their various 
dissociation constants, which can significantly affect the physiological 
state of the cells (Hevekerl et al., 2014). The medium components were 
first optimized for strain CT81 based on the itaconate fermentation 
medium (IPM) in shake flasks. The highest TAA titer was achieved in 
IPM-15# (with 27% higher glucose, 5-fold higher FeSO4, and 100% 
higher Corn Steep Liquor powder than the basic IPM medium), which is 
48% improved aconitic acid titer than that of the IPM medium (Fig. 3A). 
The corn steep powder, the only organic nitrogen source in the medium, 
is the main factor affecting TAA production as it does in the production 
of itaconic acid. We detected its composition and content of amino acids 
and major vitamins and found which are significantly different in 
various corn steep powders (Fig. S4). It suggested that some micro­
nutrients were critical for TAA production in A. terreus. Therefore, 0.1 g 
L−1 of these micronutrients was added in the medium IPM-15# to test 
the contribution to TAA production. The result showed that adding 
L-Met significantly increased the TAA titer from 15 g L−1 to 29 g L−1 
(Fig. 3B). 
Simultaneously, the optimized mediums were evaluated by a two- 
stage fermentation process using a 100 L bioreactor. The growth phase 
ended after 24 h of cultivation without DO control (aeration 0.48 vvm 
and agitation 250 rpm). In the fermentation phase, the constant control 
of DO at 25% was set by automatic stirring adjustment (150–350 rpm) 
with an aeration rate of 0.48 vvm. For L-Met addition in IPM-15# 
(medium IPM-15# +L-Met), the maximum titer (65 g L−1 TAA) was 
produced at 120 h with a productivity of 0.64 g L−1 h−1, and a yield of 
0.43 g g−1 glucose, which is significantly higher compared with the 
absence of L-Met. Then, the optimization of dissolved oxygen (DO) was 
performed using the medium of IPM-15# +L-Met. In the fermentation 
phase, the constant control of DO at 15%, 25%, 30%, 35%, and 45% was 
set by automatic stirring adjustment (150–350 rpm) with an aeration 
rate of 0.48 vvm. The TAA titer reached 61.8 g L−1 at 69 h under the low 
DO (25%) condition, which is higher than that achieved under DO 
conditions of 15%, 30%, 35%, and 45% (Fig. 3C). This is an efficient 
fermentation process for the production of TAA. 
3.4. Scale-up of TAA production at demonstration scale of 5 m3 and 20 
m3 fermenters 
The optimized two-stage fermentation process was scaled up to a 5 
m3 fermenter. In the growth phase, the process had a constant initial 
setting agitation (80 rpm) and constant aeration rate without DO con­
trol. In the production phase, the dissolved oxygen tension (DO) was 
kept constant at 25% saturation by automatically adjusting the stirring 
rate at a fixed aeration rate. Here, three levels of aeration rate were 
tested, 0.14 vvm, 0.33 vvm, and 0.47 vvm. Fig. 4A shows that TAA 
production is most efficient when the aeration rate is 0.14 vvm, and the 
TAA titer reaches 50.2 g L−1 at 100 h. The fermentation process was 
further demonstrated at the industrial scale using a 20 m3 fermenter 
(Fig. 4B). Meanwhile, the strains of 1st-generation (At-ΔcadA) and 2nd- 
generation (cadA::PcadA-tbrA) were further evaluated. For the 1st-gen­
eration strain, 42.2 g L−1 aconitic acid was produced by fed-batch 
fermentation (900 kg of glucose was fed four times) at 250 h, 
achieving a productivity of 0.17 g L−1 h−1 with a yield of 0.21 g g−1 
glucose. The fermentation using the 2nd-generation strain is more

3.5. The nematicidal activity of produced TAA was evaluated in a field 
trial 
Plant parasite nematodes (PPNs) pose a serious threat to global 
agriculture, which causes tremendous economic losses in crops (John 
et al., 2013). The control of plant parasite nematodes has become 
increasingly urgent after reducing traditional chemical nematicides 
because of the increasing drug resistance, environmental contamination, 
and high application costs (Chen et al., 2020). To evaluate the nemati­
cidal activity of TAA produced by our engineered A. terreus, a field trial 
was performed on cucumbers with plant root-knot nematodes (mainly 
M. hapla and M. arenaria), and a commercial nematicide fosthiazate was 
used as a positive control. The TAA powder from the fermentation su­
pernatant was prepared by spray drying and diluted into different con­
centration solutions for root irrigation of plants. the root-knot galling of 
all plants were counted and evaluated after twice root irrigations, 
including the positive and untreated controls. It shows that many big 
root knots were formed in the roots of untreated plants, and this was 
significantly alleviated in the experimental groups treated with diluted 
TAA and fosthiazate (Fig. 5). 
Furthermore, the disease index and control efficiency were calcu­
lated according to the extent of root-knot galling (Table 2). The disease 
index of nontreated control was 27.3, while fosthiazate (180 g mu−1) 
was 6.37, with a control efficiency of 75.5%. The disease index of TAA 
treatments decreased gradually from 14.25 to 7.28 with increasing TAA 
concentration, and the control efficiency increased from 46.4% to 
71.3%. Statistical analysis showed that root-knot nematode infections 
treated with a TAA formulation of 690 g mu−1 and 560 g mu−1 were not 
significantly different from those treated with fosthiazate (180 g mu−1). 
This demonstrated that the produced TAA could efficiently control the 
plant parasite nematodes in the cucumber field trials. Given its low

4. Conclusions 
In this study, an efficient microbial synthesis and fermentation 
technology was developed for TAA production. First, the production of 
aconitic acid was achieved by blocking the itaconic acid biosynthesis 
pathway in the industrial strain A. terreus CICC40205. Second, the 
production of TAA was significantly enhanced by the heterologous 
expression of exogenous aconitate isomerases using suitable promoters, 
resulting in an approximately 3.6-fold improvement in productivity. The 
fermentation process was developed for TAA-producing strains and 
scaled up to the industrial scale gradually. The highest titer of 60.0 g 
L−1, with a productivity of 0.62 g L−1 h−1 and yield of 0.41 g g−1glucose, 
was achieved in a demonstration using a 20 m3 fermenter. Finally, the 
nematicidal activity of produced TAA was evaluated in a field trial. The 
plant parasite nematodes of cucumber were efficiently controlled. The 
technology developed in this study is promising for achieving com­
mercial production of TAA, which will greatly facilitate its subsequent 
application as a biopesticide and bio-based chemical.
