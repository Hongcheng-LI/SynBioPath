Picrachinentins A−F, 14-Membered Cyclopeptide Alkaloid-Type
Burpitides with Uncommon N‑Terminal Modifications from Picrasma
chinensis and Their Neuroprotective Activity

ABSTRACT: Picrachinentins A−F (1−6, respectively), six novel
cyclopeptide alkaloid-type burpitides (CPABs), were isolated and
fully elucidated from the EtOH extract of the stems and leaves of
Picrasma chinensis. Structurally, compounds 1−6 have a 14-
membered paracyclophane ring system that was closed through
an ether bond between the β-hydroxy amino acid and tyrosine and
modified with a 4,5-methylenedioxybenzoyloxy (MDBz, 3 and 5)
or hexanoyl (Hexa, 1, 2, 4, and 6) group at the N-terminus.
Interestingly, this is the first report on the isolation and
characterization of CPABs from plants of the Simaroubaceae
family. In addition, all compounds showed a neuroprotective effect
against H2O2-damaged SH-SY5Y cells. Compound 1 was further
investigated for its neuroprotective activities using a 1-methyl-4-
phenyl-1,2,3,6-tetrahydropyridine (MPTP)-induced Parkinson’s disease animal model, and it dramatically improved MPTP-
impaired motor behavioral performance. Biochemical analysis revealed compound 1 restored the tyrosine hydroxylase expression in
the striatum of the MPTP-damaged mouse brain, which demonstrates its protective effect on dopaminergic neurons.
W
ith ∼230 members, cyclopeptide alkaloids (CPAs)
make up one of the most prevalent groups of plant-
produced peptides.1 CPAs are distributed in a wide variety of
plant species such as Asteraceae, Rubiaceae, Euphorbiaceae,
etc., and most prominently in the Rhamnaceae family.2
Typically composed of four to six amino acids, CPAs are
characterized by an ether linkage between the phenolic oxygen
of tyrosine and the β-carbon of a nearby amino acid.3 Some
CPAs have a wide range of bioactivities, including antiviral,
antiproliferative, antibacterial, analgesic, and antiplasmodial
activity.4 As these compounds are formed by copper-
dependent BURP domain-containing proteins named burpitide
cyclases, Chekan and colleagues have recommended a novel
nomenclature and classificated CPAs as burpitides.5 Therefore,
we refer to these cyclic peptides as CPABs. These compounds
have attracted more attention from organic chemists and
pharmacologists, and cyclic peptide-based drugs have grown in
popularity in recent years.6 Several cyclic peptides such as
vancomycin (an antibiotic), octreotide (a growth hormone),
and caspofungin (an antifungal agent) are clinically used as
therapeutic agents.7
Picrasma chinensis (Simaroubaceae) is a perennial tree that is
mainly found in southwestern China and the IndoChina
Peninsula.8 Compared with the number of studies of other
species of the Picrasma genus, only a few studies have focused
on P. chinensis. So far, quassinoids, sesquiterpenoids, and β-
carboline-type alkaloids have been identified from P. chinensis.9
In the continuous effort to find new neuroprotective
phytochemical members from the Picrasma genus, we for the
first time isolated six unique CPABs (1−6) with a 14-
membered paracyclophane ring from P. chinensis (Figure 1).
Structurally, all of the CPABs are modified by a 4,5-
methylenedioxybenzoyloxy (MDBz, 3 and 5) or hexanoyl
(Hexa, 1, 2, 4, and 6) group at the N-terminus. To the best of
our knowledge, the isolation and characterization of CPABs
have never been reported in previous publications from any
plants of the Simaroubaceae family. Herein, we report the
discovery of CPABs with uncommon N-terminal modifications
in P. chinensis; the in vitro neuroprotective effects of all of the
isolated CPABs on H2O2-damaged SH-SY5Y cells were
investigated, and the in vivo neuroprotective effect of CPAB
1 was further examined on a 1-methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-

Picrachinentin A (1) was obtained as colorless crystals. On
the basis of HRMS (ESI) m/z [M + H]+ calcd for C39H49N4O7
685.3596, found 685.3594, the molecular formula of 1 has
been deduced as C39H48N4O7, with an index of hydrogen
deficiency of 18. The presence of OH/NH bond(s) and
carbonyl groups was suggested by the IR absorption bands at
3297 and 1639 cm−1, respectively. The 1H NMR data (Table
S1) showed signals for 14 aromatic protons. The 13C NMR
data (Table S3) revealed signals for five carbons that could be
assigned to amide or carbonyl carbons (δC 175.5, 173.0, 171.6,
171.1, and 170.6); 18 aromatic carbons, including one
oxygenated tertiary aromatic carbon (δC 156.5), three
quaternary carbons (δC 139.2, 138.6, and 132.3) and 14
tertiary carbons [δC 133.0, 130.6, 129.9 (2C), 129.5 (2C),
128.8 (2C), 128.7, 128.6 (2C), 127.0, 121.3, and 116.8]; five
tertiary carbons connected to oxygen or nitrogen (δC 81.6,
58.3, 54.6, 54.4, and 52.3); one common tertiary carbon (δC
25.4); seven secondary carbons (δC 43.3, 39.2, 39.0, 36.7, 32.0,
26.1, and 23.1); and three methyl carbons (δC 23.0, 22.9, and
14.5). This information indicated that 1 is an alkaloidal peptide
possessing four amino acid units, including one Tyr, one Leu,
and two Phe (Phe1 and Phe2) residues. This inference is
supported by MS/MS experiments (Figure S13). Further
analysis of the two-dimensional (2D) NMR spectroscopic data
of 1 revealed that it was a cyclopeptide alkaloid consisting of a
14-membered paracyclophane ring with an exocyclic side chain
(Figure 2).
The 1H−1H COSY correlations from Phe1-H5/Phe1-H9 (δH
7.82) to Phe1-H7 (δH 7.31) through Phe1-H6/Phe1-H8 (δH
7.41) confirmed the existence of a phenyl moiety. Phe1-H2 (δH
5.84) showed cross-peaks with Phe1-H3 (δH 6.42) and Phe1-
NH (δH 9.43) in the COSY spectrum, together with the
HMBC correlations from Phe1-H3 to Phe1-C4 (δC 139.2) and
Phe1-C5/9 (δC 129.5) that showed the existence of the β-
hydroxyphenylalanine unit. The HMBC correlation of Phe1-
H3/Tyr-C7 (δC 156.5) suggested that the hydroxy group in
the β-hydroxyphenylalanine residue was connected to a
tyrosine unit via an aryl−alkyl ether bond.10 The tyrosine
moiety was identified from the
1H−1H COSY cross-peaks
between Tyr-H5 at δH 7.48 and Tyr-H6 at δH 7.12, between
Tyr-H8 at δH 7.56 and Tyr-H9 at δH 7.07, and between Tyr-
H2 (δH 5.89) and both Tyr-H3 (δH 3.85 and 2.95) and Tyr-
NH (δH 9.82), together with the HMBC correlations from
both Tyr-H5 and Tyr-H9 to Tyr-C3 (δC 39.2) and Tyr-H2
and Tyr-H3 to Tyr-C1 (δC 175.5). In addition, the HMBC
correlation between Tyr-H2/Tyr-NH and the amide carbonyl
carbon at δC 171.6 (Leu-C1) suggested that the tyrosine
moiety was linked to a leucine moiety by an amide bond. The
leucine fragment was characterized from the 1H−1H COSY
correlations between Leu-H4 (δH 1.75) and both Leu-H5 and
Leu-H6 (δH 0.74) and among Leu-H4/Leu-H3 (δH 1.65)/Leu-
H2 (δH 5.10)/Leu-NH (δH 9.55), together with the HMBC
correlations from Leu-H2 to both Leu-C3 (δC 43.3) and the
amide carbonyl carbon [δC 171.6 (Leu-C1)]. HMBC cross-
peaks were observed for Leu-NH and Leu-H2 with the
carbonyl carbon of the β-hydroxyphenylalanine moiety [δC
170.6 (Phe1-C1)]. Thus, the 14-membered macrocyclic ring
was clarified. In addition, phenylalanine was identified as the
side chain amino acid by detailed NMR spectroscopic data.

171.1) also showed that an amide linkage attached Phe2 to the
macrocyclic ring. Notably, the lipophilic moiety of the

The relative configuration was elucidated via NOESY
experiments in C5D5N as shown in Figure 2A. From the
coupling constants and 13C NMR chemical shifts, the relative
configuration of the β-hydroxyphenylalanine residue (Phe1-
H2/H3) was deduced. Phe1-H3 was observed as a doublet
peak in C5D5N with a large coupling constant J2,3 of 9.6 Hz,
indicating that H2 and H3 have a large dihedral angle.
Furthermore, the presence of an L-erythro-β-hydroxyphenyla-
lanine residue (2S*,3S*) was confirmed by the chemical shift
of Phe1-C3 in 1 (δC 81.6 in C5D5N).11 Hydrolysis of the
peptide under acidic conditions followed by derivatization with
L-FDAA and LC-MS analysis confirmed the presence in the
molecule of L-Tyr, L-Phe, and L-Leu (Figure S75). As shown in
Figure 1, the structure of 1 was determined. Its absolute
configuration was further verified by X-ray diffraction data
(Figure 2B).
The direct comparison of the NMR data of 2 with those of 1
indicated that there are two fewer methyls, one fewer methine,
and one more oxymethylene in compound 2 (Figure S25). The
difference between the two compounds is that the ring-bonded
α-amino acid in 1 was leucine and in 2 was serine. The serine
moiety was identified from the
1H−1H COSY cross-peaks
between Ser-H2 (δH 5.45) and both Ser-H3 (δH 4.23 and
3.98) and Ser-NH (δH 9.63). Analysis of the NOESY
correlations and NMR data (Tables S1 and S3) suggested
that 2 and 1 share identical relative configurations.
Picrachinentin C (3) and picrachinentin D (4) were isolated
as white powder with molecular formulas of C41H43N7O9 and
C39H49N7O7, respectively. Analysis of the 2D NMR spectra of
3 and 4 allowed the assignment of tyrosine, β-hydroxypheny-
lalanine, arginine, and phenylalanine amino acid units and
provided the connections among these subunits. Comparing
the NMR data (Tables S1 and S3) of 4 with those of 3 showed
that the only difference is in the terminal side chain unit. The
side chain units of compounds 3 and 4 were identified as 4,5-
methylenedioxybenzoyloxy (MDBz) and hexanoyl groups,
respectively. The HMBC correlations from MDBz-H3 (δH
7.61) and Phe2-NH (δH 8.93) to the carbonyl group at δC
167.6 (MDBz-C1) for 3 and the NOESY correlation of Phe2-
NH (δH 8.88) with Hexa-H2 (δH 2.21) for 4 suggest the
location of the two groups.
Picrachinentin E (5) showed an HRMS (ESI) m/z [M +
H]+ calcd for 685.2869, found 685.2882, corresponding to the
molecular formula C37H40N4O9, with 20 degrees of unsatura-
tion. Comparison of the NMR spectra (Tables S1−S3) of 5
with those of 3 indicated the existence of Tyr, Phe, Pro, Ile,
and MDBz, which were also confirmed by 2D NMR analyses.
The NOESY correlation between Phe-NH (δH 8.49) and Pro-
H2 (δH 4.85) revealed that the proline residue was attached to
the β-hydroxyphenylalanine unit. In addition, the NOESY
correlation of Pro-H5 (δH 3.32 and 3.28) with MDBz-H3 (δH
7.32) indicated the connection between proline and the MDBz
group.
Picrachinentin F (6) was identified as a methyl ether
derivative of compound 1, and the HMBC correlation between
OMe (δH 3.77) and Tyr-C1 (δC 173.3) showed the methoxy
group was attached at C1 of the tyrosine fragment in
compound 6. Finally, the full assignment of the chemical
structures of 2−6 was achieveded using the other 2D NMR
correlations (Figures S1 and S2), Marfey’s methods (Figure
S80), and a comparison of the ECD curves (Figure 3). A
description of the detailed structural analysis is available in the
Supporting Information.
All six CPABs were tested for the protective effects on
H2O2-damaged SH-SY5Y cells by using the CCK8 reagent.
The statistical results demonstrated the cell viabilities
decreased by H2O2 can be restored by these compounds to
varying degrees (Figure S81). Among them, compounds 2, 4,
and 5 can significantly increase the viability of SH-SY5Y cells
decreased by H2O2 at both 25 and 50 μM, while compounds 1
and 6 at 50 μM and compound 3 at 25 μM exhibited
significant activities. The protective activities of these
compounds were better than or equivalent to that of positive
control Trolox (as shown in Figure S81 and Table S4).
In an MPTP-induced PD mouse model, the neuroprotective
effects of compound 1 were further studied. From the day after
the first MPTP injection, mouse body weights decreased
sharply due to MPTP toxicity. No significant difference in
body weight was found between the MPTP control and the
groups treated with compound 1 or ropinirole (as shown in

hydroxylase (TH) is the key enzyme for dopamine (DA)
synthesis, and its level in the brain reflects the survival of the
dopaminergic neuron.12 The neurotoxin MPTP blocks the
respiratory chain by inhibiting complex Ι in mitochondria,
which leads to dopaminergic neuron death, a decrease in the
level of dopamine, and then impairment of motor behavior.13
Compound 1 improved MPTP-impaired motor behavior in the
grip test and pole test to varying degrees 4, 24, and 48 h after
the last MPTP injection (as shown in panels B and C of Figure
4).
Western blot analysis showed that the level of TH
expression in ST was notably decreased by MPTP injection,
and 50 mg of compound 1 per kilogram counteracted the
toxicity of MPTP and enhanced TH expression in ST (as
shown in panels D and E of Figure 4). The results were further
confirmed by an immunofluorescence assay (as Figure 4F,G);
compound 1 at a dose of 50 mg/kg markedly counteracted the
damage caused by MPTP and promoted the survival of the
dopaminergic neuron, and the activity was equivalent to that of
positive control ropinirole at a dose of 5 mg/kg.
In summary, six novel cyclopeptide alkaloid-type burpitides,
picrachinentins A−F (1−6, respectively), were isolated from P.
chinensis and elucidated by various spectroscopic methods. All
of the isolated compounds exhibited neuroprotective effects
against H2O2-damaged human neuroblastoma SH-SY5Y cells.
Compound 1 can attenuate MPTP-impaired motor behavioral
performances in the grip test and pole test on an MPTP-
induced PD mouse model and restore TH expression in the ST
of PD mouse brain, reflecting its beneficial effects on
dopaminergic neuron survival. Altogether, our study reports
N-terminally acylated CPABs for the first time; the uncommon
N-terminal acylation in CPABs may contribute to their ability
to cross the blood−brain barrier, thus promoting the anti-
Parkinsonian efficacy. Although more structure−activity
relationships (SARs) and pharmacological mechanisms of
action need to be studied in the future, the current evidence
proves that compound 1 is one of the potential candidates for
PD therapeutic drugs.
