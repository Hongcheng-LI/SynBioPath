Unraveling the Fungal Strategy for Tetrahydroxanthone
Biosynthesis and Diversiﬁcation

ABSTRACT: In vivo and in vitro reconstitution of the biosynthesis of
fungal tetrahydroxanthones (THXs), blennolides A and C, led to the
identiﬁcation and characterization of the key enzymes for THX
biosynthesis and diversiﬁcation. The unusual isomerase NsrQ plays a
crucial role in the THX skeleton synthesis probably by catalyzing the 1,2-
hydride shift and methyl group epimerization, thus allowing dearomatizing
cyclization to provide the THX architecture.
X
anthones make up a widespread and representative class
of natural products that display various potent biological
activities.1 Thus, the xanthone scaﬀold has been recognized as
a “privileged structure”,2 and xanthones have attracted an
immense amount of attention for more than a century because
of their structures, biological functions, chemical synthesis, and
biosynthetic pathways. Among the producers of naturally
occurring xanthones, fungi exhibit a remarkable ability to
synthesize diverse and structurally intriguing molecules (Figure
1A),1b,3 as exempliﬁed by secalonic acid D with potent
biological activities.4 Recently, the biosynthetic gene clusters
for several fungal xanthones, including di- and tetrahydrox-
anthones, have been identiﬁed.5 At the moment, there is a
consensus that the anthraquinone chrysophanol is the
common pathway intermediate for fungal xanthones, and
additionally, extensive bioinformatic analysis identiﬁed poten-
tially important enzymes for xanthone scaﬀold formation.5g In
our previous work, we have partially elucidated the biosyn-
thesis of the tetrahydroxanthone (THX) heterodimer, neo-
sartorin, which is synthesized by the nsr cluster in Aspergillus
novofumigatus (Figure 1B and Figure S1).5e We have revealed
that the oxidation of chrysophanol provides a branch point in
the biosynthesis to yield two monomeric units of neosartorin
and identiﬁed several enzymes involved in the late-stage
biosynthesis. Nevertheless, the mechanism of THX backbone
formation and the involved enzymes still need to be elucidated.
To deeply understand the mechanism of THX biogenesis,
we initially sought to reconstitute the biosynthesis of
blennolides A (1) and C (2) by expressing the 11 genes
expected to be involved in the biosynthesis of 1 and 2 [nsrB,
-C, -E, -D, -R, -J, -I, -F, -G, -K, and -O (Figure S1)]. Thus, the
genes were introduced into Aspergillus oryzae NSAR1,6 which
is an eﬃcient heterologous host for fungal natural product
biosynthesis.7 After the genes had been introduced, the A.
oryzae transformant yielded two major products, 3 and 4

Consistently, the 10-gene-expressing transformant without
nsrO also produced 3 and 4 (Figure 2A, lane ii), suggesting
that an additional gene(s) is required for THX production.
To identify the crucial enzyme(s) for the biosynthesis of 1
and 2, two proteins, NsrQ and NsrS, encoded by the nsr
cluster with an unknown function, were examined. Interest-
ingly, a recent study of other fungal xanthones, cryptospor-
ioptides, suggested the involvement of DmxR10, which is a
homologue of NsrQ/NsrS, in generating the xanthone scaﬀold;
however, no experimental evidence was provided.5g Therefore,
A. oryzae transformants with nsrQ and/or nsrS were generated
(Figure 2A, lanes iv−vi, and Figure 2B). As a result, only the
introduction of nsrQ yielded three new products, blennolides A
(1) and C (2) as well as the C-10a epimer of 2, epi-blennolide
C [2′ (Figure 2C)], indicating that NsrQ was the missing link
in THX synthesis.
Next, the reaction scheme providing the THX skeleton was
investigated. Because of the production of 3 and 4 in the
absence of NsrQ and NsrO, we reasoned that 3 and 4 were
transformed into 1 and 2, respectively, by these two enzymes.
To investigate this hypothesis, NsrQ and NsrO were puriﬁed
as an N-terminal His-tagged protein using an Escherichia coli
expression system and subjected to in vitro enzymatic reactions
with 3 or 4 as a substrate as well as NADPH; however, the
reactions did not yield any new products (Figure 3A),
indicating that 3 and 4 are not the pathway intermediates
and that FMO, NsrK, converts 5 and 6 to unstable products
that are subsequently accepted by NsrQ. Thus, NsrK was also
puriﬁed and incubated with NsrQ and NsrO using 5 or 6 as a
substrate. Remarkably, the reaction with 5 generated the
expected THX 1, which was observed only in the presence of
all three enzymes (Figure 3B). Similarly, 6 was eﬃciently
consumed by the enzymes to aﬀord 2 and 2′ (Figure 3C),
establishing the required components for THX scaﬀold
formation. Interestingly, the reaction of NsrK and NsrQ with
5 or 6 provided new products 7 and 8, respectively (Figure
3B,C, lane iii), whose molecular formulas were both
determined to be C16H16O8. After the isolation of 8, the
NMR spectra revealed that 8 is a mixture of diastereomers, in
which one of the aromatic rings of the substrate is opened
(Figure 3E). The structural determination of 7 was
unfortunately hampered due to the low yield and the fact
that 7 was obtained as a complex mixture; however, we
reasoned that 7 is a mixture of diastereomeric compounds with
a structure similar to that of 8 (Figure 3E), because 7 has the
same molecular formula as 8.
After successful in vitro reconstitution of THX synthesis, the
generation of diverse THXs by fungi was investigated, as
diﬀerent fungi can produce isomers of 1 and 2.1b To this end,
Claviceps purpurea, which produces dimeric THXs (ergo-
chromes) derived from blennolide A (1) and (−)-blennolide B
[9 (Figure 1A)],9 was selected to determine the mechanism by
which the fungus synthesizes two diastereomeric THXs. The
NsrO homologue, CPUR_05418, encoded by the biosynthetic
gene cluster of ergochromes (Table S1)5d,g was examined,
presuming that the SDR performs two diﬀerent reductions
with opposite stereoselectivities. In vitro reaction of
CPUR_05418, obtained from the E. coli expression system,

To obtain further insight into THX-forming enzyme
catalysis, we then evaluated the unstable product of the
FMO, NsrK, as it should be the substrate of NsrQ. Recently,
we proposed that NsrK forms products with an epoxide;5e
however, no such products were observed in the NsrK-
catalyzed reactions, and therefore, the reaction was performed
in the presence of N,N-diethyldithiocarbamate (DTC), which
has been utilized as an epoxide-trapping reagent.10 Con-
sequently, the reaction with 5 and DTC yielded only the
hydroxylated product 3 (Figure S3), indicating that no epoxide
is involved in the transformation. Subsequently, the SDR,
NsrO, was examined in detail as its function remained elusive;
we had previously proposed that NsrO may function as an
enoylreductase at C-6/C-7,5e whereas a more recent study has
suggested that NsrO homologues serve as a C-5 ketoreducta-
se.5g To resolve this issue, we prepared (4S)-[4-2H]NADPH,11
which can be used by NsrO, and obtained deuterium-labeled 1
from 5 using NsrK, NsrQ, and NsrO. The 1H NMR spectrum
of the labeled product revealed the disappearance of the signal
corresponding to H-5 (Figure 4A and Figure S4), indicating
that NsrO is a C-5 ketoreductase. Moreover, the function of
the NsrO homologue, CPUR_05418, was further investigated
to understand the mechanism by which the SDR yields two
diastereomeric products. Interestingly, the production ratio of
1 and 9 changed depending on the CPUR_05418 concen-
tration; a larger amount of 1 was produced at a lower
concentration of CPUR_05418 (Figure 3D). This observation
suggested that one of the substrates of CPUR_05418, which is
the direct precursor of 9 and is accepted by only
CPUR_05418, appears to be converted to another substrate
of the enzyme by NsrQ, which can be utilized by both NsrO
and CPUR_05418. Consistently, the reaction in D2O
generated 9 and 1 labeled with one atom and two atoms of
deuterium, respectively (Figure S5). This observation
indicated that an additional transformation is required for
the biosynthesis of 1 compared with that of 9.
Finally, the catalytic function of NsrQ was investigated via a
series of mutational experiments based on the model structure
of the enzyme generated by the Phyre2 server12 (Figure S6).
Thus, 16 amino acid residues, presumed to comprise the
substrate binding site of NsrQ, were mutated to alanine and
incubated with NsrK, CPUR_05418, and 5. Among the 16
mutants, only the E121A mutant almost completely lost the
ability to synthesize the THX products (Figure S7). Moreover,
the E121D and E121Q mutants also showed a signiﬁcantly
reduced activity (Figure S7), altogether indicating that Glu121
is the catalytic residue.
On the basis of the experimental results, we now propose
one possible mechanism aﬀording the THX structure (Figure 5
and Figure S8). For the production of blennolide A (1) and
(−)-blennolide B (9), 5 is initially hydroxylated by Fl-4a-OOH
in NsrK to yield 10, according to the general catalytic
mechanism of ﬂavoprotein hydroxylases.13 The hydroxycyclo-
hexadienone 10 rapidly undergoes rearomatization to give the
shunt pathway product 3 in the absence of NsrQ. To allow for
the dearomatizing cyclization that aﬀords the THX skeleton,
NsrQ isomerizes 10 to form 11, in which the hydrogen at C-5
might be transferred to C-6 via a 1,2-hydride shift, and a keto
group is installed at C-5. Considering that only Glu121 appears
to be catalytically critical for this transformation, it would
initially be responsible for the deprotonation of the C-5
hydroxy group and then reprotonate at C-7. The resultant
cyclohexenedione 11 is the key intermediate, after which the
pathway diverges to provide both 1 and 9. In one of the
branches, NsrQ further accepts 11 to invert the stereo-
chemistry at C-6 to give its enantiomer, 12, in which Glu121
might again be involved as the key catalytic residue. The
requirement of this epimerization is supported by the fact that

the reaction was performed in D2O (Figures 4B). Moreover,
the enzymatic product from NsrK and NsrQ, compounds 7,
seem to be derived from 11 and 12 by heterocyclization to
form a 3(2H)-furanone ring followed by retro-Claisen cleavage
(Figure S9), further indicating the intermediacy of 11 and 12
in the biosynthesis. In the last stage of the transformation, the
SDRs, NsrO and CPUR_05418, catalyze the C-5 ketoreduc-
tion either before or after the cyclization to give the xanthone
skeleton. In one possible scenario, CPUR_05418 utilizes both
11 and 12 to catalyze the C-5 ketoreduction to aﬀord 13 and
14, respectively, whereas only 12 is accepted by NsrO.
Eventually, the resultant hydroxycyclohexenones 13 and 14
undergo spontaneous heterocyclization to yield 9 and 1,
respectively, which occurs stereoselectively to prevent the 1,3-
axial−pseudoaxial interaction between the C-10a methyl ester
and the C-6 methyl group. In Aspergillus aculeatus, 12 can also
be transformed into blennolide B, the C-5 epimer of 1, most
likely by AacuF,5g functioning as a C-5R reductase (Figure
S10). Meanwhile, the synthesis of blennolide C (2) and its
epimer 2′ likely occurs in a similar manner; however, the ﬁnal
cyclization is apparently achieved nonselectively owing to the
lack of the C-6 methyl group (Figure S8). This is in
accordance with our in vivo and in vitro studies and a previous
study regarding the total synthesis of 2, wherein the
precyclized intermediate [15 (Figures S8 and S11)] is
spontaneously cyclized to yield both 2 and 2′.14
The proposed mechanism for the THX synthesis resembles
the reactions by 2-aminobenzoyl-CoA monooxygenase/reduc-
tase (ACMR).15 In the ACMR-catalyzed reactions, the
substrate anthraniloyl-CoA is initially oxidized by the
monooxygenase domain (ACM), followed by enoylreduction
by the reductase domain (ACR) to give the dearomatized
cyclohexene product (Figure S12). Isotope labeling experi-
ments indicated the requirement of a 1,2-hydride shift to
provide the substrate of ACR, supporting the plausibility of our
proposed pathway. However, ACMR does not require an
additional protein for the hydride shift, whereas NsrQ is
essential to dearomatization in the THX pathways (Figure
S13), thus providing a new enzymatic strategy for dearoma-
tization.
In conclusion, we have not only provided an in-depth model
of the THX biosynthesis but also achieved the ﬁrst total
biosynthesis of fungal THXs in a heterologous host. This study
should facilitate the biosynthetic elucidation and engineering
of this privileged class of natural products, which could further
expand the diversity of THXs.
