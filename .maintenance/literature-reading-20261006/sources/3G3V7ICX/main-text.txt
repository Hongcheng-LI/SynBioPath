ABSTRACT: Heterologous biosynthesis of paclitaxel (Taxol) has recently become a hot research topic due to its potential to
alleviate resource scarcity and shorten production cycles. However, the poor expression and low efficiency of catalytic components
and challenges in combinatorial regulation remain critical bottlenecks, hindering the efficient synthesis of paclitaxel and its
intermediates. In this study, we constructed an early stage biosynthesis pathway of paclitaxel from glycerol in an Escherichia coli (E.
coli) cell factory. A 10.3-fold increase in taxadiene titer (up to 131 mg/L) was achieved through the engineering of isopentenyl
diphosphate isomerase and geranylgeranyl diphosphate synthase, accompanied by a substrate channeling strategy. Promoter and
protein engineering were then employed to optimize the production of taxadien-5α-ol and taxadien-5α-yl-acetate, resulting in titers
of 17.9 and 7.6 mg/L, respectively. Finally, cofactor engineering strategies involving intracellular heme supplementation and
NADPH regeneration were applied to synthesize taxadiene-5α,10β-diol-5-acetate, yielding titers of 8.7 mg/L in shake-flask
cultivation and 18.5 mg/L in a 5 L jar fermenter. To the best of our knowledge, this represents the highest level reported to date in
E. coli. The engineered strains and enzymes constructed in this study will serve as a solid foundation for subsequent paclitaxel
biosynthesis, and the engineering strategies employed here may be applied to the de novo biosynthesis of terpenoids in microbial cell
factories.
KEYWORDS: early stage paclitaxel biosynthesis pathway, Escherichia coli, substrate channeling, protein engineering, cofactor engineering
■INTRODUCTION
The rise of synthetic biology has provided new methods for the
large-scale production of natural products, such as squalene,
artemisinic acid, and salidroside.1−3 In addition, the heterol-
ogous biosynthesis of complex natural products has led to
numerous breakthroughs. For example, Caputi et al. elucidated
the biosynthetic pathway of vinblastine,4 and Zhang et al.
subsequently achieved the de novo synthesis of its precursors,
catharanthine and vindoline in Saccharomyces cerevisiae.5
Similarly, Reed et al. deciphered the biosynthetic pathway of
the vaccine adjuvant QS-21, which was later synthesized de
novo by Liu et al.6,7 Paclitaxel, well known for its high efficacy,
low toxicity, and broad-spectrum anticancer properties, has
garnered significant attention.8−10 The synthesis of paclitaxel is
a critical area of focus due to its persistent market demand and
the limitations of traditional synthesis methods (plant
extraction or chemical synthesis), which are both inefficient
and unsustainable.11−13 The isolation of intermediate 10-
deacetylbaccatin III or baccatin III from yew needles, followed
by chemical synthesis into paclitaxel, represents the most
commonly employed commercial route.14 However, this
method’s reliance on yew plants remains insufficient to meet

Paclitaxel biosynthesis initiates with the cyclization of
geranylgeranyl diphosphate (GGPP) into taxadiene (Tax)
(Figure 1), a reaction catalyzed by taxadiene synthase (TS).
GGPP is formed from two common building blocks,
isopentenyl diphosphate (IPP) and dimethylallyl diphosphate
(DMAPP), under the catalysis of geranylgeranyl diphosphate
synthase (GGPPS).17 IPP and DMAPP can be produced
through either the natural methylerythritol phosphate (MEP)
or mevalonate (MVA) pathways, or the artificially constructed
isopentenol utilization pathway (IUP), with isoprenyl
diphosphate isomerase (IDI) mediating the interconversion
between IPP and DMAPP.18−20 In the early stage paclitaxel
biosynthesis pathway, Tax, as the core and hydrophobic
skeleton of paclitaxel, is converted by taxadiene-5α-hydroxylase
(T5αOH), taxadien-5α-ol O-acetyltransferase (TAT), and
taxane-10β-hydroxylase (T10βOH) in sequence, forming
taxadien-5α-ol (T5OH), taxadien-5α-yl-acetate (T5OAc),
and taxadiene-5α,10β-diol-5-acetate (T10OH).21 The key
enzymes responsible for the conversion of T10OH to paclitaxel
have been identified; however, the detailed synthetic sequence
still requires further elucidation. Moreover, paclitaxel biosyn-
thesis is not strictly dependent on T10OH, as Tax and T5OH
can also be converted to paclitaxel through alternate
routes.22−24
Hence, the construction of highly efficient recombinant
strains capable of producing early stage paclitaxel intermediates
is of particular importance for both paclitaxel synthesis and
pathway elucidation. Currently, research on paclitaxel biosyn-
thesis has predominantly been focused on early precursors
including Tax, T5OH, T5OAc, and T10OH, with the highest
recorded yields of approximately 1020, 135, 95, and 32 mg/L,
respectively.25−27 Excitingly, recent efforts by Jiang et al. have
identified the missing pathway enzymes, taxane oxetanase
(TOT) and taxane-9α-hydroxylase (T9αOH), enabling the
synthesis of baccatin III in Nicotiana benthamiana (N.
benthamiana).28 Additionally, some analogous studies have
discovered different sequences encoding TOT and T9αOH,
yet the order of formation for paclitaxel intermediates remains
unknown.22,24,29−31 Furthermore, the biosynthesis of baccatin
III has been achieved in the plant N. benthamiana, although the
yield remains very low28 potentially due to the insufficient
precursor supply and/or the poor multienzyme coregulation.
Escherichia coli (E. coli), with a well-characterized genetic
background, comprehensive metabolic tools, and the highest
reported production of Tax,25 serves as an excellent host for
the biosynthesis of paclitaxel and its intermediates. In this
study, we utilized E. coli BL21(DE3) as the host strain for
constructing the early stage paclitaxel biosynthesis pathway. To
address the aforementioned issues, we initially adopted
directed evolution to obtain IDI and GGPPS variants with
enhanced catalytic performance and a finely regulated MEP
pathway and improved the Tax synthesis via copy number
optimization. Subsequently, protein scaffolds were introduced
to form a substrate channeling effect, further increasing Tax
production. An online prediction tool was used to obtain
highly efficient variants of TAT, significantly alleviating T5OH
accumulation. Promoter engineering, coupled with an
enhanced acetyl-CoA supply, was employed to regulate
T5OAc production. Finally, cofactor engineering was applied
to increase T10OH production, reaching 18.5 mg/L in a 5 L
bioreactor, representing the highest titer reported for
heterologous biosynthesis of T10OH in E. coli. The resulting

■MATERIALS AND METHODS
Strains and Genes
E. coli TOP10 and BL21(DE3) (Novagen, Germany) were used to
construct recombinant plasmids and cultivate production strains,
respectively. The strains and genes used in this study are listed in
Tables S1 and S2, respectively. Specifically, 1-deoxy-D-xylulose-5-
phosphate synthase (DXS) and IDI were cloned from E. coli
BL21(DE3). GGPPS, TS, T5αOH, CPR, TAT, and T10βOH were
stored in our laboratory.32,33 The protein scaffolds, reporter gene
mCherry, and interenzyme SEGLinker were all codon-optimized for
E. coli and chemically synthesized by GenScript (Nanjing, China).
Detailed sequence information is provided in Table S3.
Plasmids and DNA Manipulation
All plasmids applied and constructed in this study are listed in Table
S4. All primers were synthesized by Tsingke (Shanghai, China), and
nucleic acid sequence information is listed in Table S5. The
recombinant plasmids were constructed by a ClonExpress Ultra
One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme, China), and detailed construction
steps are described in Table S5. Multicopy integration based on
transposases was used for gene editing.34−36
Culture Conditions
Gene-edited engineered strains were cultivated according to
previously reported methods.34−36 For recombinant vector con-
struction, the seamless ligation mixture was transformed into E. coli
TOP10 competent cells via heat shock. Transformed cells were
cultured on LB agar plates at 37 °C for 12−14 h. A single colony was
randomly selected and inoculated into a test tube containing an LB
medium (10 g/L tryptone, 5 g/L yeast extract, 10 g/L sodium
chloride), followed by incubation at 37 °C/200 rpm shaking for 10−
12 h. Cultures were subsequently used for sequencing and plasmid
extraction. Antibiotics were supplemented as required during
cultivation (ampicillin: 100 mg/L, kanamycin: 50 mg/L, streptomy-
cin: 100 mg/L, and chloramphenicol: 34 mg/L).
For the construction of plasmid-carrying engineered strains, the
recombinant plasmids were transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3) via a
heat shock method and cultured at 37 °C for 12−14 h. A single
colony was subsequently inoculated into an LB tube containing
appropriate antibiotics and incubated at 37 °C/200 rpm shaking for
10−14 h. The cultures were transferred at 1% inoculum into 50 mL of
a TB2 medium (12 g/L tryptone, 24 g/L yeast extract, 20 g/L
glycerol, 2.31 g/L KH2PO4, and 12.54 g/L K2HPO4) in shake flasks.
When the OD600 reached 0.6−0.8, specific inducers were added to
initiate paclitaxel precursor synthesis, including 0.1 mM isopropyl-β-
D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) and 0.2 mM δ-aminolevulinic acid
(ALA). During optimization of inducer concentrations, the levels of
IPTG and arabinose (Ara) were adjusted accordingly, while the
concentration of ALA was maintained constant. Antibiotics were
added throughout the cultivation. For fed-batch fermentation in a 5 L
jar bioreactor, the optimal strain T67 glycerol stock was activated on
chloramphenicol- and kanamycin-containing agar plates at 37 °C for
12−16 h. Single colony was inoculated into an LB medium tube and
cultivated at 37 °C/200 rpm shaking for 10−14 h. Cultures were
transferred at 1% inoculum into LB medium shake flasks and grown
for 4−5 h under identical conditions, followed by 10% inoculation
into a 5 L jar bioreactor containing 2.5 L of TB2 medium. Initial
agitation was set at 200 rpm with dissolved oxygen (DO) controlled
at 30% saturation through the automatic adjustment of the agitation
rate. The pH was maintained at 7.0 using an aqueous solution of 30%
ammonia or 20% sulfuric acid. When the OD600 reached 30, IPTG
and Ara were added to induce protein expression. A 500 g/L glycerol
solution was supplemented to maintain the carbon source
concentration at 20 g/L when it was depleted. Fermentation
parameters, including microbial growth, product formation, and
glycerol concentration, were periodically monitored throughout the
fermentation process.

4 °C for 5 min. The upper n-dodecane phase was collected and mixed
with an equal volume of ethyl acetate containing 10 mg/L n-

Purification and In Vitro Activity Assay of TAT
TAT was purified using nickel-column affinity chromatography. Cells
harvested after 24 h of growth were centrifuged at 4 °C and 12,000×g
for 3 min. The cell pellet was washed with Buffer A and then
disrupted by sonication for 15 min (4 s pulse and 6 s pause). The
lysate was centrifuged at 4 °C and 12,000×g for 45 min, and the
supernatant was collected for purification. After the supernatant was
loaded onto the nickel column, elution was performed sequentially
with 5 column volumes each of Buffer A, 2% Buffer B, 5% Buffer B,
10% Buffer B, and 20% Buffer B. The collected elution fractions were
then concentrated. Buffer A, 200 mM KPB buffer, 500 mM NaCl, pH
7.5; Buffer B, 200 mM KPB buffer, 500 mM imidazole, 500 mM
NaCl.
In Vitro Reaction System. 0.25 mM T5OH, 80 μL of cell-free
extract, 0.25 mM acetyl-CoA, 1 mM MgCl2, 1 mM ATP, and Buffer A
were added to a final volume of 500 μL. The reaction was carried out
for 1 h at 30 °C with shaking at 1000 rpm.
■RESULTS
Optimization of Tax Production by the Engineering MEP
Pathway and Its Key Enzymes
To achieve the de novo synthesis of Tax in E. coli, two
recombinant plasmids pET21a-DXS-IDI and pACYCDuet-1-
TS-GGPPS were constructed (Figure 2a and Table S4). The
plasmid pET21a-DXS-IDI was employed to enhance the
precursor supply by expressing the key MEP pathway enzymes
DXS and IDI, while plasmid pACYCDuet-1-TS-GGPPS was
used to express the heterologous enzymes (TS and GGPPS)
for Tax synthesis. The resultant two recombinant plasmids
were cotransformed into E. coli, giving a recombinant strain
designated as T11 (Table S1). After 72 h of shake-flask
cultivation, a Tax titer of 12.7 mg/L was achieved (Figure 2b).
GGPP, the direct precursor of Tax, is essential for the efficient
production of Tax. The synthesis of 1 mol of GGPP requires 3
mol of IPP and 1 mol of DMAPP, while IDI mediates the
necessary interconversion between IPP and DMAPP (Figure
2a). Enhancing the catalytic efficiency of IDI through protein
engineering can accelerate this interconversion and thereby
promote the GGPP synthesis. Therefore, it is a promising
strategy to improve Tax production through molecular
engineering of IDI.
In a study by Siedentop et al.,38 IDI variants (IDIR51K,
IDIK55A, and IDIR83K) were found to enhance the synthesis of
farnesyl pyrophosphate (FPP), the direct precursor of
sesquiterpenes. We also tested these three IDI variants (Figure
2a), giving different results in that IDIR83K increased the
production of Tax to 20.2 mg/L, whereas IDIR51K and IDIK55A
did not enhance the Tax titer at all (Figure 2b). The expression
level of the MEP pathway enzymes has a significant impact on
the titer of terpenoids. Thus, a refined expression regulation
strategy for the MEP pathway was implemented to enhance
the production of Tax (Figures 1a and 2a). DXS and IDIR83K
were integrated into multicopy site insertion sequence 3 (IS3).
Through screening, we obtained recombinant strains of T15,
T16, and T17, harboring 1, 3, and 4 copies of DXS-IDIR83K,
respectively, along with plasmid pACYCDuet-1-TS-GGPPS
(Table S1). It was shown that the Tax titers were all improved
with strains T15, T16, and T17; however, the highest titer (up
to 46.2 mg/L) was achieved when a single copy of DXS-
IDIR83K was integrated (Figure 2b).
Directed evolution of GGPPS was successfully applied to
enhance the production of diterpenoids. For example, in the
study by Leonard et al. on the biosynthesis of levopimaradiene,
two single-point mutants of Taxus cuspidata GGPPS
(TcGGPPS), namely, S142C and G198D, significantly
increased the levopimaradiene production.39 Additionally, in
another study by Dong et al. on the biosynthesis of lycopene, a
more efficient pentamutant of N. benthamiana GGPPS
(NtGGPPS) was developed.40 Furthermore, a comparison
between the protein sequences TcGGPPS and NtGGPPS
revealed that four residues (V154A, I161L, R209S, and F218Y)
might be mutated in TcGGPPS (Figure 2c). We introduced
these mutations into TcGGPPS and combined them with the
single-point mutants S142C and G198D, giving six variants for
the performance tests. It was indicated that mutations V154A,
R209S, and F218Y were beneficial for improving the Tax
production, and the corresponding recombinant strains T23,
T25, and T26 achieved Tax titers of 56.7, 59.6, and 55.3 mg/L,
respectively (Table S1 and Figure 2d). Subsequently, the
advantageous single-point mutants were combined to create
three double-point mutants. Regrettably, these modifications
did not lead to any further increase in the Tax titer (Figure
2d). AlphaFold3 was employed to model the template and
mutants of TcGGPPS, followed by molecular docking using
AutoDock Vina. As shown in Figure S1a, the mutation sites are
distributed as follows: S142C, G198D, and R209S are located
on the protein surface, while V154A, I161L, and F218Y are
situated near the active pocket. The V154A mutation reduced
the side-chain volume, potentially facilitating substrate binding
(Figure S1b). The F218Y mutation introduced an additional
hydrogen bond with IPP, which may help stabilize IPP in the
correct catalytic position (Figure S1c). Although R209S is
located on the protein surface, it may enhance the catalytic
performance by improving protein stability or exerting
allosteric effects on enzymatic activity.
Constructing a Substrate Channel to Further Enhance Tax
Production
In E. coli, heterologously introduced enzymes are randomly
distributed. By introducing protein scaffolds to construct a
substrate channeling effect, the substrate diffusion distances
between target enzymes can be reduced, thereby increasing the
reaction rate of multienzyme catalysis. Rat GTPase-binding
domains (GBDs), mouse Src homology 3 (SH3) domains, and
the PSD95/DlgA/Zo-1 (PDZ) domain from the adaptor
protein syntrophin were used to construct a protein scaffold
and applied to enhance the synthesis of various terpenoid
compounds.41,42 The principle is as follows: GBD, SH3, and
PDZ are expressed as fusion proteins via linkers to form the
scaffold. Corresponding ligands (GBDLigand, SH3Ligand, and
PDZLigand) are then appended to the target enzymes, enabling
the enzymes to interact with GBD, SH3, and PDZ through

our study, the synthesis of Tax involved the overexpression of
four enzymes: DXS, IDI, GGPPS, and TS. DXS, as the first

metabolic flux. IDI, the final enzyme in the MEP pathway,
mediates the interconversion between IPP and DMAPP,
serving as a key link for downstream terpenoid biosynthesis.
Moreover, IDI, GGPPS, and TS catalyze three consecutive
reactions, converting IPP/DMAPP into Tax. Given their
sequential catalytic roles and critical position in the pathway,
IDI, GGPPS, and TS represent the optimal candidates for
constructing a substrate channel (Figures 1b and 3a). In
addition, we altered the quantities of these protein scaffolds to
modulate the local concentrations of IDI, GGPPS, and TS,
thereby influencing the titer of Tax (Figure 3b). It was evident
that the introduction of protein scaffolds resulted in a decrease
in Tax titer for recombinant strains T32, T33, T34, T35, T37,
and T38 (Table S1 and Figure 3b). However, their specific
titers were not significantly different compared to those of the
control strain T11. Similarly, strain T31, which lacks protein
scaffold ligands, also exhibited a reduced Tax titer compared to
T11, with no significant difference in specific titer (Figure 3b).
This reduction might be attributed to the additional metabolic
burden caused by the introduction of protein scaffold plasmids.
Fortunately, strain T36 showed a 20% increase in Tax titer
compared to T11, reaching 15.3 mg/L, with improvement of
the specific titer from 1.0 mg/g DCW to 2.5 mg/g DCW
(Figure 3b). Furthermore, both the titer and specific titer of
strain T36 were significantly higher than those of T31,
indicating the successful construction of a substrate channeling
effect (Figure 3a,b). In vitro pull-down assays further validated
the formation of the substrate channel. The cell-free extract
containing 6His-GBD-SH3-PDZ was bound to a nickel
column via affinity chromatography. Subsequently, the cell-
free extract coexpressing TS-PDZLigand and GGPPS-SH3Ligand
was passed through the nickel column. Through protein−
protein interactions, TS and GGPPS were pulled down
together. Finally, the bound proteins were eluted, as shown
in Figure S3. Distinct bands corresponding to 6His-GBD-SH3-
PDZ (28.8 kDa), GGPPS-SH3Ligand (36.1 kDa), and TS-
PDZLigand (95.5 kDa) were clearly observed in the elution
fraction. It should be noted that due to the relatively poor
expression of IDI-GBDLigand, we employed mCherry-GBDLigand
(38.6 kDa) as a substitute for its interaction with the GBD
domain (Figure S4).
To reduce the potential metabolic burden introduced by the
protein scaffold plasmid, the protein scaffolds in strain T36
were integrated into the genome of E. coli. Through screening,
we obtained recombinant strains T361 and T362, harboring 3
and 4 copies of PBAD-GBD-SH3-SH3-SH3-SH3-PDZ, respec-
tively, along with plasmid pET21a-DXS-IDI-GDBLigand and
pACYCDuet-1-TS-PDZLigand-GGPPS-SH3Ligand (Table S1).
Recombinant strains T361 and T362 showed a notable

23.9 mg/L, respectively (Figure 3c). The ratio of target

result, strain T362, which controlled the protein scaffold

expression with IPTG, was used for orthogonal experimental
optimization. Among them, the concentration gradients of Ara
and IPTG are 0.3, 0.6, and 1.2 mM and 0.025, 0.05, and 0.1
mM, respectively. The Tax titer reached 43.0 mg/L under a

Optimizing Tax Hydroxylation/Acetylation Efficiencies by
Promoter Engineering
Following the development of strain T44, additional strains
capable of hydroxylation and acetylation were successively
constructed. Consistent with previous research, the T5αOH
and its reductase partner (CPR) were fused, forming a
chimeric enzyme T5αOH-CPR. The recombinant strain T44
was utilized as the chassis strain for T5OH production, while
plasmid pACYCDuet-1 served as an expression vector for
T5αOH-CPR. Constitutive promoters (J23100, J23112, trxA,
and racR) and an inducible promoter (T7) were employed to
precisely regulate the T5αOH-CPR expression (Figures 1c and
4a). As the expression intensity increased gradually, the T5OH
production first rose and then declined. Notably, the T5OH
titer of the recombinant strain T54, which was regulated by the
racR promoter, reached a maximum of 17.9 mg/L (Figure 4a).
Meanwhile, because T5αOH is promiscuous, it generates
multiple byproducts, including 5(13)-oxa-3(11)-cyclotaxane
(iso-OCT), 5(12)-oxa-3(11)-cyclotaxane (OCT), and other
unknown compounds (Figure S5).

Protein Engineering of TAT to Enhance T5OAc Production
To enhance the catalytic performance of TAT, we employed a
protein engineering approach (Figure 1c). The PROSS online
server (https://pross.weizmann.ac.il/step/pross-terms/) was
used to computationally design TAT, identifying single-point
mutations that might improve the heterologous expression
level and the degree of stability. A total of 23 candidate single-
point mutants were selected for subsequent experimental
evaluation. The T5OAc titer of five single-point mutants was
significantly higher than that of the parental strain, with
specific mutations including T262V, A218P, V332K, P65V,
and F211Y (Figure 5a). We then mutated these residues to
each of the remaining 18 amino acids to investigate their
impact on T5OAc production. The results indicated that
single-point mutants P65T, P65M, P65C, P65S, P65K, T262I,
V332G, V332F, V332W, V332C, A218K, F211H, and F211N
enhanced T5OAc production to varying degrees (Figure 5b).
Subsequently, five beneficial single-point mutants (P65S,
F211Y, A218P, T262V, and V332K) were selected to construct
possible double-site mutants (Figure 5b). Among them, the
double mutant F211Y/A218P further enhanced the titer of
T5OAc, up to 6.3 mg/L (Strain T511) (Figure 5b). Molecular
modeling of TAT indicated that A218 is situated on a loop
near the entrance of the substrate channel. Mutation of this
residue to tyrosine induced conformational changes in the
substrate channel (Figure S2a,b), which may facilitate substrate
entry. Meanwhile, F211 is located on a surface-exposed α-helix,
and its mutation to proline may influence TAT activity by
enhancing structural stability or through allosteric effects
(Figure S2a). Furthermore, due to factors such as the poor
soluble expression and challenging purification of TAT (a
protein molecular weight of 49.1 kDa, as shown in Figure S7),
we validated the conversion of substrate T5OH by the wild-
type and the F211Y/A218P mutant using in vitro cell-free
extract assays. The result clearly demonstrated a significantly
higher conversion rate for mutant F211YA218P (Figure S8),
providing strong functional support for the observed in vivo
improvement.
Since the biosynthesis of T5OAc also requires acetyl-CoA as
a cosubstrate, we sought to enhance the intracellular levels of
acetyl-CoA to further improve T5OAc titer. To enhance flux
toward acetyl-CoA, we employed a metabolic engineering
strategy involving the overexpression of acetyl-CoA synthetase
(ACS) and the knockout of the acetate-forming pathway
enzymes, phosphate acetyltransferase (PTA) and acetate
kinase (ACKA) (Figure 1). As a result, this engineering
enhanced the acetyl-CoA yield by 49% in strain T512 relative
to T511, concomitantly raising the T5OAc titer from 6.3 to 7.6
mg/L (Figure 5c).

shown that omitting n-dodecane led to a higher T10OH

In contrast, when n-dodecane was added, T5OAc did not
accumulate at all while significant T5OH accumulation was

Tax were observed. Based on these findings, we hypothesized
that n-dodecane addition hindered T10OH formation

the newly generated T5OH out of the cells during
fermentation, preventing its further conversion. The substantial
accumulation of Tax may be resulted from the insufficient
catalytic activity of T5αOH.
Therefore, to drive Tax conversion, we implemented two
cofactor engineering strategies, aiming at increasing the
intracellular heme levels and regenerating NADPH. As the
prosthetic group and cofactor of P450 enzymes, respectively,
heme and NADPH play pivotal roles in their electron transfer
processes. In E. coli, there is a native heme biosynthesis
pathway in which glutamyl-tRNA reductase (HemA) and
glutamate-1-semialdehyde-2,1-aminomutase (HemL) are
widely investigated as rate-limiting enzymes for enhancing
the heme production.43−45 Within the glycolytic pathway,
glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (Zwf) catalyzes the
conversion of glucose-6-phosphate to gluconolactone-6-
phosphate, simultaneously reducing NADP+ to NADPH
(Figure 6b,c).46,47 Therefore, to increase the intracellular
heme levels and to regenerate NADPH effectively, we
randomly overexpressed varying copy numbers of HemA-
HemL and Zwf on the chromosomes of E. coli. After screening,
we identified strains harboring 1, 3, or 8 copies of HemA-
HemL and 1 or 2 copies of Zwf (Figure 6d). We subsequently
introduced the T10OH-producing plasmids into these strains,
yielding five recombinant strains, namely, T62, T63, T64, T65,
and T66 (Figure 6d). As the intracellular heme levels
increased, the T10OH titer first rose and then declined,
reaching a maximum of 5.2 mg/L in strain T62 (Figure 6e).
However, we observed compromised cell growth likely due to
the heme toxicity at higher concentrations. In strains
overexpressing Zwf, both T65 and T66 showed moderate
increases in T10OH titer, up to 3.4 and 4.2 mg/L, respectively
(Figure 6e,f). We then combined both the strategies to
construct a new recombinant strain, T67, which overexpresses
one copy of HemA-HemL and two copies of Zwf. The T10OH
titer by strain T67 was further increased to 8.7 mg/L (Figure
6e). Compared with T61, the precursor Tax formed in T67
decreased by 68% (Figure 6e), while intermediate T5OAc was
nearly undetectable, indicating that cofactor engineering did
improve the T10OH biosynthesis substantially.
Proof of Strain T67 Performance by Fed-Batch
Fermentation in a 5 L Bioreactor
After attaining the optimal T10OH-producing strain T67, we
conducted fed-batch fermentation in a 5 L jar bioreactor. To
facilitate the bioprocess scale-up, the agitation speed was
correlated with the dissolved oxygen (DO) level monitored,
maintaining a DO around approximately 30% saturation. The
glycerol concentration remaining in the culture was regularly
monitored off-line and replenished to 20 g/L whenever
necessary. Consequently, following a 7 h induction, Tax and
T5OH began to form, with T10OH production commencing
at 18 h. The accumulation of T5OAc was minimal and nearly
undetectable in the later stages. This may be attributed to the
enhanced catalytic efficiency of T10βOH under high-agitation
culture conditions, which facilitates the conversion of T5OAc
to T10OH more readily. The T10OH titer reached a
maximum of 18.5 mg/L at 65 h (Figure 7), to our knowledge,
representing the highest record in E. coli reported to date.
■DISCUSSION
In this study, we established an early stage paclitaxel
biosynthetic pathway in E. coli, proceeding from the paclitaxel
backbone (Tax) to the double-oxidation product T10OH. We
employed a variety of metabolic and protein engineering
strategies to improve the Tax production efficiency, thereby
laying a robust foundation for subsequent oxidation and
acetylation steps. Initially, we coexpressed the key enzymes of
the MEP pathway, DXS and IDI, together with GGPPS and TS
on plasmids, resulting in an initial strain that produced 12.7
mg/L of Tax. De novo terpenoid biosynthesis typically suffers
from flux imbalance and suboptimal enzyme perform-
ance.18,48,49 To address these issues, we optimized the
expression of MEP pathway genes and improved the catalytic
activities of IDI and GGPPS, achieving a 4.7-fold increase in
Tax titer to 59.6 mg/L. The MEP pathway optimization is
commonly implemented in terpenoid production50−52 and
thus will not be overly detailed here.
Efforts to improve terpenoid titers often involve exploring
different enzyme sources. However, relatively few reports have
focused on engineering individual enzymes directly. In our
work, we identified a beneficial single-point mutant, IDIR83K,
which enhanced Tax production by approximately 59%,
underscoring the critical importance of efficient IPP/DMAPP
interconversion in terpenoid biosynthesis. We also used
sequence alignment to improve the catalytic performance of
another enzyme, GGPPS. Specifically, the single-point mutant
GGPPSR209S was proved to be effective in boosting Tax titers,
and we anticipate that this mutation could facilitate the
production of other diterpenoids as well. This mutant was
postulated to enhance catalytic performance by improving
stability or through allosteric effects, which was consistent with
the findings reported by Dong et al. in their study on
NtGGPPS.40 One noteworthy aspect of this study is the
engineering of enzyme components, a strategy that is not
widely employed in de novo terpenoid biosynthesis. Meanwhile,
a protein-scaffolding strategy was employed to establish

Promoter engineering was then applied to the biosynthesis
of T5OH and T5OAc, resulting in recombinant strains capable
of producing 17.9 mg/L T5OH and 3.3 mg/L T5OAc,
respectively. It is worth mentioning that a recent study had
found FoTO1 can enhance the titer of T5OH.53 In future
work, coexpression of this enzyme could be attempted to
further improve T5OH production in E. coli. Then, to address
the issue of excessive T5OH accumulation, the online PROSS
server was used to improve the thermal stability and protein
expression of TAT. Ultimately, the double mutant F211Y/
A218P effectively alleviated the accumulation of T5OH and
enhanced the T5OAc titer by 1.9-fold, reaching 6.3 mg/L. In
addition, by enhancing the supply of intracellular acetyl-CoA,
the titer of T5OAc can be further increased up to 7.6 mg/L.
This work represents the first protein engineering effort
targeting TAT, although further improvements remain
possible.
In the T10OH biosynthesis, different cultivation strategies
influenced the distribution of products, and the removal of
traditional n-dodecane coverage (biphasic cultivation) proved
more favorable for T10OH production, which is consistent
with the earlier findings of Zhang et al.33 Previous studies on
the biosynthesis of paclitaxel intermediates have been mainly
focused on screening and expressing enzyme components,
balancing the overall pathway, and optimizing the cultivation
conditions.54−56 Although Tax accumulation has frequently
been observed, it has not yet been addressed effectively.
Herein, we employed cofactor engineering strategies to
enhance the catalytic performance of P450 enzymes and
thereby promoted Tax bioconversion. As a result, the
optimized strain T67 exhibited a 68% reduction in Tax
accumulation, with T10OH titers reaching 8.7 mg/L in a
shake-flask and 18.5 mg/L in a bioreactor.
In summary, by integrating various metabolic and protein
engineering strategies, we succeeded in constructing an
optimized early stage paclitaxel biosynthesis pathway. Because
the paclitaxel biosynthesis pathway is both complex and
nonlinear, the high-efficiency intermediate-producing strains
developed in this study are critical for exploring downstream
steps and facilitating the efficient production of paclitaxel.
Additionally, the efficient enzyme mutants and engineering
strategies described here provide valuable references for the
biosynthesis of paclitaxel and other terpenoids.
