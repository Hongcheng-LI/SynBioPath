Depside Bond Formation by the Starter-Unit Acyltransferase
Domain of a Fungal Polyketide Synthase

ABSTRACT: Depsides are polyphenolic molecules comprising two or more phenolic acid derivatives linked by an ester bond,
which is called a depside bond in these molecules. Despite more than a century of intensive research on depsides, the biosynthetic
mechanism of depside bond formation remains unclear. In this study, we discovered a polyketide synthase, DrcA, from the fungus
Aspergillus duricaulis CBS 481.65 and found that DrcA synthesizes CJ-20,557 (1), a heterodimeric depside composed of 3-
methylorsellinic acid and 3,5-dimethylorsellinic acid. Moreover, we determined that depside bond formation is catalyzed by the
starter-unit acyltransferase (SAT) domain of DrcA. Remarkably, this is a previously undescribed form of SAT domain chemistry.
Further investigation revealed that 1 is transformed into duricamidepside (2), a depside−amino acid conjugate, by the single-module
nonribosomal peptide synthetase DrcB.
T
he term “depside” was introduced by Emil Fischer and
Karl Freudenberg in 1910 and denotes a molecule
consisting of two or more phenolic acid derivatives connected
by an ester bond, which is called a depside bond in such a
molecule (Figure 1A).1 Depside natural products are primarily
found in lichens but are also found in higher plants and fungi.2
They have attracted scientists’ attention for over a century, as
they are structurally diverse molecules with a broad range of
biological activities and thus are good targets for chemical
synthesis. The most structurally simple depsides are lecanoric
acid and 4-O-demethylbarbatic acid, which are homodimers of
orsellinic acid and 3-methylorsellinic acid (3-MOA), respec-
tively. Some depsides, such as olivetoric acid, feature
orsellinate-derived units bearing an alkyl chain. Both dimeric
(didepsides) and trimeric depsides (tridepsides) have been
isolated.3 The core structures of depsides feature diverse
moieties due to modifications such as methylation,3c
glycosylation,4 and amino acid incorporation.5
Depside biosynthesis has been investigated intensively for
many decades. The first hypothesis on depside biosynthesis
was published in 1944,6 and the first experimental studies
emerged in the 1960s.7 In recent years, several nonreducing
(NR)-polyketide synthases (PKSs) responsible for depside
synthesis have been identified and characterized (Figure S1),
and studies have indicated that these PKSs are responsible for
both aromatic unit synthesis and depside bond formation.8 A
recent report described the in-depth characterization of
AN7909, a depside-forming PKS whose major product is
diaryl ether diorcinolic acid and whose minor products are
lecanoric acid and orsellinic acid.9 Intriguingly, it was found
that the formation of the depside bond and diaryl ether linkage
are both catalyzed by the thioesterase (TE) domain of
AN7909 (Figure 1B). However, it is unclear whether other
PKSs that synthesize a depside as a major product are capable
of TE-mediated formation of depside bonds. In addition, the

During our recent investigation of the genome of the fungus
Aspergillus duricaulis CBS 481.65, in which we identified the
biosynthetic gene cluster of setosusin,10 we found that the
fungus encodes an unusual NR-PKS that features three
consecutive acyl carrier protein (ACP) domains within its
domain architecture, which is starter-unit acyltransferase−
ketosynthase−acyltransferase−product template−ACP−
ACP−ACP−C-methyltransferase−TE (SAT−KS−AT−PT−
ACP−ACP−ACP−MT−TE) (Figure 2A). We designated
this PKS DrcA and compared its sequence with those of
known PKSs. This revealed that DrcA was most homologous
to Atr1,8f which synthesizes the 3-MOA dimer 4-O-
demethylbarbatic acid, although Atr1 possesses only one
ACP domain. As such, we considered that DrcA might
synthesize a previously unknown depside. We also found that
the flanking region of drcA encodes a single-module non-
ribosomal peptide synthetase (NRPS), DrcB, which has an
adenylation−thiolation−terminal condensation (A−T−CT)
domain configuration (Figure 2A and Table S1).
To obtain the metabolites derived from the drc cluster, we
heterologously expressed drc genes in the Aspergillus oryzae
NSAR1 strain,11 which has been widely used to characterize
orphan fungal biosynthetic gene clusters.12 The metabolites
produced byA. oryzae transformed with drcA were analyzed by
HPLC, which revealed the presence of a major metabolite, 1,
that was absent from the host strain (Figure 2B, traces i and ii).
We performed large-scale cultivation of the drcA-expressing
transformant and successfully isolated 1, whose molecular
formula was established as C19H20O7 by HR-MS analysis.
Subsequent NMR analysis of 1 indicated the presence of two
aromatic rings, which appeared to be derived from 3-MOA and
3,5-dimethylorsellinic acid (DMOA), suggesting that 1 is a
heterodimer of 3-MOA and DMOA connected via an ester
linkage. A single crystal of 1 was obtained for X-ray diffraction
analysis, which confirmed that it contained an ester formed
from the carboxy group of 3-MOA and the C-4 hydroxy group
of DMOA (Figure 2C and D; CCDC 2143551). The structure
of 1 is identical to that of CJ-20,557, which was obtained as a
partial hydrolysis product of thielavin A,3c although 1 had not
been isolated as a natural product. Subsequently, drcB was
coexpressed with drcA, which led to the production of a new
metabolite, 2 (Figure 2B, trace iii). NMR analysis revealed the
planar structure of 2, in which threonine is linked to 1 by an
amide bond. The absolute stereochemistry of 2 was established
to be 2″S, 3″R using Marfey’s method13 (Figure 2C; see
Supporting Information for the detailed structural determi-
nation), and 2 was named duricamidepside.
We next sought to elucidate the biosynthesis of 1,
particularly the mechanism of depside bond formation.
Following a recent study on AN7909,9 we initially hypothe-
sized that the TE domain of DrcA is responsible for ester bond
formation via 3-methylorsellinyl- and 3,5-dimethylorsellinyl-
ACPs and for the subsequent chain hydrolysis required to
release 1. To investigate this hypothesis, we synthesized the N-
acetylcysteamine (NAC) thioesters of 3-MOA and DMOA,
respectively, as surrogates of the ACP-bound reaction
intermediates (Figure 3C),14 and purified the TE domain of
DrcA from an Escherichia coli expression system. We then
reacted 3-methylorsellinyl-SNAC (3) and 3,5-dimethylorsel-
linyl-SNAC (4) with the TE domain in vitro; however, this
reaction did not yield a detectable amount of 1 (Figure 3A,
traces i and ii). We also found that incubation of the SNAC
derivative of 1 (5; Figure 3C) with the TE domain resulted in
efficient hydrolysis of the thioester of 5 to yield 1 (Figure 3A,
traces iii and iv), suggesting that the TE domain is only
responsible for chain hydrolysis and that another PKS domain
might catalyze the formation of the depside bond.
To identify the PKS domain involved in esterification, we
purified the starter-unit acyltransferase (SAT) domain and the
KS-AT didomain for use in enzymatic reactions in vitro.
Surprisingly, the SAT and TE domains transformed 3 and 4
into 1 (Figure 3A, trace v), whereas KS-AT had no apparent
role in this reaction (Figure 3A, trace vi), indicating that SAT
is the crucial PKS domain for formation of the depside bond.
Subsequently, we found that incubation of SAT with 3 and 4 in
the absence of TE produced 5, as expected, but also generated
four additional polymerized products, 6−9 (Figure 3B, traces i
and ii, and Figure S2). The molecular formulas of 6 and 7
correspond to that of a tridepside-SNAC with three 3-MOA
units or two 3-MOA and one DMOA units, respectively,
whereas 8 and 9 appear to be tetradepside-SNACs with four 3-
MOA units or three 3-MOA and one DMOA units,
respectively (Figure 3C). We also found that 6, 7, and 9
were produced by the reaction of SAT with 3 and 5 (Figure

We then focused on identifying the amino acid residues in
the SAT domain that are required for depside bond formation.
The active site of the SAT domain contains the cysteine/
serine−histidine dyad,15 and these residues are also conserved
in the SAT of DrcA as Cys141 and His255. To clarify the
importance of these two residues, we created three variants of
SAT: C141A, C141S, and H255A. All three variants were
found to be inactive (Figure 3B, traces vii to ix, and Figure S2),
indicating that Cys141 and His255 are the catalytic residues of
the SAT domain.
Finally, we sought to clarify the importance of the three
consecutive ACP domains of DrcA, as such triplet ACPs have
been only found in a few PKSs, such as MmpB and CurA
involved in the biosynthesis of mupirocin and curacin,
respectively.16 The serine residues as the phosphopantetheine
attachment site in each ACP (Ser1688, Ser1795, and Ser1893)
were individually mutated to alanine, and the three DrcA
variants were heterologously expressed in A. oryzae. Con-
sequently, all three variants exhibited comparable activity to
synthesize 1 (Figure S3). We then evaluated three additional
variants in which two of the three serine residues are mutated
to alanine. Although these three variants all displayed
somewhat reduced activity, 1 was still obviously detected
(Figure S3). These observations indicate that only one ACP is
sufficient to synthesize 1, which is consistent with the fact that
Atr1, the PKS synthesizing the 3-MOA homodimer, possesses
only one ACP domain (Figure S1).8f
We hypothesize that the mechanism of SAT domain-
catalyzed depside bond formation is as follows (Figure 4A).
First, the 3-methylorsellinyl group on ACP is transferred to the
catalytic cysteine of SAT, Cys141. SAT might also react in an
intermolecular fashion with ACP, especially when 3-methyl-
orsellinyl-ACP is not available intramolecularly. The ACP-
bound dimethylorsellinyl moiety then approaches the active

The SAT domain was first identified in 2006 in PksA, which
is responsible for aflatoxin biosynthesis.15 Since then, several
SAT domains of fungal NR-PKSs have been studied in detail.17
It is known that the SAT domain is responsible for loading the
priming substrateeither acetyl-CoA or a polyketide/fatty
acid chain synthesized by another PKS or fatty acid synthase
complexfor polyketide chain extension.18 The SAT domain
of DrcA is clearly distinct from previously described SAT
domains, as it is responsible for ester bond formation rather
than starter-unit loading. This finding increases the under-
standing of the reactions catalyzed by fungal PKS domains.
Moreover, phylogenetic analysis revealed that the SAT domain
of DrcA is most closely related to that of Atr1 and is only
distantly related to those of other depside-forming PKSs
(Figure S4). This suggests that PKSs might form depside
bonds via various mechanisms, even when generating a depside
final product.
Based on our experimental results, we devised a possible
pathway for the biosynthesis of 2 (Figure 4B). First, the NR-
PKS DrcA synthesizes 1, which is then activated by the A
domain of DrcB and loaded onto the T domain. The CT
domain of DrcB then catalyzes the formation of an amide bond
between the enzyme-loaded 1 and L-threonine to yield 2, a
depside−threonine hybrid. This possible pathway is supported
by the fact that an in vitro enzymatic reaction of purified DrcB
with 1,
L-threonine, adenosine triphosphate (ATP), and
magnesium chloride afforded 2 (Figure S5). Although single-
module NRPSs with an A−T−CT domain configuration have
been identified in fungal natural product pathways,19 to the
best of our knowledge, the biosynthesis of 2 is the first example
of a pathway in which a single-module NRPS is coupled with
an NR-PKS.
In summary, we discovered and characterized a fungal PKS,
DrcA, that synthesizes 1, a heterodimeric depside. We recently
reported unusual fungal type I PKSs that synthesize two
different products for heterodimeric polyketide generation;20
however, DrcA is distinct from the previous cases in that it is
self-sufficient to form a heterodimeric product without the aid
of trans-acting enzymes. Importantly, we elucidated that the
SAT domain of DrcA is involved in depside bond formation,
which is unprecedented SAT domain chemistry. Although
another fungal PKS, AN7909, was recently found to catalyze
depside bond formation,9 DrcA uses a unique strategy to
create a depside bond. Our finding suggests that mining and
characterizing unexploited fungal NR-PKSs may reveal addi-
tional SAT domains that are responsible for unusual chemical
transformations.
