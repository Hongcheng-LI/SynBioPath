21   Abstract


22  A new type III polyketide synthase gene (Ssars) was discovered from the genome of

23    Shiraia sp. Slf14, an endophytic fungal strain from Huperzia serrata. The intron-free

24   gene was cloned from the cDNA and ligated to two expression vectors pET28a and

25  YEpADH2p-URA3 for expression in Escherichia coli BL21(DE3) and Saccharomyces

26    cerevisiae BJ5464, respectively. SsARS was efficiently expressed in E. coli BL21(DE3),

27    leading to the synthesis of a series of polyketide products. Six major products were

28    isolated from the engineered E. coli and characterized as 1,3-dihydroxyphenyl-5-

29   undecane,   1,3-dihydroxyphenyl-5-cis-6'-tridecene,1,3-dihydroxyphenyl-5-tridecane,

30    1,3-dihydroxyphenyl-5-cis-8'-pentadecene,  1,3-dihydroxyphenyl-5-pentadecane and

31    1,3-dihydroxyphenyl-5-cis-10'-heptadecene, respectively, based on the spectral data

32   and biosynthetic origin. Expression of SsARS in the yeast also led to the synthesis of

33    the same polyketide products, indicating that this enzyme can be reconstituted in both

34    heterologous  hosts. Supplementation of soybean  oil  into the culture of E.  coli

35   BL21(DE3)/SsARS increased the production titers of 1-6 and led to the synthesis of an

36    additional product, which was identified as 5-(8'Z,11'Z-heptadecadienyl)resorcinol.

37   This work thus allowed the identification of SsARS as a 5-alk(en)ylresorcinol synthase

38   with flexible substrate specificity toward endogenous and exogenous fatty acids.

39   Desired resorcinol derivatives may be synthesized by supplying corresponding fatty

40    acids into the culture medium.

41   Keywords Shiraia sp. Slf14, type III polyketide synthase, akylresorcinols, substrate

42    flexibility, precursor-directed biosynthesis

43   Introduction

44   Fungi are known to produce a large variety of bioactive natural products, such as

45    lovastatin (anti-cholesterol) and penicillin (antibacterial). Polyketides are a large family

46    of  structurally  diverse  natural  products  including  erythromycin  (antibacterial),

47    tetracycline (antibacterial) and doxorubicin (antitumor). They are assembled from acyl-

48   coenzyme A precursors by polyketide synthases (PKSs), which are classified into three

49   groups based on sequences and functional mechanisms, including types I, II, and III.

50   Type I PKS, similar to type I fatty acid synthase (FAS), is a giant assembly of

51    multifunctional  polypeptides, each  consisting  of  a  series  of  catalytic domains

52   (Keatinge-Clay 2012). Representative bioactive compounds synthesized by type I PKSs

53    are lovastatin and erythromycin. Type  II PKSs are discrete enzymes  that work

54    collaboratively  to form aromatic  polyketide  products, such  as  tetracycline and

55   doxorubicin (Zhan 2009). Type  III PKSs are self-contained enzymes  that form

56   homodimers. These single enzymes can catalyze precursor priming, chain extension

57   and cyclization. Type III PKSs are involved in the biosynthesis of a wide array of

58    natural products such as chalcones, pyrones, acridones, phloroglucinols, stilbenes, and

59    resorcinolic lipids (Yu et al. 2012). All three types of PKS are present in bacteria and

60    type II PKSs are exclusively found in bacteria such as actinomycetes. Plants contain

61    various type III PKSs, while fungi are known to harbor type I and type III PKSs. These

62   PKSs are involved in the formation of various metabolites in these hosts.

63        Alk(en)ylresorcinols are phenolic lipids synthesized by type III PKSs. They are

64   commonly present in several plant families and have been proposed as specific dietary

                                       3


[PDF physical page 4]
65   biomarkers of whole-grain wheat and rye intake (Kyro et al. 2014; Landberg et al. 2008).

66   They were also found in fungi, algae, mosses, bacteria, and marine sponges (Landberg

67    et al. 2014). Many of natural alk(en)ylresorcinols have shown antimicrobial activities.

68   For example, DB-2017 is an alkylresorcinol antibiotic from Pseudomonas sp. B-9094.

69     It has shown antimicrobial activity against mycobacteria, Gram-positive bacteria, and

70    fungi (Kanda et al. 1975; Kitahara and Kanda 1975). Some resorcinol derivatives were

71    also found to possess cytotoxic, anticarcinogenic, antiproliferative, antileishmanial and

72    antioxidant properties (Luis et al. 2016).

73        Symbiotic microorganisms in plants live closely with the hosts and are found to

74   produce various valuable compounds. Shiraia sp. Slf14 is a promising endophytic

75   fungus from Huperzia serrata. This strain possesses the ability to produce hypocrellins

76   and huperzine A (Yang et al. 2014). As polyketide natural products, hypocrellins are a

77   group of molecules with a good prospect of clinical applications, and have been used

78    as an effective photosensitizer for photodynamic therapy (PDT) of tumors (Ali and

79   Olivo 2002; Ali et al. 2001; Estey et al. 1996). Huperzine A is a sesquiterpene alkaloid

80    originally isolated from H. serrata. It acts as a cholinesterase inhibitor and thus has

81    potential for use in the treatment of Alzheimer’s disease (Damar et al. 2017).

82       To better understand Shiraia sp. Slf14, we have sequenced the genome of this

83   fungus (Yang et al. 2014). Analysis of the sequence genome revealed a putative type III

84   PKS. Here we report the  identification of  this type  III PKS and heterologous

85    reconstitution of this enzyme in Escherichia coli and Saccharomyces cerevisiae. Six

86    alk(en)ylresorcinols were synthesized by this enzyme from malonyl-CoA and various

                                       4


[PDF physical page 5]
 87    fatty acyl starter units. An additional alkylresorcinol was generated by E. coli when

 88   soybean oil was supplemented into the medium.

 89   Materials and Methods

 90   Strains and media

 91  DNA manipulations were performed using standard techniques with E. coli XL1-Blue

 92    as a host organism. Expression vector pET28a was purchased from Novagen (Madison,

 93   WI, USA). E. coli BL21(DE3) purchased from Stratagene (La Jolla, CA, USA) was

 94   used as the expression host. The E. coli strains were routinely grown in LB medium.

 95    Shiraia sp. Slf14 (CCTCC No. M209294) was previously isolated from H. serrata and

 96   was grown in potato dextrose broth (PDB) medium (Zhu et al. 2010). S. cerevisiae

 97   BJ5464 (ATCC 208288) was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection and

 98    routinely grown in YPD medium.

 99   Construction of pET28a-Ssars (pHW4) and YEpADH2p-URA3-Ssars (pHW8)

100   Wild type Shiraia sp. Slf14 was grown in PDB for 3 days. The cells were harvested by

101    filtration, from which the RNA was extracted with TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen, USA).

102   The extracted RNA was purified with a Direct-zol RNA MiniPrep kit (Zymo Research,

103   CA, USA). The cDNA was  synthesized from mRNA with M-MuLV Reverse

104    Transcriptase (NEB, MA, USA). With the cDNA as a template, the 1,278-bp DNA

105    intron-free Ssars gene (GenBank accession no. MH048853) was amplified by PCR with

106   a      set     of     specific     primers     including    SsARS-F-NheI      (5'-

107   aaGCTAGCatgtcccacgtggctaacac-3', with a NheI  site shown by underlining) and

108   SsARS-R-BamHI (5'-aaGGATCCttaacctgttcgtacagtgt-3', with an BamHI site shown by

                                        5


[PDF physical page 6]
109    underlining). The resulting PCR product was cloned into the pJET1.2 cloning vector.

110    After sequencing, this gene subsequently cloned into the pET28a vector between the

111   NheI and BamHI sites, yielding pHW4. The same gene was amplified by PCR using

112    another primer set including the above mentioned SsARS-F-NheI and SsARS-R-PmeI

113   (5'-aaGTTTAAACttaacctgttcgtacagtgt-3', with a PmeI site shown by underlining). The

114    resulting PCR product was cloned into pJET1.2, and subsequently inserted into the

115    yeast expression vector YEpADH2p-URA3 between the NheI and PmeI sites, yielding

121    for 12 hrs. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 4,000 rpm for 10 min, resuspended

122    in the lysis buffer composed of 20 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.9) and 0.5 M NaCl, and

123    disrupted by sonication. A crude cell lysate was prepared by removal of cell debris by

124    centrifugation at 13,000 rpm at 4 °C for 15 min, which was then analyzed by SDS-

125   PAGE.

206   Results

207   Phylogenetic analysis of SsARS from Shiraia sp. Slf14

208   Annotation of the genome sequencing data of Shiraia sp. Slf14 led to the discovery of

209   a putative type III PKS SsARS in scaffold 13. BLAST analysis showed that SsARS is

210   homologous to a number of type III PKSs. A phylogenetic analysis of SsARS and nine

211   known type III PKSs from fungi, plant and bacteria revealed that SsARS falls within a

212    clade of type III PKSs, and it forms a separate subclade together with Neurospora

213   crassa ORAS (Funa et al. 2007) and Sporotrichum laxum Sl-PKS2 (Sun et al. 2016).

214   As shown in Fig. 1, the horizontal dimension gives the extent of genetic change, and it

215   was found that SsARS are more similar to Sl-PKS2 and ORAS. Both Sl-PKS2 and

216  ORAS were previously reported to synthesize fatty acid-primed resorcinols, suggesting

217    that SsARS  is likely an alk(en)ylresorcinol synthase (ARS). Sequence alignment

218    revealed that SsARS has 34% identity with N. crassa ORAS and 36% identity with S.

                                       10


[PDF physical page 11]
219   laxum Sl-PKS2. SsARS maintains an identical CoA binding site with the reported type

220     III PKSs and a conserved catalytic triad of Cys-153, His-308, and Asn-341 (numbering

221    in SsARS).

222   Heterologous expression of SsARS in E. coli BL21(DE3) and characterization of

223   the polyketide products

224   The 1,278-bp intron-free cDNA of Ssars was synthesized from mRNA through reverse

225    transcription. The gene was ligated to the expression vector pET28a to yield pHW4. E.

226    coli BL21(DE3) was transformed with pHW4 and cultured in LB broth and induced

227   with 200 μM IPTG. After incubated at 28℃ and 250 rpm condition overnight, the cells

228   were collected and lysed for SDS-PAGE analysis. Compared to E. coli BL21(DE3)

229   harboring the empty vector pET28a, a protein band at ∼47 kDa was observed (Fig. 2A),

230    suggesting that SsARS was successfully expressed in this heterologous host. We next

231   examined whether this enzyme can generate any polyketide products in E. coli. The

232    extracts of E. coli BL21(DE3)/pHW4 and E. coli BL21(DE3)/pET28a were analyzed

233   by HPLC. As shown in Fig. 2B, BL21(DE3)/pHW4 produced a series of nonpolar

234    products with retention times between 20 and 45 min (trace i), while the control with

235    the empty vector did not have the same peaks. These six major products were numbered

236    as 1-6. Therefore, through SDS-PAGE and HPLC analysis, we confirmed that SsARS

237   were functionally expressed in E. coli BL21(DE3).

238   Characterization of the six polyketide products from E. coli BL21(DE3)/pHW4

239   To structurally characterize the six products, these compounds were isolated from the

240    culture of E. coli BL21(DE3)/pHW4. Compounds 1-6 were obtained as yellowish oil.

                                         11


[PDF physical page 12]
241   These compounds were identified as alk(en)ylresorcinols with either an unsaturated or

242    saturated aliphatic side chain (Fig. 2B) based on their UV, MS and NMR spectra and a

243   comparison with previously published data (Suzuki et al. 1997 ). The molecular weights

244    of 1-6 were determined to be 264, 290, 292, 318, 320 and 346 based on their [M+H]+

245    ion peaks at m/z 265.2, 291.2, 293.2, 319.2, 321.4 and 347.3 in the ESI-MS (Fig. 3A),

246    respectively. Compounds 1, 3 and 5 were deduced to be alkylresorcinols with a

247    saturated side chain, while 2, 4 and 6 have an unsaturated side chain. The 1H NMR

248   spectrum of 1 revealed two aromatic proton signals at δH 6.26 (2H, d, J = 1.8 Hz, H-4

249   and H-6) and δH 6.22 (1H, t, J = 1.8 Hz, H-2), suggesting that a resorcinol moiety is

250    present in the structure. In addition, proton signals typical for a long aliphatic chain

251    including the benzylic methylene at δH 2.49 (2H, t, J = 7.5 Hz) and terminal methyl

252   group at δH 0.90 (3H, t, J = 6.9 Hz) were also found. Thus, 1 can be identified as 1,3-

253   dihydroxyphenyl-5-undecane (Fig. 3B). The NMR data of 3 and 5 are highly similar to

254    those of 1, except that they have two and four more aliphatic proton signals, respectively.

255   Accordingly,  3 was  identified  as  1,3-dihydroxyphenyl-5-tridecane  and  5 was

256    characterized as 1,3-dihydroxyphenyl-5-pentadecane (Fig. 3B).

257       There are two aromatic proton signals at δH 6.26 (2H, d, J = 1.8 Hz, H-4 and H-6)

258   and δH 6.20 (1H, t, J = 1.8 Hz, H-2) in the 1H NMR spectrum of 2, indicating that a

259    resorcinol moiety is also present in the structure. Similar to 1, proton signals for a long

260    aliphatic chain including the benzylic methylene at δH 2.51 (2H, t, J = 7.5 Hz) and

261    terminal methyl group at δH 0.92 (3H, t, J = 6.9 Hz) were also present. However, two

262   overlapping olefinic protons at δH 5.38-5.36 indicated that there is a double bond in the

                                       12


[PDF physical page 13]
263    side chain. Thus, 2 was characterized as 1,3-dihydroxyphenyl-5-cis-6'-tridecene (Fig.

264   3B). Similarly, 4 and 6 were identified as resorcinol derivatives containing an

265    unsaturated C15 and C17 side chain, named 1,3-dihydroxyphenyl-5-cis-8'-pentadecene

266   and 1,3-dihydroxyphenyl-5-cis-10'-heptadecene, respectively (Fig. 3B). Compounds 2,

267   4 and 6 were previously isolated and characterized by our group from an engineered E.

268    coli strain that expressed Sl-pks2 from the biosynthetic pathway in spirolaxine in

269   Sporotrichum laxum ATCC 15155 (Sun et al. 2016). Overall, the MS and 1H NMR data

270    of these known 5-alkenylresorcinols were consistent with our and others' reported data

271   (Knodler et al. 2008; Sun et al. 2016; Suzuki et al. 1997 ).

272    Effect of supplementation of exogenous fatty acids on the biosynthesis of 5-

273    alk(en)ylresorcinols

274   While the fatty acyl side chains of 1-6 were believed to be from the corresponding fatty

275    acyl starter units caught by SsARS from the biosynthetic or degradative pathways

276    within E. coli cells, we next tested how supplementation of exogenous fatty acids

277    affects the product profile in E. coli BL21(DE3)/pHW4. Soybean oil is known to

278    contain various fatty acids. To this end, 20 g/L soybean oil was supplied into the culture

279   medium. The extract was analyzed by HPLC, and a new product 7 at 33.2 min was

280   observed (Fig. 4A). This compound has a typical UV spectrum of alkenylresorcinols

281    (Fig. 4B), suggesting that 7 is a derivative of alk(en)ylresorcinol.

282      Compound 7 was then isolated from the culture and subjected to MS and NMR

283    analyses. The molecular weight of 7 was determined to be 344 based on the [M+H]+

284    ion peak at m/z 345.3 (Fig. 4C), which is two mass units less than 6, suggesting that an

                                       13


[PDF physical page 14]
285    additional double bond is present in the fatty acyl side chain of 7. The 1H NMR

286   spectrum showed the three aromatic protons at δH 6.25 (H-4 and H-6) and 6.20 (H-2),

287    benzylic methylene protons at δH 2.50 (H-1'), and terminal CH3 signal at δH 0.90 (H-

288    17'). Four overlapping olefinic proton signals were found at δH 5.34-5.41 and four

289   corresponding carbon signals were found in the 13C NMR spectrum, including δC 130.2,

290    130.1, 128.0 and 127.9, confirming that there are two double bonds in the side chain.

291   By comparing the 1H and 13C NMR data with those of linoleic acid (C18:2 cis, cis-9,12-

292    octadecadienoic acid) (Bus and Frost 1976; Frost and Gunstone 1975; Glass and Dutton

293    1964), compound 7 was identified as 5-(8'Z,11'Z-heptadecadienyl)resorcinol.

294     We next compared the  titers of compounds 1-7 in the cultures of E.  coli

295   BL21(DE3)/pHW4 with and without supplementation of soybean oil. As shown in Fig.

296   4E,   it  is  apparent  that  supply  of  soybean  oil  increased  the  production  of

297    alk(en)ylresorcinols. Additionally, compound 7 was only produced when soybean oil

298   was added into the fermentation medium, indicating that the exogenously supplied fatty

299    acids can be used by SsARS to generate the polyketide products.

300   Heterologous expression of SsARS in S. cerevisiae BJ5464 and characterization of

301   the polyketide products

302   The intron-free gene of Ssars was ligated to the YEpADH2p-URA3 vector to yield a

303    yeast expression plasmid pHW8, which was then expressed in S. cerevisiae BJ5464.

304  HPLC analysis of the fermentation broth of S. cerevisiae BJ5464 revealed that the same

305    set of alk(en)ylresorcinols 1-6 were produced (trace i, Fig. 5), while the strain harboring

306    the empty YEpADH2p-URA3 vector did not produce these products. This result

                                       14


[PDF physical page 15]
307   confirmed that SsARS can also be functionally reconstituted in yeast and synthesize the

308   same products as E. coli.

309   Discussion

310    Shiraia sp. Slf14 is an endophytic fungus from H. serrata. It was found to produce the

311    medicinally important molecules (Yang et al. 2014). In this work, we found a putative

312    type III PKS gene Ssars from the genome of this fungal strain and functionally

313   expressed  it in E. coli and S. cerevisiae. Expression of this enzyme in the two

314    heterologous hosts led to the production of six major alk(en)ylresorcinols 1-6, which

315   unambiguously confirmed that SsARS is a type III PKS that specifically synthesizes

316    resorcinol derivatives with flexible substrate specificity toward the fatty acyl starter

317    units (Fig. 3B). The thioesters of common fatty acids in E. coli (Mejía et al. 1999; Shaw

318   and Ingraham 1965 ) and yeast (Khalilova et al. 2016; Lamacka et al. 1998) are

319   proposed to be used by SsARS as the starter units to synthesize 1-6, with malonyl-CoA

320    as the general substrate for polyketide chain elongation. Specifically, the thioesters of

321    the unsaturated fatty acids including tetradecenoic acid (cis-7), hexadecenoic acid (cis-

322    9), and octadecenoic acid (cis-11) were used by SsARS to generate 2, 4 and 6, while

323    the thioesters of myristic acid (C14:0), palmitic acid (C16:0) and stearic acid (C18:0)

324    acted as the starter units to afford 1, 3 and 5, respectively (Fig. 3B). Fatty acid

325   composition of soybean oil of 18 varieties in the United States was reported to contain

326   about 5% to 11% in linolenic acid (C18:3), 43% to 56% in linoleic acid (C18:2), 15%

327    to 33% in oleic acid (C18:1), as well as 11% to 26% in saturated acids such as 4%

328    stearic and 10% palmitic acids (Collins and Sedgwick 1959). These fatty acids

                                       15


[PDF physical page 16]
329    apparently have contributed to the production of the corresponding alk(en)ylresorcinols,

330    leading to the increased production of 1-6 and the biosynthesis of an additional product

331   7 (from the major unsaturated fatty acid linoleic acid in soybean oil). This indicated

332    that exogenous fatty acids can be used by the engineered E. coli strain to synthesize

333    these alk(en)ylresorcinols.

334       Compared to many other natural products, alk(en)ylresorcinols are relatively rare

335    in nature, with the main known sources being wheat, rye, barley, triticale (cereal

336    grasses), the fruit of Ginkgo biloba, a mollusc and some species of bacteria. The

337    concentrations of alk(en)ylresorcinols are normally between 300 and 1500 μg/g in

338   wheat and rye (Ross et al. 2004). Due to their almost exclusive presence in the outer

339    part of wheat and rye grains among commonly consumed foods (Ross et al. 2003),

340    alk(en)ylresorcinols have been suggested and evaluated as biomarkers of whole grain

341   wheat and rye intake in different populations (Biskup et al. 2016; Kyro et al. 2014;

342   Magnusdottir et al. 2014; McKeown et al. 2010; Wierzbicka et al. 2015). A recent

343    research revealed the dual nature of alk(en)ylresorcinols, direct antioxidant properties

344   and the ability to indirectly regulate the activity of cellular antioxidative defense

345   mechanisms (Gryazeva et al. 2015). Discovery and heterologous reconstitution of

346   SsARS in this work provides an efficient way to produce desired alk(en)ylresorcinols

347   by supplying corresponding fatty acids.

348   Conclusion

349    In summary, we  identified a new  alk(en)ylresorcinol synthase SsARS from an

350   endophytic fungal strain Shiraia sp. Slf14. This enzyme was functionally reconstituted

                                       16


[PDF physical page 17]
351    in both E. coli and S. cerevisiae. This fungal type III PKS showed relaxed substrate

352    specificity toward endogenous and exogenous fatty acids. Depending on the availability

353    of fatty acids in the cells or culture medium, different alk(en)ylresorcinols can be

354    synthesized using this enzyme.
