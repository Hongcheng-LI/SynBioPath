ABSTRACT: Glycosylation of biomolecules can greatly alter their
physicochemical properties, cellular recognition, subcellular local-
ization, and immunogenicity. Glycosylation reactions rely on the
stepwise addition of sugars using nucleotide diphosphate (NDP)-
sugars. Making these substrates readily available will greatly
accelerate the characterization of new glycosylation reactions,
elucidation of their underlying regulation mechanisms, and
production of glycosylated molecules. In this work, we engineered
Saccharomyces cerevisiae to heterologously express nucleotide sugar
synthases to access a wide variety of uridine diphosphate (UDP)-
sugars from simple starting materials (i.e., glucose and galactose).
Specifically, activated glucose, uridine diphosphate
D-glucose
(UDP-D-Glc), can be converted to UDP-D-glucuronic acid
(UDP-D-GlcA), UDP-D-xylose (UDP-D-Xyl), UDP-D-apiose (UDP-D-Api), UDP-D-fucose (UDP-D-Fuc), UDP-L-rhamnose (UDP-
L-Rha), UDP-L-arabinopyranose (UDP-L-Arap), and UDP-L-arabinofuranose (UDP-L-Araf) using the corresponding nucleotide
sugar synthases of plant and microbial origins. We also expressed genes encoding the salvage pathway to directly activate free sugars
to achieve the biosynthesis of UDP-L-Arap and UDP-L-Araf. We observed strong inhibition of UDP-D-Glc 6-dehydrogenase (UGD)
by the downstream product UDP-D-Xyl, which we circumvented using an induction system (Tet-On) to delay the production of
UDP-D-Xyl to maintain the upstream UDP-sugar pool. Finally, we performed a time-course study using strains containing the
biosynthetic pathways to produce five non-native UDP-sugars to elucidate their time-dependent interconversion and the role of
UDP-D-Xyl in regulating UDP-sugar metabolism. These engineered yeast strains are a robust platform to (i) functionally characterize
sugar synthases in vivo, (ii) biosynthesize a diverse selection of UDP-sugars, (iii) examine the regulation of intracellular UDP-sugar
interconversions, and (iv) produce glycosylated secondary metabolites and proteins.
KEYWORDS: uridine diphosphate sugar metabolism, nucleotide sugars, Saccharomyces cerevisiae, de novo pathway,
nucleotide sugar enzymes, salvage pathway
■INTRODUCTION
Glycosylation is a ubiquitous modification found in proteins,
lipids, and natural products, and the presence of sugars on these
molecules can drastically change their bioactivity and
stability.1−3 Extensive chemical efforts have been devoted to
access the synthetic space of glycoconjugates, as well as to
understand the physiological role that sugars play in biology.4
However, chemical synthesis of glycans is nontrivial and involves
selective protection of sugar monomers to direct chemo- and
stereoselective glycosidic bond formation.4
In nature, sugar addition is achieved by highly specific
glycosyltransferases. NDP-sugars serve as the monomer
substrates for more than 90% of glycosylation reactions and
are essential building blocks for naturally occurring poly-
saccharides and glycoconjugates.1 These nucleotide sugars are
primarily synthesized either by (i) the salvage pathway, where a
free sugar is activated by a kinase leading to a sugar-1-phosphate,
which is then converted by an NDP-pyrophosphorylase to an
NDP-sugar, or (ii) the de novo pathway, which involves
nucleotide sugar synthases (Figure 1).5,6 These enzymes provide
a biosynthetic network to interconvert existing nucleotide sugars
to access a wide structural variety of NDP-sugar monomers via
reactions such as oxidation, decarboxylation, epimerization,
etc.5,6
We chose Saccharomyces cerevisiae (baker’s yeast) as the
biosynthetic platform to harbor such enzymatic networks. Due
to its genetic tractability and amenability for scale-up, yeast is an

UDP-D-galactose (UDP-D-Gal).11 These UDP-sugar substrates

Here, we demonstrate that nucleotide sugar synthases of plant
origin can be expressed in engineered S. cerevisiae, both
individually and combinatorially, to access a wide variety of
UDP-sugars. Specifically, UDP-D-Glc can be converted to UDP-
D-GlcA, UDP-D-Xyl, UDP-D-Api, UDP-D-Fuc, UDP-L-Rha,
UDP-L-Arap, and UDP-L-Araf by using their corresponding
nucleotide sugar synthases. The interconversions of UDP-sugars
are allosterically regulated by certain nucleotide sugars.12 One
such example is the strong inhibition of UDP-D-Glc 6-
dehydrogenase (UGD) by the downstream product UDP-D-
Xyl. We circumvented the inhibition by generating a UGD
mutant and screening functional homologues as well as an
orthogonal induction system to delay production of UDP-D-Xyl
to maintain the upstream UDP-sugar pool. As an alternative
synthetic strategy, we expressed the genes encoding the salvage
pathway to synthesize UDP-L-Arap and UDP-L-Araf. Finally, we
integrated pathways to produce five non-native UDP-sugars to
investigate the time-dependent interconversion of these sugars
and the role of UDP-D-Xyl in regulating the UDP-sugar
metabolism. The resulting engineered yeast-harboring pathways
to produce these UDP-sugars will increase the accessibility and
availability of UDP-sugars; facilitate the understanding of
biosynthesis, regulation, and the physiological role that
glycosylated biomolecules play in biology; and serve as a host
for the production of glycosylated products.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Heterologous Expression of UDP-Sugar Synthases in
Engineered Yeast. Plants have elaborate biosynthetic and
transport routes to make more than 30 NDP-sugars.5,13 NDP-
sugar synthases/interconversion enzymes are found in both the
cytoplasm and Golgi apparatus, and these enzymes provide
necessary sugar substrates for glycosylation reactions in various
organelles.5 Highly specific sugar transporters localized in the
Golgi membrane serve as important regulators for NDP-sugar
biosynthesis and metabolism in plants.5,14 While yeast natively
lacks most of these sugar synthases or transporters, its large
cytoplasmic pool of UDP-D-glucose (∼2 μmol/g dry cell
weight) can be harnessed as a systematic platform for the
production of a variety of UDP-sugars.
We functionally expressed the cytosolic homologues of
nucleotide sugar synthases in yeast by constructing expression
cassettes with a galactose-inducible promoter.15,16 The ex-
pression cassettes were integrated into neutral integration loci in
the genome of S. cerevisiae strain Cen.pk2-1c17 using a CRISPR/
Cas9 toolkit.18 The resulting strains were cultured in rich media

The strains were cultured for an additional 48 h before being

UDP-D-GlcA. We started with the biosynthesis of UDP-D-
GlcA, a precursor of several important nucleotide sugars (Figure
1). UGD, an oxidoreductase, oxidizes the C6 position of UDP-D-
Glc to synthesize UDP-D-GlcA.12 UGD requires NAD+/NADP+
cofactors that naturally exist in yeast. Two UGD homologues of
plant and microbial origins were chosen from Arabidopsis
thaliana and Paramoeba atlantica, respectively, and their genes
were integrated into Cen.pk2-1c, creating strains NS-01 and NS-
02, respectively. NS-01 produced 1.2 μmol/g DCW of UDP-D-
GlcA, and NS-02 produced 0.14 μmol/g of DCW (Table 1).
UDP-D-Xyl. UDP-D-Xyl is synthesized from UDP-D-GlcA
through the decarboxylation of the C6 position by UDP-D-Xyl
synthase (UXS).21 We integrated into Cen.pk2−1c a cassette
containing the genes encoding AtUGD and AtUXS from A.
thaliana, creating strain NS-03, which produced 1.3 μmol/g
DCW of UDP-D-Xyl and a negligible amount of UDP-D-GlcA
(Table 1). As UDP-D-Xyl is known to be a strong allosteric
inhibitor of UGD, this result is consistent with previous
reports.12
UDP-D-Api/Xyl. UDP-D-Api is the active form of D-apiose and
a structural isomer of UDP-D-Xyl.22,23 Similarly, UDP-D-Api is
also produced from UDP-D-GlcA by decarboxylation at the C6
position, followed by ring restructuring and branching by UDP-
D-Api/Xyl synthase (AXS).22 UDP-D-Api is a very unstable
compound with a half-life of 100 min at room temperature.24
Previous in vitro studies have shown that UDP-D-Xyl and UDP-
D-Api are both produced by AXS in a range of ratios (from 2:1 to
1:1) at neutral pH.22,25 We constructed strain NS-04 by
integrating genes encoding AtUGD and QsAXS from Quillaja
saponaria. Although we could not detect UDP-D-Api, we were
able to measure the concentration of UDP-D-Xyl (0.55 μmol/g
of DCW), which should correspond to the minimum
concentration of UDP-D-Api (Table 1).
UDP-L-Rha. UDP-L-Rha can be synthesized from UDP-D-Glc
using UDP-L-Rha synthase (RHM). This trifunctional enzyme is
composed of three domains: a 4,6-dehydratase, which
dehydrates UDP-D-Glc to yield UDP-4-keto-6-deoxy-D-Glc; a
3,5-epimerase, which epimerizes UDP-4-keto-6-deoxy-D-Glc to
UDP-4-keto-L-Rha; and a NADPH-dependent 4-ketoreductase,
which reduces UDP-4-keto-L-Rha to UDP-L-Rha.26 A construct
with the gene encoding AtRHM2 from A. thaliana under the
control of a Gal1 promoter was integrated into Cen.pk2-1c to
make strain NS-05, which produced 0.39 μmol/g of DCW of
UDP-L-Rha (Table 1).
UDP-D-Fuc. While L-fucose is a more common isomer, D-
fucose is frequently found in plant metabolites. The biosynthetic
route of UDP-D-Fuc remained unknown until our recent
report.27 It shares the same first synthesis step as the synthesis
of UDP-L-Rha with a UDP-4-keto-6-deoxy-D-Glc intermediate,
which is reduced by a 4-ketoreductase to form UDP-D-Fuc.
Genes encoding SvUG46DH (4,6-dehydratase) and SvNMD
(4-ketoreductase) from Saponaria vaccaria were integrated into
Cen.pk2-1c, creating strain NS-06, which produced 0.18 μmol/g
of DCW UDP-D-Fuc (Table 1).
UDP-L-Arap. UDP-D-Xyl and UDP-L-Arap can be intercon-
verted by UDP-D-Xyl 4-epimerase (UXE).28 Additionally, the
enzyme UDP-D-Glc 4-epimerase 3 (AtUGE3) from A. thaliana
is a bifunctional enzyme that can interconvert UDP-D-Xyl/-L-
Arap as well as UDP-D-Glc/-D-Gal.29 We constructed two strains
by integrating the genes encoding AtUXE2 from A. thaliana and
AtUGE3 into NS-03, creating strains NS-07 and NS-08,
respectively, and measured the UDP-L-Arap concentration.
NS-07 produced 0.31 μmol/g of DCW for UDP-D-Xyl and 0.036
μmol/g of DCW for UDP-L-Arap, whereas NS-08 produced
0.20 μmol/g of DCW for UDP-D-Xyl and 0.038 μmol/g of
DCW for UDP-L-Arap (Figure 2b, Table 1).
To increase the availability of UDP-L-Arap, we utilized the
salvage pathway. This pathway has several advantages, including
orthogonality with yeast’s native sugar metabolism (e.g., less
impact on cellular growth) as well as independence from
allosteric regulation of the nucleotide sugar synthase UGD by
the downstream UDP-D-Xyl. Two enzymes, a kinase and a
pyrophosphorylase, are involved via a sugar-1-phosphate
intermediate prior to the biosynthesis of the complete UDP-
sugar (Figure 2a). Many sugars have known specific kinases
(including D-GlcA, L-Rha, L-Arap, D-Glc, etc.), while pyrophos-
phorylases are more promiscuous.5 However, utilizing the
salvage pathway to make UDP-sugars is limited by the
unavailability and costliness of many sugars as well as the lack
of known kinases for some sugars. We integrated into Cen.pk2-
1c the genes encoding L-arabinokinase (AraK) and UDP-sugar
pyrophosphorylase (USP) from either A. thaliana or Leptospira
interrogans, creating strains NS-09 and NS-10, respectively. The
pathway from L. interrogans produced 76% less UDP-L-Arap
(0.48 μmol/g DCW) compared to the salvage pathway from A.
thaliana (2.0 μmol/g DCW) (Figure 2c, Table 1).

referred to as RGP, reversibly glycosylated polypeptide) is an
enzyme that interconverts UDP-L-Arap and UDP-L-Araf (Figure
2a).30 In plants, the majority of arabinose is found in the

Constructing a Yeast Platform for a Variety of UDP-
Sugars. Tuning Intracellular UDP-D-GlcA and UDP-D-Xyl
Pools. Many glycosylated products are functionalized with
multiple different sugars, so having a yeast strain that can
simultaneously produce multiple UDP-sugars would be very
useful. However, this can be challenging owing to UDP-D-Xyl
being a strong competitive inhibitor of UGD, and this feedback
mechanism is observed ubiquitously in all characterized UGD
homologues.12 Such inhibition is presumed to regulate the
conversion of UDP-Glc to downstream sugars and maintain a
constant intracellular UDP-D-Glc pool (Figure 3a). This is
consistent with our observation that when AtUXS was
coexpressed in the presence of AtUGD in yeast (NS-03), a
low concentration of UDP-D-GlcA was detected (Figure 3b). To
mitigate this inhibition and maintain significant concentrations
of both UDP-D-GlcA and UDP-D-Xyl in the same strain, three
different strategies were tested: (i) mutation of UGD

To alleviate the inhibitory effect of UDP-D-Xyl on the Homo
sapiens UGD (HsUGD), a single mutation A104L has been
shown to lower the allosteric binding affinity of UDP-D-Xyl.31
The amino acid sequence of AtUGD has 61% homology to that
of HsUGD, making the analogous A101L mutation to the
AtUGD have significantly reduced allosteric regulation32
(Figure S1). We constructed a cassette harboring genes
encoding AtUGDA101L and AtUXS, which was integrated into
Cen.pk2-1c to make NS-17. In a similar fashion, PaUGD from P.
atlantica and AtUXS were integrated to make strain NS-18. In
addition to the 56% sequence identity to AtUXS, the bacterial
homologue naturally has the necessary mutation (tyrosine
instead of alanine) for a higher UDP-D-Xyl tolerance (Figure
S2). NS-03 and NS-17 had similar UDP-D-Xyl concentrations,
while NS-18 had 10% of their concentrations, possibly due to the
lower enzymatic activity of PaUGD compared to that of AtUGD
(Figure S3). However, all strains showed negligible UDP-D-
GlcA accumulation at 48 h (Figure 3c).
Alternatively, we sought to delay the expression of the
downstream UXS so that sufficient UDP-D-GlcA could
accumulate in the cell prior to the synthesis of UDP-D-Xyl by
UXS. The common induction systems that are used in yeast (e.g.,
CTR, MET, etc.) are derived from yeast native genes. As a result,
the induction strength is often affected by the cell’s physiological
state and is therefore not compatible with the culture conditions
for galactose induction. The Tet-On promoter was introduced
because it is heterologous33 and thus orthogonal to yeast’s native
metabolism (Figure S4). Doxycycline binds to the reverse
tetracycline-controlled transactivator (rtTA) transcription
factor, allowing it to bind DNA at the promoter and enabling
gene expression.33 We used a Gal1 promoter to drive the
expression of AtUGD and Tet-On promoter (utilizing the G72
V mutant of rtTA-M2 to reduce leakiness33) to drive the
expression of AtUXS, creating strain NS-19. Induction of UXS
(by addition of doxycycline) was initiated 24 h after that of Gal
promoters, which drive expression of all other heterologous
genes. In the absence of the inducer, 1.3 μmol/g DCW of UDP-
D-GlcA and 0.054 μmol/g DCW of UDP-D-Xyl were detected
due to the basal level of UXS expression (Figure 3d). When 50
μg/mL doxycycline was added to the media, the UDP-D-GlcA
concentration decreased by 58% to 0.56 μmol/g of DCW,
accompanied by a large increase of UDP-D-Xyl to 0.37 μmol/g.
However, increased doxycycline concentrations (100 and 200
μg/mL) only led to a decrease in overall sugar levels, possibly
due to an increase in the metabolic burden.

Time-course study of UDP-sugar interconversion. UDP-
sugar biosynthesis and interconversion are tightly regulated in
plants, which makes it challenging to study the dynamics of the
UDP-sugar pools.5 Heterologous expression of UDP-sugar
synthases and interconversion enzymes in a heterologous host
enabled us to study the dynamic interconversion of these UDP-
sugars. Strains NS-27 and NS-28 harbor all of the nucleotide
sugar synthases that are necessary to produce UDP-D-GlcA,
UDP-D-Xyl, UDP-L-Arap, UDP-L-Rha, and UDP-D-Fuc. NS-27
has all of the nucleotide sugar synthase genes under the control
of galactose-inducible promoters, whereas NS-28 has UXS
driven by the Tet-On promoter. We analyzed the levels of UDP-
D-Gal, UDP-D-Glc, UDP-D-GlcA, UDP-D-Xyl, UDP-L-Arap,
UDP-L-Rha, and UDP-D-Fuc over a period of 48 h in both
strains to study nucleotide sugar interconversion dynamics. We
were also able to study the role that UDP-D-Xyl plays in
regulating UDP-sugar metabolism by comparing the UDP-sugar
profile of NS-27 to that of NS-28, where UDP-D-Xyl production
is delayed.
In NS-27, UDP-D-Gal and UDP-D-Glc concentrations were
high at 8 h at 2.3 and 3.3 μmol/g DCW, respectively, and sharply
decreased at 24 h to 1.1 and 1.0 μmol/g DCW, respectively,
where they remained for the rest of the time course (Figure 5).
This decrease can be explained by these precursor UDP-sugars
accumulating early during induction before being converted to
the other downstream UDP-sugars. UDP-D-Gal and UDP-D-Glc
concentrations were also correlated due to their equilibrated
interconversion by native UGE. The concentrations of both
UDP-sugars one enzyme downstream from UDP-D-Glc (UDP-
L-Rha and UDP-D-GlcA) peaked early in the time course at 8 h.
The UDP-L-Rha concentration was 0.14 μmol/g at 8 h and
steadily decreased to 0.059 μmol/g DCW at 48 h. UDP-D-GlcA
showed a similar trend but was very low in concentration,
decreasing from 0.019 μmol/g DCW at 8 h to 0.0059 μmol/g
DCW at 48 h. Other UDP-sugars two or more enzymes
downstream of UDP-D-Glc peaked at 24 h. UDP-D-Xyl and
UDP-L-Arap levels were highest at 24 h at 1.5 and 0.26 μmol/g
DCW, respectively, and their concentrations were correlated
due to their reversible interconversion by AtUGE3. The UDP-D-
Fuc level also peaked at 24 h with a concentration of 0.12 μmol/
g DCW. Overall, the timing of accumulation of UDP-sugars
correlated with the number of enzymes downstream from UDP-
D-Glc, whereas UDP-D-Gal, which is interconverted with UDP-
D-Glc, showed the same trend as UDP-D-Glc.
We observed a very different behavior in NS-28, where the
production of UDP-D-Xyl was delayed. UDP-D-Gal and UDP-D-
Glc pools were 0.22 and 1.3 μmol/g of DCW, respectively, at 8
h, lower than those in NS-27, and steadily increased to 1.5 and
3.3 μmol/g of DCW at 48 h. This suggests that the absence of
UDP-D-Xyl to inhibit UGD initially led to a faster conversion of
UDP-D-Glc to UDP-D-GlcA, and there was a response by yeast
metabolism to increase the UDP-D-Gal and UDP-D-Glc pools.
UDP-D-GlcA concentration peaked at 4.5 μmol/g DCW at 24 h
before the induction of the Tet-On promoter and production of
UDP-D-Xyl. The concentrations of UDP-D-Xyl and UDP-L-Arap
were highest at 48 h at 1.3 and 0.082 μmol/g DCW, respectively.
On comparing the total nucleotide sugar concentrations
between NS-27 and NS-28 over the time course (Figure S6),
NS-27 had more total UDP-sugar than NS-28 at 8 h (6.7 vs 3.1
μmol/g DCW, respectively). At later times, the total UDP-sugar
concentration in NS-27 decreased over time to 3.1 μmol/g
DCW. In NS-28, the total UDP-sugar concentration peaked at
24 h at 8.6 μmol/g DCW right before UDP-D-Xyl production
started and maintained a higher total concentration than NS-27
for the rest of the production. Delaying the production of UDP-
D-Xyl led to a higher total UDP-sugar concentration.
These distinctively different accumulation patterns of UDP-D-
Glc and UDP-D-Gal and total UDP-sugar concentration in NS-
27 and NS-28 can be better understood by comparing them with
those of the unmodified parent strain Cen.pk2-1c and NS-01
(Figures S7 and S8). In Cen.pk2-1c, UDP-D-Gal levels followed
the same trend as NS-27 while UDP-D-Glc levels stayed
consistently high. In NS-01 when UGD was introduced to make
UDP-D-GlcA, UDP-D-Gal and UDP-D-Glc levels followed a
trend similar to that of NS-28, where they increased over time
(Figure S7). Introducing UGD without the production of UDP-
D-Xyl led to higher overall UDP-sugar accumulation in NS-01
(Figure S8). The delayed production of UDP-D-Xyl in NS-28 led
to higher total nucleotide sugar concentrations, indicating that
UDP-D-Xyl helps to maintain UDP-D-Glc pools and possibly
minimize the production of UDP-sugars.

higher concentration of galactose did not lead to an increase in
the total UDP-sugar concentration in NS-27. In NS-28,

■CONCLUSIONS
In summary, we engineered S. cerevisiae to be a biosynthetic
platform for UDP-sugars by utilizing its native pool of UDP-D-
Glc and galactose metabolism combined with plant or bacterial
nucleotide sugar synthases. We have functionally expressed and
confirmed the activity of 11 different types of nucleotide sugar
synthases that are involved in UDP-sugar biosynthesis and
interconversion. In addition to the UDP-D-Glc and UDP-D-Gal
that are native to yeast, we were able to achieve the production of
seven heterologous UDP-sugars (UDP-D-GlcA, UDP-D-Xyl,
UDP-D-Api, UDP-L-Rha, UDP-D-Fuc, UDP-L-Arap, and UDP-L-
Araf) using both the salvage pathway and de novo pathways from
plants. To circumvent the inhibitory effect that UDP-D-Xyl has
on UGD as well as to better tune the intracellular levels of both
UDP-D-GlcA and UDP-D-Xyl, we investigated two strategies: (i)
testing UGD variants with reduced allosteric regulation and (ii)
delayed induction of UXS via an orthogonal induction system.
While the first strategy did not regenerate UDP-D-GlcA pools,
the second strategy enabled the accumulation of both UDP-D-
GlcA and UDP-D-Xyl in yeast. Over the course of 48 h after
induction, we observed the role of UDP-D-Xyl in regulating the
UDP-sugar synthesis to maintain a high intracellular concen-
tration of UDP-D-Glc. In the future it may be necessary to
increase nucleotide sugar concentrations either by testing
different homologues of nucleotide sugar enzymes or by
increasing the native pool of UDP-D-Glc. There have been
previous efforts to increase glycosylation with UDP-D-Glc either
by overexpressing different enzymes involved in UDP-D-Glc
biosynthesis or by knocking out native β-glucosidases.35,36
Additionally, the concentrations of these UDP-sugars in yeast
listed in Table S4, ranging from 62 μM to 3.4 mM, are
comparable with the Kms of various UGTs listed in Table S5
ranging from 44.7 μM to 3 mM, indicating that the nucleotide
sugar concentrations are high enough for intracellular
glycosylation. With this work, we aim to increase the
accessibility and availability of UDP-sugars to accelerate the
characterization of novel glycosylation reactions, as well as to
understand the physiological role that glycosylated biomolecules
play in biology. This engineered yeast will be a useful platform
for the production of a variety of glycosylated natural and new-
to-nature products and proteins.
■METHODS
Plasmid Construction. All plasmids were constructed by
Gibson assembly (New England Biolabs, HiFi DNA Assembly
Master Mix), followed by heat shock transformation into
Escherichia coli DH5α competent cells and plated on Luria−
Bertani (LB) agar containing 100 μg/mL carbenicillin and
grown at 37 °C overnight. E. coli transformants were grown in 5
mL of LB medium containing 100 μg/mL carbenicillin at 37 °C
overnight, followed by miniprep plasmid extraction (Qiagen)
and validation by Sanger sequencing. All genes were codon-
optimized for yeast expression and synthesized by Integrated
DNA Technologies. All genes were assembled as expression
cassettes in pESC plasmids for yeast expression. All enzymes
used in this study are listed in Table S1.
Strain Engineering of S. cerevisiae Cen.pk2-1c. DNA
integrating sequences were constructed using a previously
described method.18 Manufacturer protocols and standard
recombinant DNA procedures were followed for DNA
purification (Qiagen) and DNA amplification (New England
Biolabs, Q5 HighFidelity 2X Master Mix). All primers were
designed using CASdesigner.18 Briefly, DNA fragments to be
integrated were PCR-amplified and then cotransformed with a
Cas9-based plasmid facilitating integration at the targeted locus.
Alternatively, selection markers were integrated by using
homologous recombination. For transformations, a fresh
overnight culture of parent yeast was inoculated into 25 mL of
2xYPD in a 250 mL shake flask at an optical density measured at
600 nm (OD600) of 0.2 and was incubated at 30 °C and 200 rpm
until the OD600 reached 1.0. Then, 5 OD600 of cells were
harvested by centrifugation for 2 min at 3000g and washed with
H2O of half of the media volume. The pellet was then
resuspended with DNA fragments for integration (2 μg) and
pCUT plasmid (0.25 μg) and then mixed with the trans-
formation mix (260 μL of 50% PEG3350, 36 μL of 1 M LiOAc,
and 10 μL of ssDNA).10 The mixture was incubated at 42 °C for
30 min, and the pellet was collected by centrifugation for 2 min
at 3000g. The pellets were then resuspended in 150 μL of H2O,
and the resuspended cells were then plated onto selective agar
plates. The integration was validated by colony PCR and
sequencing; the correct colonies were used for further
engineering after pCUT plasmid curing. Oligonucleotides and
codon-optimized gBlock gene fragments were obtained from
Integrated DNA Technologies. Yeast culture media were
purchased from Becton, Dickinson and Company, and all agar
plates were obtained from Teknova. All strains constructed in
this study are listed in Table S2.
Chemical Standards. All chemical standards used in this
study are analytical grade and are given in Table S3.
Production and Metabolite Extraction of Nucleotide
Sugars. Desired strains were streaked from their glycerol stock
onto YPD (yeast extract, peptone, 2% dextrose) plates and
grown for 48 h at 30 °C to obtain single colonies. Colonies were
picked from the plates and cultured for 48 h in 2 mL of YPD (20
g/L peptone, 10 g/L yeast extract, 20 g/L dextrose) shaking at
200 rpm at 30 °C. The cultures were then centrifuged and
resuspended in 2 mL of YPGal (20 g/L peptone, 10 g/L yeast
extract, 20 g/L galactose unless otherwise noted) and cultured
for another 48 h at 200 rpm and 30 °C, which induced the
expression of genes under Gal1 and Gal10 promoters. For the
induction of the Tet-On promoter, 50−200 μg/mL doxycycline
was added 24 h after the addition of galactose. Samples were
collected 48 h after galactose induction for metabolite extraction
unless otherwise noted.
Nucleotide sugars were extracted using an adapted protocol
for extracting metabolites from S. cerevisiae.20 1 mL of culture
media was collected for each sample and centrifuged at 15,000g
for 1 min and the supernatant was discarded. The pellet was
resuspended in 1 mL of 2:2:1 methanol/chloroform/water
(2:2:1 v/v/v), and the cells were disrupted by bead-beating
using a Qiagen Tissue Lyser (30 Hz, 1 min × 2). The cells were
allowed to cool on ice for 2 min in between. The mixture was
then centrifuged at 4 °C and 14,000g for 10 min to separate the
aqueous and organic layers, from which the aqueous layer was
collected. 400 μL of 1:1 methanol/water was then added to the
remaining organic layer and extracted following the previously
described procedure. The aqueous layers were combined and
evaporated in a SpeedVac vacuum concentrator at room
temperature and resuspended in 100 μL of H2O.

GFP Measurements. High-throughput flow cytometry
experiments were performed using the Accuri C6 flow
cytometer equipped with an autosampler (BD Accuri RUO
Special Order System, Becton, Dickinson and Company, New
Jersey). All fluorescence measurements were performed on
biological triplicates. A total of 30,000 events were recorded at a
flow rate of 35 μL/min and a core size of 16 μm for each sample.
EGFP was excited at 488 nm and emission was detected at 530
nm. Data acquisition was performed as described in the Accuri
C6 Sampler User’s Guide. The acquired data were analyzed in
the FlowJo software (Becton, Dickinson & Company), where
populations were gated and the median of all fluorescence curves
was obtained.
