Thiamine-Dependent Enzymes for the Enantioselective Synthesis of

ABSTRACT: While biocatalysis and electrochemistry each offer
unique advantages, such as enzyme selectivity and traceless electron
use, their integration for unlocking new enzyme function remains
limited. Here, we report a new-to-nature electroenzymatic
desymmetrization strategy that enables the enantiodivergent
synthesis of biaryls bearing axial chirality. By synergistically
combining protein engineering and mediated electrolysis, we
repurpose thiamine-dependent enzymes for the highly efficient
biocatalytic desymmetrization of biaryl dialdehydes, achieving high
enantioselectivity (up to >99% e.e., with enantiodivergence
accessible in some cases), excellent yield (up to 99%), and low
enzyme loading (down to 0.1 mol %, up to 930 TON). Mechanistic investigations confirm that electrochemically generated enzyme-
bound radicals are key to this unnatural reactivity, and the engineered protein scaffolds provide precise enantiodiscrimination while
preventing detrimental overoxidation. This work showcases the potential of synergistic electroenzymatic synthesis as a powerful
platform for unlocking new enzyme reactivity, offering a compelling alternative to established methods such as photobiocatalysis and
artificial enzymes.
■INTRODUCTION
Developing efficient methods for the enantioselective synthesis
of atropisomeric compounds remains a longstanding and
important challenge in chemical research.1 Biocatalysis,2 with
its remarkable ecofriendliness, selectivity, and capacity for
evolution, serves as a highly promising platform for achieving
this goal.3 Selected strategies involve oxidative coupling,4
dynamic kinetic resolution5 (DKR), kinetic resolution6 (KR),
deracemization,7 and desymmetrization (Figure 1A). With
respect to desymmetrization, P450s,8 imine reductases9
(IREDs), alcohol dehydrogenases10 (ADHs), ketoreductases11
(KREDs), lipases,12 and galactose oxidases13 (GOases) have
been utilized for the atroposelective synthesis of axially chiral
compounds. Additionally, artificial metalloenzymes provide an
alternative means to assemble sterically hindered biaryl
atropisomers.14 Despite these elegant advances, which mainly
exploit natural biocatalytic pathways, the burgeoning field of
radical biocatalysis offers distinct and largely untapped potential
for the enantioselective synthesis of atropisomers. The
introduction of radicals as highly active intermediates can
break through native catalytic patterns and unlock new
enzymatic transformations. Additionally, the high selectivity
and evolvability empower enzymes to precisely regulate radicals,
which is a longstanding goal in synthetic chemistry.
Electrochemical synthesis,15 which uses electrons as traceless
redox reagents to initiate and promote chemical trans-
formations, holds promise as an effective strategy to expand
enzyme reactivity. The state of the art in electroenzymatic
synthesis remains largely confined to electrochemical regener-
ation of cofactor16 or hybrid electrochemical-enzymatic cascade
reactions.17 For example, electricity is used as the reducing
power to recycle NAD+ to NADH for the enzyme’s natural
reaction. Very recently, our group reported an electroenzymatic
dynamic kinetic resolution to reshape thiamine diphosphate
(ThDP)-dependent enzymes.18 Moving beyond their native
two-electron umpolung mechanism19 and recently established
light-induced radical−radical coupling pathways,20 we expanded
the functional repertoire of ThDP-enzymes by integrating
electrochemistry with radical biocatalysis. We therefore sought
to investigate whether such an electroenzymatic approach could
serve as a generalizable strategy for unlocking new enzyme

Herein, we report the design and development of a
cooperative electroenzymatic desymmetrization strategy for
the enantioselective construction of axial chirality (Figure 1C).
Our design initiates with the enzymatic ThDP cofactor, which
selectively differentiates between the two aldehyde groups in the
symmetric biaryl substrate. The condensation of dialdehyde and
ThDP cofactor within the active site generates the key Breslow
intermediate (Int. A). Subsequently, single-electron transfer
occurs from the enzyme’s active site through the mediator to the
anode, yielding the enzymatic ketyl radical species (Int. B). Int.
B undergoes another equivalent single-electron oxidation
process to form the acyl azolium cation (Int. C). Finally,
hydrolysis of Int. C affords enantioenriched carboxylic acids
bearing axial stereogenicity and regenerates the catalytically
active carbene for the next cycle.
The key challenge lies in the careful tuning of heterogeneous
electron transfer with enzymatic kinetics and compatibility
between enzyme and electrode (Figure 1C). The reaction
proceeds in an undivided cell, requiring precise enzymatic
intermediate regulation to suppress parasitic side reactions.
Besides, overoxidation of the second aldehyde group could lead
to resymmetrizationa common limitation in conventional
chemocatalysis.22 In this work, we present solutions to these
challenges, demonstrating a robust and efficient route to
enantiopure axially chiral biaryls via synergistic electroenzymatic
desymmetrization.

Subsequently, semirational protein engineering (Figure 2C)
was performed. Based on the wild-type Pf BAL, 13 amino-acid
residues within 5 Å of the polarity-reversed carbon center were
chosen for site-directed mutagenesis (Figure 2D). These
positions were substituted with amino acids of varying steric
bulk: alanine (A) or glycine (G), leucine (L) or valine (V), and
phenylalanine (F) or tryptophan (W), to modulate the binding
pocket’s steric environment. Encouragingly, the A28G mutant
significantly enhanced enantioselectivity (88:12 e.r.), while the
H29A variant afforded the opposite enantiopreference (9:91
e.r.) (Table S16). Further combination of beneficial mutations
(Table S18) led to the identification of the double mutant
A28G-E553L, designated Pf BAL-M1, which delivered (S)-2a in
73% yield with 98:2 e.r. Conversely, the double mutant H29A-
F484G (PfBAL-M2) furnished (R)-2a in 22% yield with 3:97
e.r.
To improve the reaction efficiency, key parameters were
optimized, including the electrochemical mediator (Tables S19
and S24), buffer (Table S21), reaction volume (Table S23), and
ThDP loading (Tables S22 and S25). This led to our optimized
conditions, with the amount of FcMeOH reduced to 10 mol %
and employing a more basic TEOA buffer (pH = 8.0) in a
decreased reaction volume of 2.5 mL. The final loading of ThDP
was 25 mol %, as it demonstrated the highest yield, although
reducing ThDP loading still achieved good yields (Table S25).
Under the optimized conditions, (S)-2a was obtained using
Pf BAL-M1 in 96% yield with 98:2 e.r., while Pf BAL-M2
provided (R)-2a in 60% yield with 1.5:98.5 e.r. The
corresponding Faradaic efficiencies were 24% for Pf BAL-M1
and 15% for Pf BAL-M2 (Figure 2A, entries 2−3).
At a reduced enzyme loading of 0.1 mol %, Pf BAL-M1
maintained high efficiency with a 930 TON, affording (S)-2a in
93% yield without erosion of enantioselectivity (Figure 2A, entry
4). The reaction also proceeded efficiently under ambient
conditions (Figure 2A, entry 7). Control experiments
unequivocally established the essential roles of FcMeOH,
applied potential, and enzyme for reactivity (Figure 2A, entries
8−10). Furthermore, tested chemical oxidants25 instead of
electricity were ineffective (Figure 2A, entry 6). All of these
results underscored the exceptional efficiency and selectivity of
the integrated electroenzymatic system.

Substrate Scope Investigations
With optimized variants and conditions in hand, we next
explored the substrate promiscuity (Figure 3). A range of
symmetric biaryl dialdehydes bearing various monosubstituents
on the benzene ring were well tolerated, delivering products in
good yields and high enantioselectivities (2a−2j). Although
obtaining both enantiomers was not always feasible, one
configuration consistently exhibited superior e.r. values. The
electroenzymatic system proved compatible with both electron-
withdrawing and electron-donating substituents. Substrates with
a sterically demanding group (2h, 2j) also reacted smoothly,
yielding products in good yields with <0.5:99.5 e.r. and 3.5:96.5
e.r., respectively. Naphthalene- and benzoheterocycle-derived
substrates (1k, 1l) underwent efficient conversion with high
yield and excellent enantioselectivity (up to >99% e.e.).
Although the selectivity for 2m was moderate, the trans-
formation remained feasible. Modifications of the aldehyde-
bearing ring were also evaluated, yielding products 2n and 2o
with exceptional stereocontrol. Di- and multisubstituted axially
prochiral dialdehydes were also applicable (2p−2s). For
substrate 2r, the methoxy and fluoro substituents were
effectively differentiated (6:94 e.r.).
Interestingly, monitoring the reaction course suggests that
even if a small portion of the enzyme was deactivated during the
electrolysis, the enantioselectivity of product 2f remained
unchanged (Table S28). The absolute configuration of
compound 2a obtained from the 1.0 mmol-scale reaction
using Pf BAL-M1 as the biocatalyst was determined as (S) via
single-crystal X-ray diffraction. In contrast to our earlier DKR
approach,18 which was occasionally hampered by a mismatch
between the racemization and resolution steps for certain
substrates, the current desymmetrization strategy offers unique
aspects: more straightforward substrate synthesis, broader
substrate tolerance, and consistently high yields and enantiose-
lectivity.
Mechanistic Investigations
Our mechanistic study aims to understand how the engineered
electroenzymatic system overcomes the challenges mentioned
above (Figure 4). Basically, experiments and computational
simulations are designed to address three key issues: (1)
understanding the single-electron oxidation process mediated
by FcMeOH; (2) exploring the origin of the enantiodivergence
using Pf BAL-M1 and Pf BAL-M2; and (3) revealing how
overoxidation was prohibited.
Mediated SET Process
We first elucidate how the enzyme and substrate interact
through ultraviolet−visible spectroscopic analyses. The spectra
revealed a significantly enhanced absorption band around 400
nm for the Pf BAL-M1 and 1a complex relative to controls
(Figure 4A, blue line vs red and green traces), indicating
successful accommodation of the sterically demanding substrate
within the engineered active site. This may be assigned as an
indicator for the formation of the key Breslow intermediate (Int.
A).
To probe the electron-transfer pathway and demonstrate the
enzyme-electrode compatibility, cyclic voltammetry (CV) was
performed. No Faradaic response was observed for the
substrate-enzyme mixture alone, indicating inefficient direct
heterogeneous electron transfer (Figure 4B, purple line). While
FcMeOH exhibited a reversible redox wave, its signal remained
unchanged upon addition of either substrate or enzyme alone
(Figure 4B, gray, green, and red lines). Combining all three
components resulted in a doubled anodic peak and complete
disappearance of the cathodic peak (Figure 4B, blue line),
supporting mediated electron transfer from the corresponding
ferrocenium (FcMeOH+) to the enzymatic Breslow intermedi-
ate.
Low-temperature electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR)
experiments provided additional indications for a single-electron
oxidation pathway (Figure 4C). A radical signal was detected
under standard conditions, though its intensity was significantly
lower than that observed in aerobically prepared samples
without electricity or without the mediator. This observation
may be rationalized by the highly reducing nature of the
proposed Breslow intermediate A, which could undergo SET
oxidation by molecular oxygen. The resulting ketyl radical is
susceptible to further SET oxidation (Int. B →Int. C),
potentially accounting for its diminished concentration under
standard conditions. Control experiments confirmed that no
radical signal was detected in the absence of enzyme, ThDP, or
substrate 1a, consistent with the proposed generation of the
ketyl radical intermediate B.
Origin of Enantioselectivity
The absolute configuration of the final product is determined by
either the strong preferential binding of one enantiomer in the
enzyme pocket when forming the Breslow Int. A or the
mediator-involved selective oxidation of Int. A and/or Int. B.
Alternatively, both factors may work in concert to dictate the
final configuration.
To verify whether the Breslow Int. A generation is
stereoselective, we performed deuterium-labeling experiments26
using substrates 1a and 1l under standard conditions but
without electrolysis and using deuterated buffer (Figures 4D and
S7−S10). As determined by 1H NMR spectra, 1l was labeled in
45% D-inc. with Pf BAL-M1 and 49% D-inc. with Pf BAL-M2
(Figure 4D). More importantly, only the monodeuterated 1l
was detected for both Pf BAL-M1 and Pf BAL-M2 as suggested
by high-resolution mass spectrometry analysis (Figure 4D).
Deuterium labeling of substrate 1a also yielded exclusively
monodeuterated substrate in less than 20% labeling ratio
(Figure 4F, see Supporting Information Section 6.4 for more
discussions). These findings indicate that only one enantiomer
binds to the enzyme with strong preferential affinity, if not
exclusively, which likely serves as the major origin of the
enantioselectivity.
To explore the potential stereoselectivity of the SET steps
involving the mediator, we performed molecular dynamics
(MD) simulations on the binding of Int. A with the oxidized
mediator, FcMeOH+. Although the simulations suggest a
potential binding difference of the mediator with different
configurations of Int. A (Figures S24−S30), this could not be
directly translated into stereodiscrimination. Notably, when
different mediators were employed, we observed variations in
yields but only minimal changes in e.r. values (Table S24), which
argues against enantioselectivity being determined by a
mediator-involved step.
To further understand the origin of enantiodivergence, we
performed MD simulations of the Breslow intermediates bound
to Pf BAL-M1 and Pf BAL-M2 (Int. A in Figures 1 and 4E).
Structural analysis revealed different binding modes. In Pf BAL-
M1, (S)-Int. A formed a more stable hydrogen-bonding network
with Q113, characterized by shorter N−H···O (2.6 vs 3.6 Å) and
O−H···O (2.6 vs 4.1 Å) distances compared to the (R)-Int. A. In
the (R)-Int. A, a steric clash between the bromine atom and the

observed (R)-selectivity. Structural analysis suggests that in the
PfBAL-M2 system, the benzene ring of (R)-Int. A engages in a
T-shaped interaction with the imidazole ring of residue H415

favorable electrostatic and dispersion contributions. These
results underscore the cooperative role of hydrogen bonding
and specific noncovalent interactions in achieving stereo-
divergence with engineered variants. It should be noted that
direct cross-mutant comparisons of these calculated absolute
binding energies are not thermodynamically meaningful. Since
Pf BAL-M1 and Pf BAL-M2 possess different amino acid
sequences, their potential energy surfaces shift from distinct
thermodynamic baselines. Therefore, our computational anal-
ysis strictly relies on intramutant relative energy comparisons.
Collectively, we hypothesize that the origin of enantiodiver-
gence primarily stems from Breslow intermediate formation
preferences, where distinct enzymes favor different enantiomers.
The mediator-involved oxidation step may contribute a minor
influence, but is not decisive.
Preventing Overoxidation
Finally, we investigated the basis of product avoidancea key
feature preventing overoxidation (Figure 4F). Deuterium
labeling experiments showed that 1a was monodeuterated in
20 and 13% ratios, respectively, catalyzed by Pf BAL-M1 and
PfBAL-M2. In contrast, the product 2a exhibited no deuterium
incorporation with the aldehyde hydrogen under identical
conditions. Moreover, subjecting 2a to standard electro-
enzymatic conditions resulted in nearly full recovery without
dicarboxylate formation. We also conducted time-doubled
electrolysis of 1a under standard conditions (Figure S13), in
which no diacid was observed. Collectively, we draw the
conclusion that it is the selectivity of the ThDP enzymes rather
than careful control over the reaction time that prevents the
overoxidation.
■CONCLUSIONS
We report the development of ThDP-dependent electro-
desymmetrizing enzymes for the efficient synthesis of
enantioenriched biaryl compounds. This electroenzymatic
method achieves high enantioselectivity while avoiding over-
oxidation across a broad substrate scope. Mechanistic studies
elucidate key design principles, providing a versatile strategy for
expanding enzyme reactivity by using electricity-driven radical
pathways.
