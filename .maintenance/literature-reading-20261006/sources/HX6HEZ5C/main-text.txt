    OPEN A new 13-lipoxygenase from Taxus
        media callus is functionally linked
         to paclitaxel biosynthesis
                      Abdulsamie Hanano1, Farzana Rahman2 & Denis Murphy3

                       The enzyme 13-Lipoxygenase (13-LOX) plays a pivotal role in the biosynthesis of jasmonates. While
                         Taxus sp. cell suspension cultures have displayed the crucial involvement of Jasmonic acid (JA) in
                               paclitaxel (PTX) biosynthesis activation, 13-LOX had not been identified in this plant species. In this
                          work, a complete cDNA, encoding an 891-amino acid protein sharing high identity with other plant 13-
                         LOXs, was identified (NCBI Genbank MW030627). The purified recombinant TmLOX13 demonstrated
                           optimal enzymatic activity at pH 6.5 and 45 °C, displaying a strong specificity for α-linolenic acid
                             (C18:3) and regioselectivity for carbon 13, producing 13-Hydroperoxyoctadeca-9,11,15-trienoic acid
                          (13-HPOT). Furthermore, TmLOX13 expression in T. media cell suspension cultures peaked before
                          the PTX production phase, suggesting a potential biological link between the 13-LOX pathway
                        and PTX biosynthesis. This connection was validated through a small interference RNA (siRNA)-
                              silencing approach, where silencing of TmLOX13 in T. media cells led to a significant deficiency in PTX
                             biosynthesis, accompanied by a marked down-regulation of PTX gene expression. Similar effects
                        were observed when the 13-LOX activity was inhibited using nordihydroguaiaretic acid (NDGA), a
                                specific inhibitor of 13-LOX. Of particular interest, the PTX-deficient phenotype of TmLOX13-silenced
                                  cells was effectively rescued by exogenous supplementation with oxylipins derived from the 13-LOX–
                         peroxygenase pathway. This comprehensive investigation sheds light on the functional significance
                             of TmLOX13 in the intricate network of biochemical pathways underlying PTX biosynthesis in Taxus
                          media.

                                step being the cyclization of geranylgeranyl diphosphate (GGPP) to taxa-4(5),11(12)-diene—a reaction catalyzed
                           by taxadiene synthase (TXS)2. Through a series of enzymatic transformations, 10-deacetylbaccatin III (DBT) is
                             hydroxylated by the enzyme 10-deacetylbaccatin III-10-O-acetyltransferase (DBAT), resulting in the formation
                                of the diterpene intermediate baccatin III3. Baccatin III is then conjugated with a β-phenylalanyl-CoA side
                             chain by C-13 phenylpropanoyl-CoA transferase (BAPT), producing 3-N-debenzoyl-2-deoxypaclitaxel. Finally,
                              the enzyme 3-N-debenzoyl-2-deoxypaclitaxel N-benzoyltransferase (DBTNBT) catalyzes the attachment of a
                             benzoyl group from benzoyl-CoA, yielding paclitaxel (PTX) as the final product4,5. Classified as a member of
                                taxanes, PTX is a tetracyclic diterpenoid that was initially characterized as one of the most potent antineoplastic
                              agents with proven anti-cancer effects. Nowadays, PTX is largely applied as a chemotherapy medication against
                              various types of advanced tumors, including ovarian, breast, metastatic pancreatic, Kaposi’s sarcoma and non-
                                 small-cell lung tumors6–9. Initially, PTX was extracted from the bark of the Pacific yew tree, Taxus brevifolia, and
                         showed a high cytotoxicity against mouse tumor cells10. Subsequently, the chemical structure of PTX molecule
                          was determined by X-ray analysis, and it was verified as an efficient large broad-spectrum natural anticancer
                                     drug9,11,12. PTX was therefore approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration firstly for the treatment of
                           advanced ovarian cancer and later for other cancer types13.
                             Twenty years after its discovery, the sustainable production of PTX is still a multi-factorial challenge. In
                                  particular, the low yield of PTX from its natural sources, together with its structural complexity, are considered
                              the most important factors that limit its sustainable production9,14–16. Alternative biotechnological approaches
                            based on the use of Taxus sp.cell suspension cultures have been established and effectively used for PTX
                              production, yet enhancing its yield remains a significant on-going challenge17–19..
                                   Intensive studies have been conducted over last decade to improve the production of PTX in Taxus sp. cell
                             suspension cultures. This research was mainly based on testing a set of variants such as Taxus sp. cell line varieties,
                                culture conditions, media composition, use of elicitors and other bioreactor systems20–23. Several of them
                              suggested promising outcomes for the use of elicitors and certain plant hormones24–26. In particular, jasmonic
                               acid (JA) and its methyl-derivative (MeJA) have been shown to play roles in enhancing the biosynthesis of PTX
                               in Taxus sp. cell suspension cultures24,26. Jasmonic acid is known as a plant oxylipin-derivative hormone that
                                regulates several key physiological processes in plants including development, stress responses and immunity
                                 systems27–31. The first step of JA biosynthesis starts by the di-oxygenation of linolenic acid (C18:3) under the
                               action of 13-lipoxygenase (13-LOX).
                                In general, plant lipoxygenases (LOXs) have well characterized iron or manganese-dependent dioxygenases
                                    activities that transfer molecular oxygen (O2) to polyunsaturated fatty acids notably, linoleic acid (LA, C18:2ω6),
                                 linolenic acid (ALA, C18:3ω3), forming therefore the corresponding hydroperoxides,  i.e. hydroperoxy
                              octadecadienoic acid (HPOD), hydroperoxy octadecatrienoic acid (HPOT), and hydroperoxy eicosatetraenoic
                               acid (HPOET), respectively32,33. LOXs are normally classified according to their regiospecificity, the position of
                           carbon atom in the fatty acid where the oxygen molecule is incorporated, into 9-LOXs and 13-LOXs34–36. Most
                               plant LOXs identified to date are 13-LOXs that produce 13-hydroperoxide fatty acids, which are concurrently
                             metabolized by at least four key enzymes, forming together the 13-LOX oxylipin biosynthetic pathways37–39.
                            These include divinyl ether synthase (DES)40, hydroperoxide lyase (HPL)41, allene oxide synthase (AOS)42,
                                also known as the JA-biosynthesis pathway, and peroxygenase (PXG), the latest identified branch in 13-LOX
                              pathway43. Together, these enzymes work cooperatively, and sometimes competitively, to transform 13-HPOT
                                into a bouquet of highly active molecules with proven impacts on seed germination and plant growth, pathogen
                                  resistance, response to abiotic stresses in many economically important plants34,44. The role of the 13-LOX
                           pathway in activating the biosynthesis of a range of plant secondary metabolites was recently reported, whereby
                           exogenous treatment of Taxus sp. cell suspensions with 13-HPOT significantly enhanced the biosynthesis and
                                secretion of PTX45 by inducing the expression of caleosins/peroxygenases, plus the accumulation of cellular lipid
                               droplets in Taxus media cell cultures.
                                In this context, although the biological importance of 13-LOX- derivative oxylipins in the stimulation of PTX
                               biosynthesis has been demonstrated, no 13-LOX enzyme has been characterized to date in any species of Taxus
                                   sp. The main objective of the current study was to identify and characterize 13-lipoxygenase from Taxus media
                                  callus and describe the functional connection of 13-LOX activity with the biosynthesis of PTX. For that, a gene
                       TmLOX13, encoding a typical 13-lipoxygenase has been cloned, heterologously expressed and the enzymatic
                                   activity of the recombinant protein has been characterized. Furthermore, the biological involvement of TmLOX13
                               in the biosynthesis of PTX has been evaluated using TmLOX13-silenced cells of T. media. The results obtained
                          from the complementation of TmLOX13-silenced cells suggested a particular role of the 13-LOX-PXG pathway
                               in the regulation of PTX biosynthesis.

                       Materials and methods
                        Chemicals and plant materials
                             Culture media components and agarose were procured from Pronadisa (Torrejon de Ardoz, Spain), while high-
                             grade liquid chromatography solvents and buffer components were provided by Merck (Darmstadt, Germany).
                             X-Gal, isopropyl-β-D-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG), RNase A, molecular markers for DNA,  restriction
                          enzymes and dNTPs were obtained from Fermentas (Vilnius, Lithuania). Antibiotics, soybean LOX, amino acids
                         and fatty acids were sourced from Sigma–Aldrich (St. Louis, MO). Jasmonic acid (purity > 98%) was procured
                            Larodan, Inc., Sweden. The study was performed on cell suspension cultures regeneration from Taxus media calli
                               previously established from stems as described before45,46. Cell suspensions were initially cultured using a cell
                           growth medium composed of Gamborg’s B5 medium supplemented with 3% sucrose, 0.01% myo-inositol, B5
                               vitamins, 4 mg L− 1 of 2,4-dichlorophenoxyacetic acid (2,4-D), 1 mg L− 1 of kinetin, and 0.1 mg L− 1 of gibberellic
                               acid (GA3). The flasks were capped with Magenta B-Caps and incubated in the dark at 25 °C and 100 rpm in a
                             shaker incubator for 24 days.

                         Isolation of full-length of 13-LOX gene from T. media calli
                mRNA extraction and cDNA biosynthesis
                             Primers design and PCR
                             Basing on the bioinformatic analysis of 13-LOX proteins sequences available in public protein databases (NCBI/
                               Proteins and Uniprot), highly conserved regions of amino acids were identified (Fig. S1). Two of them were
                                selected to generate a couple of primers LOX1F and LOX1R (Table S1). These two primers, together with 1 µL of
                    cDNA prepared from T. media calli were used to partially amplify the 13-LOX cDNA. The PCR was performed
                               in a 25 µl reaction final volume containing 200 µM of each dNTPs, 3 mM MgSO4, 10 µM of each primer and
                                2.5 U High Fidelity Taq polymerase (Thermo Fisher). PCR conditions were similar to those described before47.
                          The obtained PCR products were analysed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, purified using a QIAquick PCR
                                 purification kit (Qiagen, USA) and subsequently sequenced using an ABI 310 Genetic Analyser (Applied
                              Biosystems). This step allowed to identify a fragment of 2028 bp of 13-LOX gene with a complete 3’-end and a
                             missing 5’-end.

                   PCR amplification of cDNA 5’-end
                          The partial sequence of 13-LOX gene amplified and sequenced in the first step was used to design an overlapping
                                pair primers LOX3 and LOX4 located at the 3’-end of the gene. This pair was used as gene-specific primers to
                             amplify the 5’-end of the gene using a 5’ RACE System for Rapid Amplification of cDNA Ends from Thermo
                               Fisher scientific and according to the manufacturer’s manual. The PCR was performed as described in the
                                         first step. The Full-length 13-LOX gene sequence was analysed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, purified and
                             subsequently sequenced using an ABI 310 Genetic Analyser (Applied Biosystems). The complete sequenced
                              clone was referred to as TmLOX13.

                        Cloning and heterologous expression of T. media 13-LOX gene
                                Full-length ORF of TmLOX13 gene (2676 bp) was amplified by PCR using T. media calli cDNA. Primers used
                                 for cloning and expression of recombinant protein were designed (Table S1). The purified TmLOX13-PCR
                            product was ligated into the pGEM-T Easy vector (Promega, USA) according to the manufacturer’s manual. The
                            recombinant plasmids pGEM-T/TmLOX13 was transferred into E. coli Top10 competent cells. Recombinant
                             plasmids were extracted from E. coli Top10 using a plasmid purification mini kit (Qiagen, Germany). The
                             presence of TmLOX13 in the plasmid was confirmed by PCR and BamHI-HindIII digestion. At least three clones
                                of TmLOX13 gene were sequenced on both strands using T7 promoter and terminator primers on an ABI 310
                             Genetic Analyser (Applied Biosystems). The full-length TmLOX13 ORF was ligated into the expression vector
                           pET-28a(+) (Merck Millipore, Germany) using T4 DNA ligase (Qiagen, Germany) to generate pETt-TmLOX13
                          which encodes a fused TmLOX13 with a His tag at the N-terminus. The obtained construct was checked by
                            sequencing prior to transformation into E. coli BL21 (DE3). pETt-TmLOX13-transformants were cultivated in
                           2 mL of Luria-Bertani (LB) medium containing 50 µg mL− 1 kanamycin and incubated overnight at 37 °C. The
                                culture of the E. coli BL21 (DE3) was subsequently transferred into 1 L-volume culture of LB medium containing
                              the same concentration of kanamycin and re-incubated at 37 °C until obtaining an OD600 about of 0.6. When
                                   this is done, the protein expression was induced by adding isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) to the
                       medium at a final concentration of 0.5 mM and maintained in the same conditions for 24 h.

                      Sequence analysis of TmLOX13 protein
                              Evolutionary history of TmLOX13 gene was deduced through the utilization of the Neighbor-Joining method48.
                          The resulting optimal tree is presented, with the percentages of replicate trees in which the associated taxa
                          formed clusters in the bootstrap test (based on 1000 replicates) displayed adjacent to the branches49. To
                                 calculate evolutionary distances, the Poisson correction method was employed, expressed in units of amino acid
                                substitutions per site. This investigation encompassed a set of 20 amino acid sequences. All ambiguous positions
                           were eliminated for each sequence pair using the pairwise deletion option. The final dataset consisted of a total
                                of 1033 positions. The evolutionary analyses were carried out using MEGA1150. Detection of the presence of
                          transmembrane helices in TmLOX13 protein was performed using the TM finder tool TMHMM 2.0 ​(​h​t​t​p​s​:​/​/​s​

                          Characterization of TmLOX13 enzymatic activity
                          The LOX activity was routinely assayed by continuously monitoring the formation of conjugated dienes at
                           234 nm. Linoleic (C18:2) and linolenic (C18:3) acids were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. The substrates were
                             prepared according to Gata et al.52,. The standard reaction mixture was composed of 1.5 mL of 100 mM sodium-
                            phosphate buffer pH 6.5, 0.5 mM of fatty acid solution in DMSO and 5 µL (10 µg) purified enzyme at saturating
                           oxygen concentrations, incubated at 25 °C. The increase of hydroperoxidation was detected at 234 nm at 25 °C in
                             a Jenway 6840 spectrophotometer for 3 min and the slope of the linear phase of the plot was used to calculate the
                    LOX activity. Thus, one unit (U) of LOX activity is defined as the amount of enzyme catalyzing the formation of
                           1 µmol of product per minute at 25 °C. The kinetic values of TmLOX13 against linoleic and linolenic acids were
                                calculated using a range of substrate concentrations in standard reaction conditions. The substrate specificity
                          was determined by incubating the recombinant TmLOX13 with 0.5 mM of linoleic (C18:2) and linolenic (C18:3)
                                acids in standard reaction conditions. The optimum pH for the enzymatic activity of TmLOX13 was determined
                             over a pH range of 4.0–9.0 at 25 °C as described by Cao et al.53,. The optimal temperature of the enzymatic
                                   activity was determined over a range of 15–60 °C in increments of 5 °C using the standard assay conditions.
                            Hydroperoxides (-OOH) fatty acids were determined according to Hanano et al.44, using a Jasco LC-2000 plus
                                  series HPLC system (Jasco, USA) with a UV-detector (RF-10Axl, Shimadzu) (234 nm) and a C18 column
                                (Eclipse XDB-C18 150 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm; Agilent Technologies, USA). The analysis was performed using a mobile
                            phase of acetonitrile/water/acetic acid (50/50/0.1, v/v/v) at a flow rate of 0.6 mL min− 1. FA-hydroperoxides were
                               quantified using their respective standards.

                           Transcript analysis
                          The expression profile of TmLOX13 gene during time course of T. media cell suspension cultures were analysed
                           by reverse-transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR). Briefly, samples from whole cells (about 1 g) at stages 4,
                                    8, 12, 16, 20 and 24 days after inoculation (DAI) were taken to extract total RNA using an RNeasy kit according
                                to the manufacturer’s protocol (Qiagen, Germany). An aliquot of 1 µg total RNA was used for first-strand cDNA
                                synthesis according to Hanano et al. (2014)54. Real-time PCR was performed using a AriaMx Real-time PCR
                           System from Agilent technologies, USA. Briefly, 25 µL reaction mixtures contained 0.5 µM of each specific
                               oligonucleotide primer for the target genes (TmLOX13, TXS, DBAT and DBTNBT) and the reference gene (TIP-
                             41) (Table S1), 12.5 µL of SYBR Green PCR mix (Bio-Rad, USA) and 100 ng cDNA. Subsequently, the relative
                                quantification (RQ) of target genes was calculated directly using the software installed in the qPCR system.

                         Extraction and analysis of PTX
                     PTX from the culture media was extracted according to Cusido et al.19,. Briefly, 10 mL of media was mixed and
                             vortexed for 2 min with 5 mL of dichloromethane (DCM), then sonicated for 1 h at 25° C. The lower organic
                            phase of DCM was carefully recovered by a glass Pasteur pipette, replaced in a 5-mL amber vial and evaporated
                                to dryness under a stream of nitrogen gas. The residual PTX was re-suspended into 200 µL acetonitrile. Finally,
                              the extracts were filtered before analysis through a 0.22 μm PVDF filters (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). The
                             accuracy of extraction was evaluated using a spiked sample with known concentration of a commercial PTX
                         (T7402-5MG, purity ≥ 95%, Sigma-Aldrich) and the extract recovery (%) was therefore calculated. Quantification
                                of PTX was performed using a Sykam HPLC system (Germany) with a UV-detector at 227 nm. The separation
                                of PTX was performed on a C8 HPLC Column (5 μm particle size, L × I.D. 15 cm × 4.6 mm) (Sigma-Aldrich)
                                as described by Yousfan et al.55,. The following conditions were used: column temp: 25 °C; mobile phase: water:
                                   acetonitrile; (50:50, v/v): flow rate:1 mL min− 1; pressure: 2727 psi (188 bar); injection volume: 5 µL. To avoid any
                                technical variations, an internal standard of PTX (25 µg mL− 1) was added to the experimental samples. The peak
                                areas corresponding to PTX extracted from the samples and its authentic standard were therefore compared.
                          Measurements were performed in triplicate.

                          Silencing of TmLOX13 gene by SiRNA approach
                        siRNA primers used in this study were designed online by siDirect Designer (http://design.RNAi.jp/)56 to target
                              highly conserved region of the mRNA sequence of TmLOX13 gene (accession number NCBI: MW030627).
                          The tool identified 473 potential (21nt)-target regions in the Tm13LOX mRNA that could be degraded by 743
                                  specific siRNA oligos. Among these, only three 21nt target regions were selected, corresponding to specific
                                variant domains in the Tm13LOX protein. These domains were identified through multi-alignment analysis of
                           20 well-characterized LOX isoforms from various plants. The positions of the (21nt)-target regions, siRNA oligo
                              sequences, and additional details are provided in the supplementary information, where the three selected target
                              regions are highlighted in yellow. The silencing efficiency of the three proposed siRNA oligos was tested, and
                                  ultimately, the first oligo, located at position 93–115, was selected. This oligo was designated as siRNATmLOX13
                               (Table S1). Delivery of siRNA to protoplasts was carried out according to57[,58. In a sterile 1.5 ml-centrifuge tube,
                           10 µl of each siRNA was mixed with an equal volume of Lipofectin reagent (Invitrogen Life Technologies, UK)
                         and allowed to stand for 15 min at 20º C. A volume of 50 µl of T. media cells were added and mixed gently. The
                              tubes were incubated at 20º C for 24 h to allow transfection to proceed59. Then the mixture was inoculated in 10
                        ml of growth medium with 1.2 M of sorbitol for 7 days at 28º C in the dark. A similar treatment of cells without
                        siRNA was performed as negative controls. All experiments were carried out using three biological replicates.

                           Inhibition of 13-LOX activity
                          The inhibitory effect of nordihydroguaiaretic acid (NDGA) against TmLOX13 activity60 was firstly determined
                               in vitro using the purified recombinant TmLOX13. The enzyme was pre-incubated with 1 mM of NDGA for
                           5 min at 25 °C. Then, the enzymatic activity was assayed as described before using 0.5 mM of linolenic acid
                              (C18:3) acids in optimal reaction conditions for the enzyme activity. Subsequently, the inhibition of LOX by
                  NDGA was assayed in T. media cell suspension culture. In this experiment, the inhibitor was added to the
                                  T. media cell culture at 5 mM and the production of PTX as well as the transcripts levels of TXS, DBAT and
                     DBTNBT were evaluated at 4, 8, 12, 16, 20 and 24 days after inoculation (DAI).

                       JA-exogenous treatment of TmLOX13-silenced cells
                                  Elicitation of both TmLOX13-silenced and wild-type T. media cells was carried out using jasmonic acid (purity
                       > 98%, Larodan, Inc., Sweden) at the onset of the cell growth stage and maintained for 24 days19. For JA-treated
                                  cultures, 1 mg of jasmonic acid was initially dissolved in ethanol, then evaporated to dryness under a nitrogen
                              stream, and subsequently reconstituted in 1 mL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare a stock solution at
                             a concentration of 1 mg mL− 1. Cell cultures were elicited with 100 µM JA for treated samples, while control
                                cultures received an equal volume of DMSO alone. PTX was extracted and quantified at 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, and 24
                                days. Additionally, cells from both treated and control cultures were harvested by filtration at the specified time
                               points for gene expression analysis.

                      Complementation of TmLOX13-silenced cells with 13-LOX/PXG-pathway Oxylipins
                              Oxylipins derived from the 13-LOX/PXG pathway were synthesized in three successive steps as described
                                 previously57. In  brief, commercial 13-HPOT was incubated overnight at 26 °C with 50 µg of purified
                            recombinant AfPXG, a fungal peroxygenase with well characterized hydroperoxide-reductase activity57,61. The
                                respective oxylipin derivatives (13-HOT, 9,10-epoxy-octadecadienoic acid, 12,13-epoxy-octadecenoic acid,
                              9,10-dihydroxy-octadecadienoic acid, 12,13-octadecadienoic acid, 9,10,18-trihydroxy-octadecadienoic acid
                         and 12,13,18-trihydroxy-octadecadienoic acid) were separated and determined according to Hanano et al., 44.
                     An ethanol solution of 100 µM of 13-HOT or a mix of epoxides (Mix-EOE) or dihydroxys (Mix-DHOE) was
                          added to cultures of (+) siRNTmLOX13 cell line. Thereafter, the biomass, PTX production and the expression of
                          TXS, DBAT and DBTNBT were determined at 4, 8, 12, 16, 20 and 24 days after inoculation (DAI). Six separated
                              treatments were examined for their responses to each group of oxylipins. In parallel, a control experiment was
                               carried out using ethanol only.

                         Extraction and analysis of JA
                          JA was extracted according to Liu et al.62,. Briefly, 500 mg of harvested frozen cells was ground to fine powder
                             with a mortar and pestle in the presence of liquid N2. JA was extracted with 600 µL of methanol at 4 °C overnight.
                          The samples were subsequently centrifuged at 4800 × g for 10 min. The supernatant was transferred to a new 5
                   mL glass tube, evaporated under N2 flow until dryness, then the residue was re-taken with 200 µL of methanol.
                           3000 µL of distillated H2O was added to the extract and the mixture was applied onto the Waters Sep-pak C18
                                 cartridge. The cartridge was washed with 200 µL of 20% methanol and 250 µL of 30% methanol. Finally, the JA
                                extract was eluted with 300 µL of methanol for further analysis. JA concentration was determined using a Sykam
                    HPLC System, Germany, with an UV-Vis Diode Array Detector (DAD) following the method described by Liu
                                   et al.62,. The mobile phase was composed of acetonitrile and triethylamine buffer (3 mL L− 1, pH 2.6). The C18
                          was used as the stationary phase (4.6 × 250 mm; 5 μm) at 35 ˚C with a flow rate of 1 mL min- 1. The detection
                            wavelength was 195 nm and the injection volume was 10 µL.

                             Statistics
                                All data presented were expressed as means ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was performed using
                     IBM SPSS statistics version 23.0. Statistical significance between control and treatments was evaluated by
                     ANOVA.

                       Results
                   TmLOX13 encodes a typical 13-lipoxygenase
                   A gene sequence homology approach coupled with 5’-end Rapid Amplification cDNA end, identified a full-length
                    cDNA of 2676 bp. This clone was referred to as TmLOX13 and its complete coding sequence (CDS) was deposited
                               in GenBank database under the accession number (MW030627). This CDS predictably encodes a protein of 891
                         amino acids that shares a high identity with other plant 13-lipoxygenases. Table S2 (supplementary information)
                               presents the identity scores between the TmLOX13 amino acids sequence and the top 20 representative plant
                          LOXs. We note that the highest three scores of identities (55.53, 55.41 and 54.82%) were detected between
                     TmLOX13 and 13 S-LOX from Olea europaea var. sylvestris and with two 13-LOXs isoforms from Olea europaea

                       Discussion
                            13-Lipoxygenase (13-LOX) is a key enzyme in the biosynthesis of a bouquet of plant oxylipins that play important
                                 roles in plant growth, development and responses to biotic and abiotic stresses. In particular, jasmonic acid (JA)
                         and its methyl ester (MeJA) have roles in activating the biosynthesis of PTX63. Together with jasmonates, the
                           13-LOX-PXG-derivative oxylipins also have been recently described as new modulators for the biosynthesis
                         and secretion of PTX58. A full-length ORF of 2676 bp (TmLOX13) encoding a typical 13-LOX of 891 amino
                                acids was identified in Taxus media callus. TmLOX13 shares a high identity with a linoleate 13 S-lipoxygenase
                          from Olea europaea but less with 9-LOX from Taxus chinensis64,65, suggesting that TmLOX13 likely exhibits
                             13-hydroperoxidase activity.
                      We show here that the purified recombinant TmLOX13 protein has an optimal enzymatic activity at pH 6.5
                         and 45 °C with highest specificity towards α-linolenic acid (C18:3). Similar parameters and substrate specificities
                           were found for other plant 13-LOXs from olive (Olea europaea)65–67, from rapeseed (Brassica napus L.) 68, from
                         melon (Cucumis melo L. var. makuwa Makino)53, suggesting that the enzymatic activity of TmLOX13 requires
                                similar reaction parameters to those of other characterized plant 13-LOXs.
                            The regioselectivity of LOXs is a significant catalytic characteristic that determines the biological functions of
                              the products produced through the metabolism of 9- or 13-hydroperoxides via two separate enzymatic pathways,
                       9-LOX and 13-LOX. Analytically, our data revealed that purified TmLOX13 mainly peroxidates α-linolenic acid
                              (C18:3) at position 13, producing 13-hydroperoxyoctadeca-9,11,15-trienoic acid. This result was consistent with
                              the presence of highly conserved sites in plant LOX proteins that determine the regioselectivity and stereo-
                                   specificity of LOXs. In line with this, a space-filling histidine (His) or phenylalanine (Phe) residue at the bottom
                                of the substrate-binding pocket was identified in almost all plant 13-LOXs, while a valine (Val) residue was
                                 identified in plant 9-LOXs69,70. Amino acid sequence alignment of the deduced TmLOX13 with other plant 13-
                      LOXs also showed that this ortholog harbours all the structural and functional domains responsible for substrate
                                    fixing, oxygen binding, iron binding as typically present in other 13 S-lipoxygenases53.
                       PTX gene expression and enzymatic activity were then followed during the two phases of T. media cell
                             suspension culture, the growth phase (1–12 DAI) and the PTX production phase (12–24 DAI). Interestingly, the
                               highest activity of TmLOX13 was detected in the beginning of PTX production phase, suggesting a possible link
                           between the 13-LOX pathway and PTX production. Before starting the discussion of a such biological role of 13-
                        LOX, it is worthy to note that exogenous treatment of T. media cell suspensions with 13-HPOT was also found to
                                  significantly enhance the biosynthesis and secretion of PTX58. It is well recognized that 13-hydroperoxide FAs act
                                as activator molecules for producing secondary metabolites in plant cells71. This is due to the direct participation
                                of such hydroperoxides in the biosynthesis of oxylipins72–75. For example, 13-(S)HPOT is known as common
                                substrate for at least four main enzymatic branches, i.e., allene oxide synthase (AOS)42, peroxygenase (PXG)76,
                                divinyl ether synthase (DES)40 and hydroperoxide lyase (HPL)77, giving a family of oxylipin compounds (Fig.
                                 10). Together, these enzymatic studies demonstrate a series of particular oxylipin signatures to meet specific
                                physiological requirements in plants78–80. In this context, among the oxylipins derived from the metabolism
                                of 13-HPOT, C6-volatile oxylipins like hexenal and traumatine, hydroxy- and epoxy-fatty acids, along with
                                   esterified oxylipins such as etherolenic acid and jasmonates, are considered the most active oxylipins, enhancing
                                therefore the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites and the immunity system of plant cells71. This can be

                            supported by earlier observations that showed a significant increase in the production of PTX in T. media cells
                                cultures treated with MeJA81,82.
                            The involvement of caleosin/peroxygenase (CLO/PXG) in the biosynthesis and secretion of PTX was recently
                               highlighted at two complementary levels: the first one is based on the fatty acid hydroperoxide (FA-OOH)-
                              reductase activity of CLO/PXG, leading to the formation of their corresponding alcohols (FA-OH), that impact
                              the plant growth, development and its responses to stress43,44,58,83,84. The second level is based on the structural
                                 roles of CLO/PXG in cellular lipid droplet (LD) assembly85–87. This is consistent with recent data indicating
                                 that treatment of T. media cells with 13-HPOT-with induced expression of CLO/PXG, thus increasing the LD
                            production and activating the secretion of PTX58,63. Similar observations were also reported in plants exposed
                                to hydrophobic environmental contaminants such as dioxins44,47,86–89.
                                Further data on the role of TmLOX13 in the biosynthesis of PTX were gained by siRNA-based silencing
                                of TmLOX13 gene in T. media cell suspension. Silencing of TmLOX13 reduced the production of PTX with
                        no significant effects on cell growth. This decline is probably due to down-regulation of some key genes of
                              the PTX biosynthesis pathway, most notably TXS, DBAT and DBTNBT. In line with this, silencing of 13-
                    LOX led to decreased tolerance of cotton against Verticillium dahliae through a JA-mediated pathway90. Also,
                           OsHI-LOX-silenced rice became more susceptible to chewing herbivores, but more resistant to a phloem
                                    feeder91,92. Moreover, silencing of LOX H1 in potato plants resulted in a marked reduction in the production
                                of volatile aliphatic C6 aldehydes, acting as either signalling molecules for wound-induced gene expression or
                                as antimicrobial substances93. A recent report showed that silencing of CLO/PXG-encoding genes in T. media
                                      cell cultures reduces production of PTX58. This implies that the TmLOX13-silencing phenotype is due to the
                              deprivation of 13-LOX/PXG pathway-derivative metabolites.
                          Our data indicate that exogenous application of JA to TmLOX13-silenced cell suspensions restored the PTX-
                                 deficient phenotype by upregulating PTX biosynthetic genes. Consistent with these findings, the direct role of JA
                               in activating PTX biosynthesis has been shown to be mediated by MYC2, a key JA-responsive transcription factor
                             belonging to the basic helix–loop–helix (bHLH) family94, MYC2 regulates the expression of critical genes involved
                               in secondary metabolite biosynthesis in plants95. Specifically, MYC2 directly modulates PTX biosynthesis by
                             binding to cis-regulatory elements, such as the G-box, within the promoters of its target genes96. In this context,
                   MYC2 has been reported to activate several key genes in the PTX biosynthetic pathway, notably TXS (taxadiene
                               synthase, following JA treatment95,97. Moreover, our results also indicate that exogenous treatment with 13-LOX/
                             PXG-derivative oxylipins restored the PTX-deficient phenotype of T. media cells resulting from the silencing of
                       TmLOX13. However, PTX secretion by TmLOX13-silenced cells was re-established up to normal level compared
                                to wild type-cells after treatment with 13-HOT, Mix-EOE and Mix-HOE, respectively. Intriguingly, similar
                                    effects of such oxylipins have been reported in fungal cells silenced of AfPXG gene, a fungal CLO/PXG with roles
                               in fungal growth, development and secondary metabolite production57,98.

                      Conclusions
                             In conclusion, our work emphasizes the pivotal role of 13-Lipoxygenase (13-LOX) in enhancing PTX
                                biosynthesis, thereby contributing to the advancement of production strategies. Through the identification and
                                characterization of a novel ortholog, TmLOX13, in Taxus media, the study provides compelling evidence for the
                                 direct involvement of the 13-LOX pathway in PTX production. The observed correlation between TmLOX13
                              expression and the activation of PTX biosynthesis in T. media cell cultures underscores the biological significance
                                of this enzyme in the process. Most notably, the ability of exogenous treatment with 13-LOX/PXG-derivative
                                oxylipins to rescue the PTX-deficient phenotype of T. media cells further emphasizes the potential applications
                                of harnessing 13-LOX for improving PTX production. These results suggest that manipulating the 13-LOX
                           pathway could serve as a viable strategy to enhance PTX yield in Taxus sp. This newfound understanding of the
                              interconnectedness between 13-LOX and PTX biosynthesis provides a foundation for targeted biotechnological
                               interventions aimed at optimizing PTX production, which holds significance for pharmaceutical and medicinal
                                 applications.
