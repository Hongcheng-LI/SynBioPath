ABSTRACT: Nonribosomal peptide synthetases (NRPSs) are
sophisticated molecular machines that biosynthesize peptide drugs.
In attempts to generate new bioactive compounds, some parts of
NRPSs have been successfully manipulated, but especially the
influence of condensation (C-)domains on substrate specificity
remains enigmatic and poorly controlled. To understand the
influence of C-domains on substrate preference, we extensively
evaluated the peptide formation of C-domain mutants in a
bimodular NRPS system. Thus, we identified three key mutations
that govern the preference for stereoconfiguration and side-chain
identity. These mutations show similar effects in three different C-
domains (GrsB1, TycB1, and SrfAC) when di- or pentapeptides
are synthesized in vitro or in vivo. Strikingly, mutation E386L
allows the stereopreference to be switched from D- to L-configured donor substrates. Our findings provide valuable insights into how
cryptic specificity filters in C-domains can be re-engineered to clear roadblocks for NRPS engineering and enable the production of
novel bioactive compounds.
■INTRODUCTION
Nonribosomal peptide synthetases (NRPSs) are intricate
enzyme machineries that produce diverse peptides with varied
chemical structures and bioactivities.1 NRPSs normally consist
of multiple modules, one for each amino acid, containing
functional domains responsible for amino acid activation (A-
domain), peptide chain transport (T-domain), and peptide
bond formation (C-domain).2 The final peptide is often
released by the C-terminal thioesterase (TE) domain (Figure
1A). Engineering exploits the modular biosynthetic concept of
NRPSs to generate novel natural products. Common
manipulations are site-directed mutagenesis3,4 and swapping
of subdomains,5−7 domains,8 and modules.9,10 Greater
sequence databases obtained by next-generation sequenc-
ing11,12 and advanced cloning methods, such as CRISPR/
Cas9-mediated gene editing,7,13 have accelerated NRPS
engineering in the past decades.14−16 However, applying a
“plug-and-play” approach to NRPS modules still often results
in little or no product.
Although A-domains are widely found to be the primary
determinants of NRPS specificity, accessory substrate specific-
ity of C-domains has been proposed as one reason for
efficiency losses after engineering.20,21 Structurally, C-domains
form a V-shaped pseudodimer consisting of an N- and a C-
terminal lobe (Figure S1A).22,23 During the condensation
reaction, the substrate-loaded 4′-phosphopantetheine (Ppant)
arms from the upstream and downstream T-domains enter the
donor and acceptor sites, respectively, and converge near the
conserved “HHxxxDG” motif at the bottom of the central
valley between the lobes (Figure 1B).2 It is known about C-
domains that those following epimerization (E-)domains
precisely distinguish the L-/D-configuration of the upstream
substrate.24,25 Also, different degrees of side-chain specificity of
C-domains have been observed. While a substrate similar to
the native one often passes through the C-domain specificity
filter with ease after the A-domain preference has been
changed (e.g., Pro to Piz, Phe to β-Phe),26,27 larger structural
changes (e.g., Phe to Val) are less well tolerated.28 Notably,
broad substrate flexibility at the C-domain is one reason for the
high evolvability of NRPSs.8,15,29,30 However, in special cases,
C-domains are directly responsible for selecting the correct
building block.31 Consequently, gaining control over the C-
domain specificity is an important challenge for NRPS
engineering.
Substrate specificity, the ability of an enzyme to process
some substrates but not others, is perhaps best understood for
a subtype of the C-domain, the unusual C starter domain (Cs-
domain) that employs fatty acyl chains as donor substrates.24

Recently, crystal structures of Cs-domains bound to substrates
have been determined with the rhizomide NRPS RzmA (PDB:
7C1S).32 These structures have revealed a specific set of
residues located at the binding pocket, which are responsible
for substrate acyl chain selectivity during the lipoinitiation
process.32,33 In a separate study, the corresponding residues
were modified in the surfactin NRPS enzyme SrfAC to
incorporate non-native fatty acid substrates.34 However, the
mechanisms underlying the substrate specificity of typical C-
domains from elongation modules with amino acyl or peptidyl
substrates remain unknown. Additionally, how DCL and LCL
domains distinguish between
L- or
D-configured donor
substrates remains elusive.
Here, we investigate mutationally how C-domains from
elongation modules control substrate selectivity at the donor
site. We identify positions that recognize the side chain of the
upstream aminoacyl substrates and achieve an increased level
of incorporation of non-native substrates by mutating them.
Most notably, we reveal a crucial residue that governs the
selection of L- or D-configured substrates, which allowed us to
flip the stereospecificity of DCL domains.
■RESULTS
Multispecific Donor Module GrsA* Probes GrsB1-C-
Domain Specificity. To investigate the C-domain substrate
specificity, the well-studied gramicidin S NRPS module GrsB1
(Figure 1A) was chosen as a model system because it interacts
productively with the multispecific initiation module sdVGrsA-
STAP (named GrsA* here). In the native NRPS, the GrsB1-C-
domain (53 kDa) catalyzes the condensation reaction between
an epimerized upstream
D-Phe and a downstream
L-Pro
(Figure 1A) and therefore belongs to the DCL subtype.20 In the
GrsA*/GrsB1 system, shifts in substrate specificity can be
detected sensitively because multiple products are made as a
result of GrsA*’s multispecificity. The multispecific module
GrsA* has earlier been obtained from Phe-incorporating wild-
type GrsA through subdomain swapping with a Val-specific
module5 and introduction of stabilizing mutations.28,35 The
chimeric A-domain of GrsA* activates both the native
substrate L-Phe and the non-native substrate L-Val.5,28 The
cognate communication (COM) domain mediates non-
covalent interaction between GrsA* and GrsB1, resulting in
the production of four diketopiperazines (DKPs), FP/fP-DKP
and VP/vP-DKP, where small-case letters denote D-amino
acids (Figure 2A). In this model system, it has been observed
that the GrsB1 C-domain exhibits a preference toward its
native substrate D-Phe at the donor site,28 but it was not known
what residues cause this preference or how it can be altered.
Homology Models Reveal Relevant Residues in
GrsB1-C. To prioritize a set of residues in this protein with
potential influence on specificity, models of GrsB1-C were
constructed using SWISS-MODEL36 and AlphaFold237 and
used for molecular docking (Figure S1B). Residues hypothe-
sized to participate in protein−ligand interactions were
carefully chosen, mostly situated in the vicinity of the entry
channel surrounding the Ppant arm (Figure S2). Additional
residues were included in the analysis, either within a 5 Å
radius of the conserved H147 (center region) or located in the
putative amino acid/peptide chain binding site (Figure
2B).32,33 In total, 30 residues of the GrsB1-C-domain were
selected for mutagenesis (Table S2).

Residues F155, N256, and E386 Control Substrate
Specificity. The most impactful GrsB1 mutations, F155I,
N256A, and E386L, were investigated more closely. It is
noteworthy that these residues are not clustered together but
spread out along the cleft of the C-domain at a remarkable
distance from catalytic H147 (Figure 2D). By recording
product formation kinetics for all four DKPs (Figure 2E), it
was confirmed that these mutants exhibit markedly distinct
behaviors compared to each other and to GrsB1. In the case of
F155I, all DKPs except for FP-DKP were produced at higher
rates and with a moderately higher Val %. With N256A, the L-
form DKPs VP-DKP and FP-DKP were formed faster,
resulting in a significantly higher Val % and lower D-form %.
With E386L, the L-form DKPs became the major products
with a formation rate comparable to or higher than that of the
D-form DKPs with GrsB1, indicating a reversed stereo-
specificity.

In vivo, reaction conditions such as enzyme and substrate
concentration are more difficult to control than in vitro, but
reactions are easier to scale up.39 Hence, we studied the effect
of specificity-controlling mutations in vivo. To this end,
plasmids harboring the DNA fragment grsA*-grsB or mutants
thereof were generated and independently introduced into the
heterologous expression host HM0079. Instead of changing
one cultivation factor at a time, Design of Experiment
(DOE)40,41 was employed to optimize the cultivation
conditions, which were applied in all subsequent experiments
(Supporting Information “Experimental Procedures”, Table S4
and Figure S8). Five single colonies of each HM0079 strain
carrying grsA* and grsB, grsB-F155I, grsB-N256A, or grsB-
E386L were cultivated in parallel, and pentapeptides were
extracted from the cultures after 48 h. With mutations F155I
and N256A, only
D-form peptides were detected and the
percentage of vPVOL increased from 9% (HM0079/grsA*-
grsB) to 30% and 40%, respectively (Figure 3C). With
mutation E386L, no fPVOL or vPVOL was detected and the
major product was FPVOL. The elimination of fPVOL and
vPVOL production again confirms that E386 tightly controls
stereospecificity. In conclusion, mutations F155I, N256A, and
E386L also exert control over substrate specificity in the
complex in vivo environment.
Residues F155, N256, and E386 Play Similar Roles in
Different C-Domains. To investigate whether the positions
identified in GrsB1 would also be determinants of specificity in
other NRPSs, we replaced GrsB1 in the GrsA*/GrsB1 model
system with TycB1 (native substrate:
D-Phe) from the
tyrocidine NRPS38,42 or SrfAC (native substrate:
D-Leu)
from the surfactin NRPS.43 TycB1-C and SrfAC-C share
59% and 42% identity with GrsB1-C, respectively (Figure S9).
TycB1 and SrfAC are known to cooperate with GrsA to
produce DKP and dipeptide, respectively (Figures S10A and
S11A).44 To evaluate the mutational effects, TycB1, TycB1-
F155I, TycB1-S255A, TycB1-E385L, SrfAC, SrfAC-F155I,
SrfAC-S255A, and SrfAC-E382L were heterologously pro-
duced and purified (Figure S3). In TycB1, all three mutations
showed similar effects as the GrsB1 mutants with increased Val
% (F155I and S255A) and inverted stereopreference (E385L).
In SrfAC mutants, the increase in Val % was less pronounced,
presumably because Val-preference in the Leu-module was
higher from the beginning (Figure 3D). However, the effect of
SrfAC-E382L was again drastic, lowering the D-form % from 83
to 12%. All of the above results confirm a general role of F155,
N256, and E386 (positions in GrsB1) in substrate selection at
the donor site across different NRPS enzymes.
Importance of Residue E386 for Stereocontrol Is
Supported by Bioinformatic Analysis. Our study of three
C-domains from the phylum Firmicutes provides clear
evidence that F155 and N256 are involved in recognizing
the substrate side chain, while E386 is crucial for distinguishing
its stereoconfiguration. To gain a phylogenetically broader
perspective from a bioinformatic analysis, we extracted 2319
DCL and LCL domain sequences from MIBiG45 and analyzed
the differences at these three positions (Figures S12 and S13).
First, we tried to establish a correlation between 245 C-domain
sequences and their annotated donor substrate identity.
Seemingly, there is a significant correlation between the
residues at positions 155 (p = 0.019) and 256 (p = 0.018) and
the donor substrate (Figure S13A). However, also positions
39, 143, 145, and 326 with little mutational influence on side-
chain specificity show significant correlations with p < 0.05
(Figure S13). Most likely, the database of annotated C-domain
sequences is still too small for this type of analysis. However,
when focusing only on the stereopreference (Figures 3E and
S12), a high degree of conservation at position 386 was seen in
DCL domains compared to LCL sequences. In the DCL domains,
the prevalent residues at position 386 are Glu and Asp (Figure
3E). Three signature sequences (WebLogos “WL1”, “WL2”,
and “WL3”) have been used to identify the DCL and LCL
subgroups in the C-domain family.46 For position 386, which is
part of WL3, our results confirm a functional significance and
demonstrate the potential for engineering the stereopreference
through single point mutation.
■DISCUSSION
NRPS engineering has made significant progress due to the
increase in knowledge of the underlying mechanisms and the
rapid development of new technologies.2,14−16 However, while
A-domains have been effectively repurposed in several
cases,30,47 C-domains have remained more enigmatic. Even
for the conserved His it is still under debate whether it
deprotonates the α-amino group of the substrate at the
acceptor site,48 serves for the electrostatic stabilization of the
putative reaction intermediate,49 or helps substrate position-
ing.50 Conceptual limitations hinder the investigation of the
catalytic mechanism and substrate specificity. The dynamic
three-dimensional structure of the C-domain with its two
halves enclosing the Ppant arms poses a challenge in obtaining
defined complexes for crystallography. C-domains may not
have felt evolutionary pressure to develop the same over-
whelming catalytic efficiencies and transition-state affinities,
which go along with high substrate specificity, as typical
enzymes because the reaction barely needs catalysis. Both
substrates are covalently bound to the enzyme, and the acyl
donor is strongly preactivated as thioester. Consequently, in
contrast to the well-defined A-domain specificity code that

engineers can rewrite for new substrates, a similar specificity
code for the C-domain has not been discovered.24 Although
there is support for C-domains interfering with substrate
selection,20,21,31,50−53 other studies have questioned its role as
a proofreader.29,30,54,55 The weak and poorly defined
interactions between the C-domain and substrates are not
only difficult to characterize but also even more difficult to
engineer.
To learn C-domain engineering, we screened for mutations
that affect product specificity. For this purpose, we analyzed
274 mutants of the GrsB1-C-domain, covering the entry
channel, active center, and putative binding site. Our results
show that some mutations can improve specificity for a non-
native substrate, irrespective of their locations in the entry
channel (N256) or binding site (F155, E386; Figure 2D).
Similar to R148A in RzmA-Cs,32 replacing F155 with smaller
residues, such as Leu or Ile, likely creates more space for the
donor substrate and thus increases the Val preference.
Position N256 would not immediately be suspected to
impact C-domain specificity, as it is distant (∼23 Å) from the
catalytic H147 in the active center. N256 is located at the
beginning of an α-helix (residue L255−N271) and likely
contacts the Ppant arm (Figures 2D and S2). According to
amino acid sequences and 3D structure alignment, R243 in
RzmA-Cs (PDB: 7C1S)32 and F1060 in AmbB-C (PDB:
7X0F)56 occupy the same position. R243 forms a hydrogen
bond with the phosphate moiety of the substrate octanoyl-
CoA,32 while F1060 contributes to the protein−ligand
interaction by forming a hydrophobic pocket around the
dimethyl moiety of Ppant.56 In contrast, Y1015 in the LgrA-C-
domain (PDB: 6MFW)18 shares the same position but does
not participate in Ppant binding. Therefore, the exact
functional role of N256 is uncertain. It is conceivable that
position 256 distinguishes the donor substrates not during the
condensation reaction in the active site but while the loaded
Ppant is threaded into the C-domain tunnel.57
Compared to the Val %, the D-form % generally does not
shift dramatically, except when position E386 is mutated
(Figure S6D). Especially in vivo, mutation E386L abolishes
products containing the D-configured residue, which are the
only products without the mutation. E386 is situated at the
end of the latch loop and aligns with S386 in CDA-C1(E17C)
from calcium-dependent antibiotic synthetase (PDB: 5DU9).50
The α-amino group of the substrate at the acceptor site forms
hydrogen bonds with the ε-nitrogen of the critical histidine
and the backbone carbonyl group of S386 in CDA-C1. Thus, it
appears that the carbonyl group at position 386 plays an
essential role in recognizing the
L-/D-configuration of the
substrate. Bioinformatic analysis of the currently available DCL
and LCL domain sequences further endorses the importance of
negatively charged residues at this position. It is worth noting
that Glu and Asp are even more conserved (87.3%) at this
position in dual functional C/E domains (Figure S12B). In our
3D model of GrsB1-C, the side-chain carboxyl group of E386
approaches the active center of the C-domain. In one scenario,
this carboxyl group may interfere with the amino group of the
L-configured substrate in the donor site, resulting in
mispositioning of the substrate and suspension of peptide
bond formation.
The optimistic lesson we draw for NRPS engineering is that
C-domain specificity, if it interferes with the formation of a
new product, can be improved by mutagenesis, even if it
remains structurally ill-defined. Similar mutagenesis campaigns
will most likely be able to remove obstacles to peptide
formation in other engineered NRPS systems. In the
gramicidin S NRPS, the effects of C-domain mutation were
particularly striking when the entire synthetase was used
instead of excised modules (Figure 3B,C). A single mutation,
N256A, has increased the Val % associated with the GrsA*/
GrsB system 10-fold. However, this mutation is 23 Å from
catalytic H147 and apparently exerts a long-range effect that
would be difficult to predict in advance. With our initial alanine
scanning strategy, we show how such scattered hotspots of
mutagenesis can be identified with minimal effort.
In summary, we demonstrate how the substrate specificity at
the donor site of C-domains can be modified through
mutagenesis. Importantly, a key residue has been identified
that allows conversion of a DCL into an LCL domain. Our
results not only extend the knowledge of the substrate
specificity of C-domains but also show the potential for
manipulating C-domains to enhance the formation of non-
natural peptides in search of new bioactive compounds.
