ABSTRACT: Nature remains a vast repository of complex and functional
metabolites whose structural characterization continues to drive innovations in
pharmaceuticals, agrochemicals, and materials science. The cryogenic electron
microscopy (cryoEM) method, microcrystal electron diffraction (microED, a
3D ED technique), has emerged as a powerful tool to structurally characterize
small molecules. Despite this emerging role in structural chemistry, the cost and
throughput of microED have limited its application in the discovery of natural
products (NPs). While recent advances in sample preparation (e.g., arrayED)
have provided a conceptual framework to address these challenges, they have
remained unproven. Herein, we report the arrayED-driven discovery of a
structurally unprecedented family of NPs (zopalides A−E), a muurolane-type sesquiterpene glycoside (rhytidoside A), aspergillicin
analogs (aspergillicins H and I), and four crystal structures of previously reported fungal metabolites. Lastly, this the first time that
the absolute stereo configuration of newly discovered NPs has been determined directly by microED alone without other methods
using a small amount of sample
■INTRODUCTION
From the seminal total synthesis of urea1 to the discovery of
penicillin as an antibiotic drug,2 natural products (NPs) have
played a critical role in biology, chemistry, and medicine.
Indeed, NPs remain instrumental in modern medicine,
comprising or inspiring >50% of on-market drugs.3 Despite
this monumental societal impact, the discovery of structurally
unique natural products has slowed over the last several
decades.4,5 While approximately 400,000 natural products have
been discovered to date, less than 1% of earth’s biodiversity has
been investigated.6 Many modern programs in NP discovery
utilize a bioactivity-guided strategy, where fractionated NP
extracts are subjected to biological assays and promising
fractions are prioritized for further isolation, purification, and
structural characterization.7 Other strategies are structure-
driven, where mass spectrometry (MS) or nuclear magnetic
resonance (NMR) spectra collected from fractionated extracts
are utilized to identify unannotated and potentially new NPs.8,9
More recently, genome-mining approaches rely on bioinfor-
matics to identify unique biosynthetic gene clusters that could
lead to new NPs (Figure 1).10
For bioactivity-, structure-, and genome mining-guided
discovery campaigns, the structural elucidation of nascent
NPs often limits throughput. Despite advances in modern
analytical methods, combinations of NMR spectroscopy, MS,
infrared (IR) spectroscopy, circular dichroism (CD), and
ultraviolet−visible (UV−vis) spectrophotometry data are

while the ab initio determination of stereochemistry relies on
single-crystal X-ray diffraction (SCXRD). These methods often
require milligram quantities of purified analyte and prior
knowledge of the analyte.
MicroED, or 3D ED, has emerged as a complementary
analytical approach that can address these limitations,11−19
allowing for ab initio structural solutions from nanogram
quantities of analyte and, in some cases, the determination of
absolute stereochemistry.20,21 MicroED has been successfully
implemented in traditional NP-discovery workflows, providing
the structures of NPs identified in bioassay-,22−24 struc-
tural-,25,26 or bioinformatics-guided27−33 approaches. However,
it can take an hour or more to load and remove a single sample
from a TEM, limiting throughput. With existing curated
libraries of uncharacterized, bioactive NPs containing a million
or more samples,34,35 state-of-the-art microED is too expensive
and too slow to address vast quantities of known unknowns in
a meaningful way, let alone untapped NP space.
Our group recently reported the arrayED workflow, where
noncontact printing is used to deposit >100 samples onto a
single TEM grid, enabling high-throughput (HT) screening for
crystalline natural products amenable to microED (Figure
1B).36 Herein, we report the application of arrayED in a
diffraction-guided NP discovery campaign, departing from
bioassay-, structural-, or genome mining-guided approaches.
Our diffraction-guided approach is applied to the study of two
fungal extracts, enabling the discovery of an unprecedented
family of NP zopalides A−E (1−5) (Figures 2 and 3) and a
muurolane-type sesquiterpene glycoside rhytidoside A (6)
(Figure 4), with each having its absolute configuration
established through dynamical refinement of microED data
alone without other methods and utilizing a small amount of
sample. Moreover, this workflow has enabled the full
characterization of two new cyclic depsipeptide NPs:
aspergillicin I (7), whose discovery would have proven
challenging without a crystallographic model given the
structural similarity to aspergillicin A, and aspergillicin H (8)
(Figure 5). This screening strategy lays the groundwork for
diffraction-guided NP discovery, which is poised to advance
metabolomics and medicine.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Initial efforts were focused on fungal strains TTI-0151 and
TTI-0118 isolated from dead twigs and cow dung, respectively,
during a field collection (Texas, USA). Morphological
characteristics and rDNA sequence analysis identified TTI-
0151 as Rhytidhysteron hysterinum and TTI-0118 as an
undescribed Zopfiella sp. (details in the SI). The mycelial
extracts of R. hysterinum and Zopfiella sp. were subjected to
generalized HPLC to generate 96 time-resolved fractions. The
microarray of each extract was then screened in a TEM for the
presence of microcrystalline particles, whose exhibited
diffraction was then binned as “hits”.
Discovery of the Zopalide NP Family Derived from
Zopfiella sp. ArrayED screening of 203 mg of an extract
isolated from the mycelial culture of Zopfiella sp. identified
wells C7 and C9 as “hits”. Deposition of these fractions onto
individual grids revealed several crystals with high-quality
diffraction (Figure 2A) and allowed for the collection of
multiple continuous rotation data sets. The diffraction data sets
were automatically processed with in-house package AutoPro-
cess.37 Merging of three data sets from fraction C7 of Zopfiella
sp. and ab initio phasing using kinematical approaches yielded
scaffold A1 (Figure 2B) in space group P212121 (92.6%
completeness at 0.8 Å resolution, R1 18.49%) (Figure S8).
Initial similarity searches for preliminary scaffold A1 in public
databases (Reaxys and Dictionary of Natural Products) yielded
no exact structural matches. To elucidate the molecular
structure, we obtained high-resolution ESI-MS data to
determine the molecular formula of scaffold A1, as similar

Further HPLC-MS analysis of well C7 revealed a complex
mixture of related congeners (Figure 2C). From well C7, we
annotated five UV-active peaks corresponding to the following
molecular formulas: [C22H26O9 + H]+, [C22H26O9 + H]+,
[C24H28O10 + H]+, [C24H28O10 + H]+, and [C22H23O8 + H]+.
We leveraged these data to assign the scaffold with atom
identities and bond unsaturation to yield proposed structure 1
(Figure 2B). Kinematical refinement in space group P212121
provided high confidence in our assignment, with 93.6%
completeness at 0.8 Å resolution and R1 14.92%. We named
compound 1 zopalide A (1).39 We then set out to establish the
absolute configuration of zopalide A by utilizing dynamical
refinement. In this approach, the structural models of each
enantiomer are refined against a dynamical reflection file
containing information on multiple scattering events occurring
between the electron beam and the crystal. The correct
enantiomer is assigned based on the structural model yielding
lower residual factors and a higher Z-score after refine-
ment.20,21 The results determined the absolute stereochemistry
of zopalide A (1), as depicted in Figure 2B. A Z-score of 11.1σ
obtained from the methods of Klarr and co-workers supports
this assignment (Figure S10 and Table S13).20
A comparison of the refined structure of zopalide A (1) with
those of public structural databases failed to yield an exact
match. Given that this is the first NP to be characterized
entirely by microED, we were compelled to validate this
finding using traditional methods. A poor isolated yield (0.4
mg/L) of compound 1 from the mycelial extract required
several rounds of scaled-up fermentation of Zopfiella sp. to

With scaled-up fermentation in hand, we pursued the
structural characterization of the isomeric components
identified earlier in HPLC-MS studies of well C7. Isomers
2−4 were isolated and characterized using traditional methods
(HPLC-MS and NMR). Compound 2 was identified as a
structural isomer of 1 and was named zopalide B (Table S2).
Compounds 3 and 4 were named zopalide C (3) and zopalide
D (4) (Tables S3 and S4) and are deacetylated analogs of
metabolites 1 and 2 (Figure 2C), respectively. NMR spectra
from a m/z 477 isomer appeared to have few analogous
chemical shifts to spectra recorded from zopalides A−D (1−4)
(Table S5). Microcrystals obtained by slow evaporation
yielded microED scaffold B1 that would later be refined to
reveal the structure of zopalide E (5) (Figure 3A).
Zopalide E (5) is proposed to be an intramolecular oxa-
Michael addition product of zopalide B (2) (Scheme S1)
representing the most structurally complex member of the
Zopalide family. Various stereocenters in compound 5, notably
the stereocenter appending the acetylcyclohexanone to the
fused ring system, were observed to be inverted relative to
compound 2. To elucidate the absolute configuration, we
utilized dynamical refinement where structural models of both
enantiomorphs of compound 5 were refined against the
dynamical reflection files. Results of dynamical refinement
(Figure 3B) determined the absolute stereochemistry of
zopalide E (5) as depicted in Figure 3A.

electron diffraction as a robust method for the structural
elucidation of novel natural products without structural
precedent.
Discovery and Isolation of Muurolane-Type Sesqui-
terpene Glycoside Derived from Rhytidhysteron hyster-
inum. With this success, we applied arrayED screening to 88
mg of crude extract isolated from the mycelium of
Rhytidhysteron hysterinum. We observed crystalline particles
in 24 of the 96 microarrayed fractions (Figure 4A). Deposition
of the best diffracting particles, obtained from well D8, onto a
TEM grid yielded several continuous rotation data sets.
Merging two of the highest-quality data sets yielded
preliminary scaffold C1 (Figure 4B) in space group P21 with
87.2% completeness at 1.0 Å resolution (R1 34.08%). A
structural database search returned several substructure
matches to muurolane-type sesquiterpene glycosides but no
exact matches. HPLC-MS analysis of well D8 revealed a
heterogeneous mixture containing at least seven detectable
analytes (Figure S15,i).
We later matched our preliminary solution to the m/z
407.2305 [M + Na]+ peak. Following bond length and bond
angle analyses, refinement of C1 to space group P21 revealed a
new NP, which we have named rhytidoside A (6) (86.4%
completeness, 0.8 Å, R1 13.06%) (Figure 4B). Dynamical
refinement established the absolute stereochemistry of rhytido-
side A (6) as shown with a Z score of 2.8σ (Figure S17).
Subsequent regrowth of R. hysterinum allowed for the
reisolation of compound 6 through HPLC-MS with a yield
sufficient to provide NMR confirmation of the proposed
structure (Table S6). Notably, a comparison of the refined
structure 6 against published muurolane-type sesquiterpene
glycosides revealed structural similarities with the balanoindi-
cosides isolated from the plant Balanophora fungosa subspecies
Indica.40
Isolation and Characterization of Aspergillus sp.
Derived Metabolites. Encouraged by the discovery of the
zopalide family (1−5) and rhytidoside A (6), we set out to
screen fractions generated from the mycelial extract of
Aspergillus sp. (Figure 5A). Initial screening revealed various
crystalline particles that were of poor diffraction quality or
particles that were observed to be beam-sensitive, thus
precluding a direct methods solution. Subsequent purification
and analysis through HPLC-MS yielded six unique fractions:
m/z 763.4101 [M + Na]+ (Figure 5B, trace (i)), m/z 793.4423
[M + Na]+ (Figure 5B, trace (ii)), m/z 777.4431 [M + Na]+
(Figure 5B, trace (iii)), m/z 763.4042 [M + Na]+ (Figure 5B,
trace (iv)), m/z 337.1545 [M + H]+ (Figure 5B, trace (v)),
and m/z 263.1242 [M + Na]+ (Figure 5B, trace (vi)).
Structural characterization of two of the six purified fractions
revealed two new secondary metabolites, aspergillicin H (7)
and aspergillicin I (8). Moreover, within this regrowth extract,
microED structures of previously reported aspergillicin A (9),
aspergillicin F (10),41 cyclopiazonic acid (11),42 aspertamar-
inolide B (12),43,44 and aspertamarinolide D (13)45 were
determined (Figure 6). All of the isolated metabolites in Figure
5, aside from 8, yielded microED structures and were
subsequently validated through HPLC-MS and NMR (Tables
S6−S10). Compounds 12 and 13 were coisolated and
characterized in cocrystal form (Figure S18).
■CONCLUSIONS
Utilization of the arrayED screening workflow enabled the
discovery of nine previously undescribed NPs (compounds 1−
8) and the first crystal structure of four previously reported
NPs (compounds 9−13). It is notable that accurately
annotating the structure of cyclic peptides such as aspergillicin
A and aspergillicin I, where the relative positions of the valine
and isoleucine are interchanged, would be intractable using
state-of-the-art structural elucidation methodology. Moreover,
the absolute stereochemistry of several of these NPs was
determined by using dynamical refinement on microED data
without necessitating other analytical methods. Although the
arrayED workflow selects compounds with a high propensity
to crystallize, this work exemplifies how electron diffraction can
be used to direct NP isolation and discovery unbiased to
biosynthetic origin, structure, polarity, or activity. It is
important to note that the rapid evaporation of the picoliter
droplets severely limits the crystallization space sampled
throughout the screening method, which may prove challeng-
ing for some NPs. Nevertheless, given that several new NPs
were discovered with as little as 88 mg of extract, this study
paves the way for a powerful application of diffraction science
in NP discovery. Efforts are currently underway to synthesize
and evaluate the bioactivities of these NPs.
