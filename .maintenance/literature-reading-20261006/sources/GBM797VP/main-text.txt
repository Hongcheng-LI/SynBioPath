ABSTRACT: Depsidones are structurally diverse polyketides with significant pharmacological potential. Their structural variability
in the marine-derived fungus Aspergillus sp. SCSIO SX7S7 stems from the substrate promiscuity of key enzymes, particularly the
starter-unit acyltransferase (SAT) domain of non-reducing polyketide synthase (NR-PKS). In this study, we combined a metabolic
blockade with the inherent biosynthetic flexibility to access rare depsidone derivatives. Inactivation of the highly reducing polyketide
synthase (HR-PKS) gene depD redirected metabolic flux to potential branching pathways, leading to the isolation of (i) aspergillol A
(1), an unprecedented depsidone featuring a unique benzene substitution, (ii) four new orsellinic acid homodimer-derived
depsidones aspergillol B−E (2−5), (iii) a new diphenyl ether derivative aspergillol F (6), and (iv) two known compounds (7 and 8)
but lacking complete NMR data from a previous publication. Further structure−activity relationship analyses revealed that the ester
linkage in compounds 1−5 is essential for antimicrobial activities, while the distinctive benzene extension in compound 1 is
responsible for its significantly enhanced activity. These findings not only expand the known structural diversity of fungal depsidones
but also reveal the complex metabolic network underlying their biosynthesis while establishing an effective approach for discovering
bioactive natural product scaffolds through rational pathway manipulation.
D
epsidones are a class of polyketide-derived aromatic
compounds featuring two aromatic units bridged by a
seven-membered lactone (ester) and a diaryl ether (C−O−C)
linkage.1 Depsidones are widely distributed in nature,
predominantly found in lichens and fungi, exhibiting
considerable structural diversity due to variations in aromatic
substitution patterns and oxidative modifications.2,3 The
pharmacological significance of depsidones has gained
substantial research interest due to their diverse bioactivities,
including antibacterial, antitumor, anti-inflammatory, and
various human enzyme−inhibitory activities.4−8 Consequently,
the structural diversification of the depsidone scaffold
represents a promising strategy for expanding the bioactive
chemical space in medicinal chemistry and drug discovery.
Most depsidone scaffolds originate from orsellinic acid, a key
precursor synthesized by non-reducing polyketide synthase
(NR-PKS) through the sequential condensation of one acetyl-
CoA starter unit with three malonyl-CoA extender units,
followed by aldol cyclization and aromatization (Figure 1).9
Additionally, the starter-unit acyltransferase (SAT) domain of
NR-PKS can exhibit substrate promiscuity, accepting alter-
native starter units, such as fatty acyl-CoA or polyketide
intermediates, from other PKS systems, thereby generating
structural variants, such as aspergillusphenol A carboxylic acid
(Figure 1).10 These subunits can undergo homodimerization

In our previous investigation of the marine-derived fungus,
Aspergillus sp. SCSIO SX7S7, we characterized a series of
chlorinated depsidones (including nidulin and related
analogues) that incorporate both orsellinic acid and aspergillu-
sphenol A carboxylic acid subunits.10 Intriguingly, only
heterodimeric combinations (aspergillusphenol A−orsellinic
acid) and aspergillusphenol A homodimers were observed,
with no orsellinic acid homodimers detected (Figure 1). This
biosynthetic preference is consistent with the findings in
Aspergillus unguis,11 despite orsellinic acid homodimers,
namely, diphenyl ethers, occurring in Aspergillus nidulans.12
Precursor feeding experiments with orsellinic acid or related
salicylates in A. unguis failed to yield nidulin analogues or
orsellinic acid homodimers.13 However, heterologous expres-
sion of the nidulin biosynthesis gene depD (encoding HR-PKS
for 1-methyl-1-propenyl unit production) and/or depH
(encoding NR-PKS for depside production) from Aspergillus
sp. SCSIO SX7S7 in A. nidulans indicated that NR-PKSDepH
is a promiscuious enzyme possessing the capacity to form
orsellinic acid homodimers but with very low efficiency.10
To redirect biosynthesis toward underrepresented orsellinic
acid homodimer-derived metabolites in Aspergillus sp. SCSIO
SX7S7, we employed a metabolic blockade, a strategy that
enhances low-abundance products by disrupting competing
pathways.14 In this study, the inactivation of depD, a gene
responsible for synthesizing the 1-methyl-1-propenyl unit,
blocked the supply of the 1-methyl-1-propenyl-CoA precursor.
This manipulation prompted NR-PKS DepH to utilize less
favored starter units, such as acetyl-CoA and salicyl-CoA. The
metabolic blockade, combined with extended cultivation,
successfully shifted the metabolic flux, yielding four new
depsidones (2−5) and a new diphenyl ether (6), all derived
from orsellinic acid homodimers as anticipated. Unexpectedly,
we also identified an unprecedented depsidone, aspergillol A
(1), which possesses a unique benzene substitution in its
orsellinic acid moiety. Notably, compound 1 demonstrated the
most potent antibacterial activity against Gram-positive strains
among the isolated metabolites, highlighting its potential as a
promising antimicrobial lead compound.

Compound 1 was isolated as a white powder, and its
molecular formula was established as C22H15Cl3O5 on the basis
of HRESIMS at m/z 462.9922 [M −H]−, indicating the
presence of 14 degrees of unsaturation (Figure S1). The 1H
NMR (Table 1) data presented signals characteristic of two
methyls at δH 2.36 (3H, s) and δH 2.42 (3H, s) and three
characteristic aromatic proton signals at δH 7.30 (2H, m), δH
7.41 (1H, m), and δH 7.44 (2H, m), indicating the existence of
a monosubstituted benzene in the structure. The 1H and 13C
NMR spectral data of 1 are similar to those of vicanicin,15
except for the difference in the C-6′ substituent and the
number of chlorine atoms, suggesting that 1 possessed the
same parent system and esterification pattern as that of
vicanicin. The HMBC correlations between H3-8 and C-1, C-5,
and C-6 revealed the similar structure of 1 and vicanicin ring A
(Figure 3). The HMBC correlations from H3-8′ to C-1′, C-2′,
C-3′, and C-4′ confirmed the existence of ring B, another
moiety in vicanicin. In addition, the HMBC correlations from
H3-9′ to C-2′ proved the methoxy substitution of C-2′ as in
vicanicin (Figures S2−S5). Furthermore, comparative analysis
of the 13C NMR chemical shifts between 1 and vicanicin
revealed that C-6′ showed a 6.4 ppm downfield shift compared
to the methylated counterpart, reflecting aromatic ring
substitution, while C-3′ resonated 3.5 ppm upfield relative to
its methylated analogue, indicating the electron-withdrawing
effect of chlorine. Although biphenyl derivatives typically
exhibit multiple conformations due to free rotation around the
central C−C single bond, compound 1 displayed only one set
of NMR signals, indicating rapid interconversion of conformers
on the NMR time scale and the absence of significant
rotational barriers. Additional information about 1 was
achieved via X-ray crystallographic studies. The suitable
crystals of 1 were obtained from a methanol solution. Analysis
of the single-crystal X-ray diffraction data further confirmed the
structure of 1 (Figure 2B). Therefore, the structure of 1 was
finally established and named aspergillol A.
Compound 2 was obtained as a white powder, with a
molecular formula of C15H9Cl3O5 (with 10 degrees of
unsaturation) as deduced from the [M −H]−ion at m/z
372.9449 of HRESIMS (Figure S7). The fact of 7 carbon
atoms and 6 hydrogen atoms less than compound 1 indicated
the lack of benzene and methyl in the structure, which was
further corroborated by the 1H NMR (Table 1) data and
comparative 13C NMR analysis. The 1H NMR data displayed
signals for two methyls at δH 2.35 (3H, s) and δH 2.51 (3H, s)
and one aromatic proton at δH 6.80 (1H, s). The 13C NMR
comparison between compounds 1 and 2 demonstrated the
disappearance of the C-3′ methyl group, as indicated by the
upfield shift of C-3′ from δC 125.9 to 105.2, while all other
carbon signals remained essentially unchanged. Additionally,
the absence of a benzene was further confirmed by the upfield
shift of C-6′ from δC 134.0 to 129.3 ppm, consistent with the
loss of aromatic conjugation effects. Furthermore, the HMBC
correlations between H3-8 and C-1, C-5, and C-6 revealed the
same structure of ring A of aspergillol A in compound 2. The
HMBC correlations from H3-8′ to C-1′, C-4′, C-5′, and C-6′
and from H-3′ to C-1′, C-2′, C-4′, and C-5′ confirmed the
existence of ring B, another moiety in 1 (Figures S8−S11).
Therefore, the structure of compound 2 was finally established
and named aspergillol B.

To evaluate the antimicrobial potential of compounds 1−8,
we conducted systematic bioassays against a panel of clinically
relevant Gram-positive pathogens. The test strains comprised
Staphylococcus aureus 3090, S. aureus 16162, S. aureus ATCC
29213, Enterococcus gallinarum 5F52C, S. aureus 718306, S.
aureus 16339, S. aureus (MRSA) shhs-A1, Enterococcus faecium
5203, S. aureus 991, Micrococcus luteus ML01, and Bacillus
subtilis BS01.
The results demonstrate that most of the tested compounds
exhibit MIC values of ≤64 μg/mL against Gram-positive
bacteria, except for compound 6 (Table 2). To further
understand how the structural characteristics of 1−8 correlate
to the observed antimicrobial activities, we analyzed the
structure−activity relationships (SARs) in detail (Figure 4).
Comparative analysis of the MIC data revealed that the ester
linkage between rings A and B is critical for its antimicrobial
activities, while the scaffold with an ether bond connection
(compound 6) showed markedly reduced potency. This
observation is consistent with previous reports on depsi-
dones.19 Meanwhile, the structural and activity differences
between compounds 2, 3, 4, and 7 showed that the chlorine
substitution at C-1′ greatly improves antimicrobial activities.
However, the C-3′ chlorine substitution in compound 8
decreased antimicrobial activity by 4−16-fold compared to
compound 2. Notably, while both 3-chlorine (2 and 7) and 4-
chlorine (5 and 8) substitutions conferred better activity than
their 2-chlorine counterparts, compounds with 3-chlorine
substitutions exhibited greater activity than those with 4-
chlorine substitutions. This observation aligns with previous
reports indicating that depsidones bearing both 3-chlorine and
C-3′ methyl substitutions exhibit optimal antibacterial
activity.20 Collectively, these findings demonstrate that (i)
antibacterial activity does not always correlate with the number
of chlorine substitutions and (ii) the position of functional
groups critically influences activity, as evidenced by the 2−8-
fold activity enhancement conferred by a C-4 methoxy group
compared to its ineffective C-2′ counterpart.
Compound 1 exhibited more potent antibacterial activity
than other compounds isolated in this study, indicating that
the C-2′ constituent contribute to the bioactivity. As the first
reported natural product featuring this depside-like scaffold
with an additional benzene moiety, we hypothesize that the
biosynthetic pathway initiates with salicyl-CoA delivered by
NR-PKS (Figure 1). This unit is likely recognized and loaded
onto the assembly line by the SAT domain of DepH,
representing a branching pathway from canonical depsidone
biosynthesis. This finding not only expands our understanding
of DepH’s SAT domain substrate promiscuity but also
demonstrates its remarkable flexibility in processing structur-
ally diverse precursors, potentially enabling the discovery of
additional structural variants.

Fungus Strain and Fermentation. Aspergillus sp. SCSIO SX7S7
ΔdepD was an engineered strain of Aspergillus sp. SCSIO SX7S7,
which was isolated from a coral Acropora sp. collected from the
Mischief Island, South China Sea, in 2018.20 The ΔdepD mutant was
constructed as previously described.10 Briefly, a sgRNA targeting the
polyketide synthase gene depH was designed and cloned into a Cas9
expression plasmid carrying an auxotrophic marker (pBSKII-toCas9-
pyrG). The resulting plasmid was then introduced into protoplasts,
and transformants were selected on uridine/uracil-deficient agar
plates. Putative mutants were randomly selected and validated by
PCR using primers flanking the sgRNA target site.
In this work, mutant strain ΔdepD was cultured onto PDA plates
and incubated at 28 °C for 3 days to produce spores. The spores were
inoculated into a 250 mL conical flask containing 100 mL of PDB
medium and incubated on a rotary shaker at 200 rpm and 28 °C for 2
days as seed culture. The seed culture was transferred into 168 1 L
flasks with 200 mL of PDB medium (total of 33.6 L) and incubated
on a rotary shaker (200 rpm) at 28 °C for 21 days.
Extraction and Isolation of Compounds 1−8. The mycelium
and supernatant were separated from 33.6 L of fermentation solution
by atmospheric filtration. The supernatant was extracted with 30 L of
butanone 3 times. Similarly, the mycelium was extracted with 5 L of
acetone 3 times. The extracts were combined according to HPLC−
DAD analysis to obtain the crude extract. The crude extract was
subjected to open column chromatography (CC) on a silica gel
(100−200 mesh) column (50 × 3 cm inner diameter). Elution was
performed with a stepwise gradient of CHCl3/MeOH (from 1:0−0:1,
v/v), yielding 10 fractions (fractions 1−10). Fractions 1 and 2 were
combined and further separated by silica gel column chromatography
(200−300 mesh, 30 × 1.7 cm inner diameter). Elution with a gradient
of petroleum ether/EtOAc (7:3−0:10, v/v) yielded eight subfractions
(fractions 1.1−1.8). Due to the large number of depsidone
compounds in fraction 1.1, it is difficult to isolate monomeric
compound directly; therefore, we further reduced the polarity of the
mobile phase. Therefore, fraction 1.1 was subjected to silica gel
column chromatography (200−300 mesh, 40 × 1 cm inner diameter).
Elution with a gradient of petroleum ether/EtOAc (1:0−7:3, v/v)
yielded 10 subfractions (fractions 1.1.1−1.1.10). However, owing to
the incomplete resolution of components in fractions 1.1.5 and 1.1.6,
these fractions were combined and subsequently rechromatographed
on a silica gel column (200−300 mesh, 30 × 0.7 cm inner diameter).
Purification was achieved using a stepwise gradient of petroleum
ether/EtOAc (85:15−7:3, v/v), yielding 10 final subfractions
(fractions 1.1.5.1−1.1.5.10). Compound 4 (4.5 mg, tR = 19.4 min)
was purified from fraction 1.1.3 by semi-preparative HPLC (MeCN−
H2O). Compounds 1 (8.9 mg, tR = 21.4 min) and 5 (9.1 mg, tR = 20.6
min) were both purified from fraction 1.1.4 by semi-preparative
HPLC (MeCN−H2O). Compound 6 (5.6 mg, tR = 18.8 min) was
purified from fraction 1.1.7 by semi-preparative HPLC (MeCN−
H2O). Compound 3 (6 mg, tR = 16.2 min) was purified from fraction
1.1.8 by semi-preparative HPLC (MeCN−H2O). Compounds 2 (33.3
mg, tR = 17.6 min), 7 (9.1 mg, tR = 20.0 min), and 8 (6.8 mg, tR =
18.6 min) were purified from fraction 1.1.5.4 by semi-preparative
HPLC (MeCN−H2O).

Antibacterial Assays. The antimicrobial activities of compounds
1−8 against a panel of Gram-negative bacteria were evaluated using
the microbroth dilution method.20 All tested strains were incubated in
Mueller−Hinton (MH) broth for 16 h at 37 °C with shaking (200
rpm). Test compounds were dissolved in DMSO and serially diluted
to concentrations spanning 64−0.125 μg/mL, with vancomycin and
ampicillin serving as positive controls. Bacterial suspensions were then
inoculated into 96-well plates and co-cultured with gradient
concentrations of the compounds for 18 h at 37 °C. All assays were
performed in three independent replicates.
