Ligand-based target prediction (MSI level 1)
MSI level 1 analysis enables ligand-based target prediction, which requires deﬁned molecular
structures as inputs [15]. Traditionally, the chemical similarity of ligands to known drugs h as
served as the main inference route [34]. More recently, the Natural Products Magnetic Resonance
Database—which contains more than 35 000 NMR-characterized NPs —enables rapid
dereplication and novelty assessment before resource-intensive target identiﬁcation campaigns
[35]. Bayesian machine learning frameworks further enhance discovery efﬁciency. Active
learning approaches have guided large-scale drug combination screening by iteratively selecting
maximally informat ive experiments, improving predictive power while reducing experimental
burden [63].


Structure-based molecular docking and dynamics (MSI level 1)
Full structural conﬁrmation at MSI level 1 enables the most data-demanding strategies in the pipeline:
structure-based docking and molecular dynamics simulation. AI-enhanced docking frameworks improve
pose prediction and binding affinity estimation compared with conventional physics-based scoring,
and, when coupled with molecular dynamics, allow researchers to evaluate the stability of predicted binding
modes under physiological conditions. For example, integrated docking and molecular dynamics analysis
of bioactive compounds from royal jelly identiﬁed stable complexes with breast cancer-associated kinase
and estrogen receptor targets [37].


For example, AI-assisted docking pipelines identiﬁed murE inhibitors in Tropheryma whipplei,
incorporating solubility considerations t o reﬁne candidate selection during antibacterial drug
discovery [38]. In another application, structure-guided modeling was utilized to evaluate target
binding and membrane interactions of antimicrobial peptides from Wuchua nin-A1, enabling
efﬁcient candidate prioritization before experimental validation [39].


Biotransformation prediction (cross-tier)
Biotransformation prediction illustrates a key principle of the data-quality-guided framework: the
same analytical task—predicting metabolic fate—yields fundamentally different levels of conﬁdence
depending on the MSI tier at which it is performed. This principle has been demonstrated in regulatory
toxicology, where MSI identiﬁcation conﬁdence directly determines whether metabolomics data can
support chemical grouping, pathway-based assessment, or formal regulatory submission [46].


At MSI level 1, predictions rely on conﬁrmed structures and are well grounded for translational
application. Certain tools, such as BioTransformer, enable systematic prediction of gut micro-
bial and hepatic metabolic fates, supporting bioactive metabolite identiﬁcation and prodrug strat-
egy development [64]. The integrated application of BioTransformer alongside GNINA-based
molecular docking and Assisted Model Building with Energy Reﬁnement (AMBER) MD simula-
tions has been demonstrated in a recent MSI level 1 validation study of cannabisin A and B,
conﬁrming anti-diabetic target interactions and metabolic fate predictions for structurally
conﬁrmed NP scaffolds [65].

chemical resolution for reliable hypothesis generation. For example, TransDiscovery integrates

Figure 1. Decision tree for AI-assisted natural product development based on analytical resolution. This framework guides the selection of AI strategies
according to the maximum achievable chemical resolution determined by available analytical infrastructure, explicitly aligning computational complexity with
Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI) identiﬁcation conﬁdence levels. High-resolution workﬂows (green), supported by 1D/2D NMR (optionally integrated with MS),
correspond to MSI level 1 and enable deﬁnitive structural conﬁrmation. In this context, structure-dependent AI platforms—including ligand-based target prediction, AI-
enhanced molecular docking, biotransformation prediction, and ADMET optimization—can be applied with high conﬁdence for pharmaceutical development.
Intermediate-resolution workﬂows (blue), primarily based on LC–MS/MS or GC–MS data, correspond to MSI levels 2–3 and provide putative or tentative compound
identiﬁcation. At this resolution, molecular networking and spectral similarity-based AI approaches (e.g., GNPS, SIRIUS, and CSI:FingerID) are applied in a constrained
and supportive manner, thereby enhancing annotation conﬁdence, prioritizing structurally related features, and assisting dereplication through taxon-speciﬁc and reference
databases, rather than establishing complete structures. Low-resolution workﬂows (orange), relying on HPLC ﬁngerprints or extract-level proﬁles with minimal structural
information, preclude structure-dependent modeling. Overall, the decision tree emphasizes that AI utility in natural product research is resolution-limited rather than univer-
sally scalable and that inappropriate escalation of AI complexity beyond data resolution risks overinterpretation and reduced reproducibility. Figure created using

At MSI level 3, compound identity is unresolved. Applying biotransformation models to complex
extracts at this tier introduces exponential combinatorial complexity and risks cascading predic-

datasets, as discussed in Box 2 [69]. Rigorous experimental validation and explicit overﬁtting
controls can mitigate these risks. However, outputs at this tier must be tr eated as preliminary
hypotheses rather than translational evidence [70].


Peptidic NPs—including ribosomally synthesized and post-translationally modiﬁed peptides

outside the chemical space represented in current small-molecule training datasets [71].

remains unavailable. Emerging peptide-focused platforms, such as pepADMET, now enable
systematic absorption, distribution, metab olism, excretion, and toxicity (ADMET) eval-
uation of linear and cyclic peptides [73], while pseudo-NP strategies that recombine NP-
derived fragments provide a complementary route to expand the training-accessible chemical
space for this underrepresented compound class [74]. Although biotransformation product
prediction for peptidic NPs remains a largely unmet frontier, these converging approaches
signal that dedicated computational tools for this modality are beginning to take shape.


Toward translational integration (cross-tier)
The AI strategies described previously each address individual analytical steps within the data-quality-
guided framework. Translational value, however, emerges only when researchers coordinate these
tools across the MSI-guided pipeline. This requires matching biotransformation prediction, ADMET
proﬁling, and target identiﬁcation to the identiﬁcation conﬁdence available at each stage and
escalating to more data-demanding methods only as analytical resolution improves.


No single biotransformation tool currently achieves sufﬁcient predictive coverage across
all tiers. Comparative evaluation of GLORYx, BioTransformer 3.0, SyGMa, and M etaTrans
revealed substantial variability, and none captured all experimentally observed metabolites
[75]. Network-level approaches  that integrate multistep metabolic context—such as
message-passing neural networks for metabolite authenticity prediction—of fer a complemen-
tary validation strategy [76]. Next-generation integrative engines, such as BioTransformer 4.0
combined with MetabFlow, represent emerging efforts to bridge this gap [77,78]. The data-
quality-guided framework supports this coordination by matching each tool to its evidence
tier, preventing premature escalation while preserving a clear upgrade path as analytical
resolution improves. For peptidic NPs—where proteolytic metabolism diverges from CYP-

Extending the framework: historical evidence and multi-omics data
The data-quality-guided framework described earlier matches AI strategies to current analytical
evidence. Yet NP research uniquely beneﬁts from two additional evidence sources unavailable
in synthetic drug discovery: historical records of human use and multi-omics biological data.
Traditional medicine records provide centuries of empirical safety and efﬁcacy observations
that can reduce translational risk before any experiment is conducted [79]. Multi-omics technol-
ogies provide biological resolution beyond what chemical identiﬁcation alone can deliver. Both
sources extend the framework by adding evidence layers that sup port AI-guided decision-
making across the discovery pipeline (Figure 2) [80].


Traditional knowledge as a translational prior
NPs enter the discovery pipeline with a retrospective translational prior that synthetic
compounds lack, rooted in prior human exposure and accumulated safety observations [81].
Traditional remedies with documented long-term use carry preliminary safety signals that reduce
early-stage attrition, where synthetic candidates most frequently fail [82].


Curated databases, such as LOTUS, COCONUT, KNApSAcK, and PlantCyc—described in
Box 2—provide structured access to NP use data [83,84]. AI tools can amplify the utility of
these databases. Machine learning models applied to traditional Chinese medicine formulations
map multi-metabolite, multi-target interactions that compound-by-compound analysis ca nnot
resolve, thereby revealing systems-level therapeutic mechanisms [82]. More recently, founda-
tion models pretrained directly on curated NP databases—such as NaFM, trained on
COCONUT-derived NP structures—have demonstrated that scaffold-level biosynthetic patterns
encoded in these repositories can improve bioactivit y prediction and taxonomic classiﬁcation
beyond what general-purpose molecular models achieve [47]. Similarly, systems pharmacology
applied to indole-derived NPs shows how network analysis can prioritize mechanistic targets
for multifactorial diseases [36].


Computational toxicology platforms—including ProTox-II, DeepTox, and Pred-hERG—
complement these safety records by predicting toxicity for novel derivatives or n onstandard
administration routes [85]. Expanding databases, such as SuperNatural 3.0, extend chemical
coverage beyond established compound classes [86], enabling the discovery of previously
overlooked mechanisms through deep learning-based frameworks [87,88]. By connecting
traditional medicine knowledge with AI-guided compound identiﬁcation, the framework can also
inform emerging translational strategies—such as NP-based nano-drug delivery systems—
where precise compound characterization is a prer equisite for optimizing bioavailability and
targeted delivery [89].


Multi-omics integration for systems-level prediction
Multi-omics technologies intersect with the data-quality-guided framework as a prospective
evidence layer in three distinct roles, each carrying a different relationship to MSI-guided
compound identiﬁcation. In this framework, they strengthen AI-guided NP discovery by reﬁning
mechanisms, resolving biological heterogeneity, and extending human translational relevance
beyond compound identiﬁcation alone. Their contribution is, therefore, best understood as

The ﬁrst role is discovery-enabling. Genome mining tools—such as antiSMASH and transformer-

The second role is mechanism-resolution. AI-enabled integration of transcriptomic, proteomic,
and epigenomic data enables mechanistic inference at a resolution not achievable through
chemical identiﬁcation alone [51,92]. Proteomic and epigenomic layers reveal post-translational
regulation and chromatin-level modulation—mechanisms frequently involved in th e pleiotropic
effects of NPs [93]. These approaches become progressively more reliable as MSI conﬁdence
increases: at MSI level 1, compound–target–pathway attribution is fully traceable; at MSI
level 2, tentative correlations between MS-annotated metabolites and transcriptomic signatures
can support hypothesis generation but not deﬁnitive mechanistic claims. At MSI level 3,
compound identity is unres olved, and systems-level inference risks attributing biological effects
to uncharacterized chemical inputs; at this stage, multi-omics integration should not substitute
for analytical resolution.


The third role is heterogeneity-resolution. Pharmacogenomic information captures interindividual
variability in drug metabolism and response [15]. Multi-omics clustering of metabolic diseases has
revealed clinically distinct subtypes with divergent biological proﬁles and therapeutic trajectories
[49], reinforcing the need for precision approaches that AI-guided NP workﬂows can support.
At single-cell resolution, foundation models such as scGPT and CellFM reﬁne cell-type-speciﬁc
NP responses beyond what bulk measurements provide [50,94]. Because these tools attribute
response variability to speciﬁc molecular mechanisms, they require at minimum MSI level 2
annotation—and ideally level 1 conﬁrmation—to avoid confounding compound-level effects
with patient-level variability. These approaches are late-stage extensions of the MSI-guided
framework, not replacements for upstream compound speciﬁcation.
