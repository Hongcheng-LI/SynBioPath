ABSTRACT: Tetracycline (TC) family natural products have
attracted significant attention due to their diverse chemical
structures and important role in drug development. As one of the
most successful classes of drugs, TC antibiotics have been used
clinically for over 70 years and remain crucial in treating infections.
Despite their importance, systematic exploration of novel TC natural
products has been limited, leaving the molecular landscape of the
TC family poorly understood and hindering further development of
these compounds for therapeutic applications. Here, we developed a
targeted strategy to identify TC biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs)
based on specific cyclase signatures involved in assembling the TC scaffold. This led to the discovery of 82 representative BGCs with
the potential to produce structurally diverse TCs. Among them, we uncovered three groups of novel natural productsmisiomycins,
varsomycins, and hibarimicins J−Land identified their biosynthetic pathways. These compounds display distinctive structural
features, with misiomycin A and hibarimicin L among the most highly modified TCs identified to date. Misiomycin A biosynthesis
involves extensive glycosylation, while biosynthesis of varsomycin A, featuring a unique six-membered lactone ring structure, requires
the coordinated action of two TC BGCs. The biosynthesis of hibarimicins J−L, derived from TC monomer dimerization, undergoes
complex oxidative modifications involving seven oxygenases. Several TCs exhibited potent activity against drug-resistant Gram-
positive pathogens. Our work further expands the structural diversity within the TC family and underscores the potential of these
BGCs for generating new TC structures, providing valuable insights for the discovery and development of novel TC-based
therapeutics.
■INTRODUCTION
Bacterial aromatic polyketides are a growing class of
structurally complex and pharmaceutically valuable natural
products.1 These polycyclic polyketides are primarily synthe-
sized by type II polyketide synthases (PKSs).2,3 The minimal
PKS, comprising ketosynthase (KS), chain length factor
(CLF), and acyl-carrier protein (ACP), catalyzes the iterative
decarboxylative condensation of malonyl-CoA extender units
to produce an ACP-tethered linear poly-β-ketone intermedi-
ates.2,4 This intermediate undergoes diverse cyclization modes,
mediated by cyclases/aromatases, and associated enzymes, to
form distinct polycyclic aromatic frameworks such as
tetracyclines (TCs), anthracyclines, and angucyclines.4−6
Further modifications by various tailoring enzymes yield the
mature polyketide products of different families.7
The TC family natural products are among the most
prominent bacterial aromatic polyketides, renowned for their
remarkable bioactivities, with particularly significant contribu-
tions to antibiotic development.1,8 Typical TC natural
products feature linearly fused tetracyclic structures, including
well-known broad-spectrum antibiotics such as oxytetracycline
(1), chlortetracycline (2), and tetracycline (3) (Scheme
1A).9−11 They primarily exert their effects by targeting the
bacterial 30S ribosomal subunit, hindering the binding of
aminoacyl-tRNA to the ribosome and thereby inhibiting
translation.12 Additionally, some TC natural products arise
from the oxidative cleavage and rearrangement of typical TC
intermediates, resulting in structures with incomplete tetracy-
clic frameworks, such as aureolic acid-type compounds.9,13,14 A
notable example is mithramycin, which has been used as an
anticancer agent.15
TC antibiotics, as one of the most successful classes of drugs,
have been used in clinical treatment for over 70 years and
continue to play a vital role.8 They are effective against Gram-
positive bacteria, Gram-negative bacteria, mycoplasma, chla-

■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Genomic Mining Identifies 82 Representative Bio-
synthetic Gene Clusters Predicted to Encode Novel
Tetracycline Natural Products. To explore the chemical
diversity of TC natural products, it is crucial to develop a
precise strategy for identifying their BGCs. While previous
studies have shown that the phylogeny of CLF proteins
correlates with aromatic polyketide families, TC natural
products cluster with other polyketides, such as four-ring
anthracyclines (e.g., steffimycin) and three-ring murayaqui-
none-type products.23,27,28 Therefore, relying solely on CLF
protein phylogeny is insufficient for the precise identification of
TC BGCs.
The key distinction in the biosynthesis of TCs, compared to
other bacterial aromatic polyketides synthesized by type II
PKSs, lies in their cyclization processes (Scheme 1B, Table
S1).4,7,9,29 The linear poly-β-ketone intermediates produced by
minimal PKSs undergo distinct cyclization patterns to form
various aromatic products, including tetracyclines, anthracy-
clines, tetracenomycins, and angucyclines, a process catalyzed
by cyclases/aromatases and other associated enzymes.7,29
Therefore, we performed a comparative analysis of cycliza-
tion-related enzymes in the biosynthesis of aromatic
polyketides from different families (Scheme 1B, Table S1).
For TC natural products, the cyclization process is
orchestrated by a distinctive set of four enzymes, exemplified
by OxyK, OxyN, OxyH, and OxyI. TCs and anthracyclines
share a common cyclization pathway up to the formation of an
ACP-tethered, three-ring anthranol intermediate catalyzed by
enzyme pairs like OxyK/OxyN and SnoaE/SnoaM, but diverge
in the fourth-ring formation mechanisms (Scheme 1B, C).4,30
In anthracyclines, the ACP-tethered three-ring intermediate is
modified by a SnoaC-like methyltransferase and SnoaB-like
oxygenase to form a three-ring anthracycline with a terminal
methyl ester, followed by fourth-ring cyclization catalyzed by a
SnoaL-like cyclase (Scheme 1C).30 In TCs, an OxyH-like acyl-
CoA ligase converts the ACP-tethered intermediate into an
AMP-tethered form, which undergoes aldol condensation
between the C18 methylene and the C1 carbonyl to complete
the fourth-ring formation, likely mediated by an OxyI-like
cyclase (Scheme 1C).9,31 Homologues of OxyK, OxyN, OxyH,
and OxyI are conserved in TC BGCs, with OxyH notably
absent in other aromatic polyketides (Scheme 1B, Table S1).
Thus, the co-occurrence of these four homologues is a reliable
marker for identifying TC BGCs.
Next, we performed a systematic analysis of 445,862
bacterial genomes from the NCBI Genome database and
126,986 bacterial genomes from the JGI IMG/M databases (as
of April 2024).32 This analysis led to the identification of
12,273 type II PKS BGCs, each encoding at least a minimal
PKS. Among them, BGCs that encode a complete set of the
four homologous proteins (OxyK, OxyN, OxyH, and OxyI) are
classified as TC BGCs. These TC BGCs are distinct from
other aromatic polyketide BGCs, as they do not encode all four
homologues simultaneously. We further compared the protein
sequence similarities encoded by these TC BGCs with those of
17 previously known TC BGCs (Supporting Information).
After removing those that were highly similar to previously
known TC BGCs, 476 new TC BGCs were identified (Scheme
1D and Supplementary sheet). Taxonomic analysis revealed
that all host strains belong to the class Actinomycetes,
encompassing at least 29 genera, with over 60% identified as
strains of the genus Streptomyces (Figure S2). The remaining
strains belong to Kitasatospora, Amycolatopsis, Catellatospora,
Actinomadura, Micromonospora, and other genera. Our findings
highlight that Actinobacteria are the primary producers of TC
family natural products. We further analyzed these BGCs,
retaining one representative BGC for those predicted to
synthesize the same or highly similar products. This resulted in
82 representative BGCs with the potential to produce novel
TC natural products.
Bioinformatic Analysis Reveals Remarkable Structur-
al Diversity in the Tetracycline Family Natural Products.
To enable further comparison, we performed hierarchical
clustering of the 82 representative BGCs (Figure 1). In
addition, sequence similarity network analysis of the encoded
enzymes was performed (Figure S3). Both approaches
highlighted the remarkable diversity of biosynthetic enzymes
within these BGCs. In known TC natural products, the C2
position typically carries a carbamoyl or acetyl group, with the
carbamoyl introduced by an amidotransferase encoded in the
BGC.9,33 Here, we observed that over half of the BGCs encode
one or more 3-ketoacyl-ACP synthase III (KAS III) enzymes,
commonly involved in forming nonacetyl starter units or
attaching tailoring groups (e.g., SF2575) (Figure 1, Figure
S4).34 Phylogenetic analysis suggests that almost half of these
KAS III enzymes may synthesize nonacetyl starter units,
indicating that these BGCs could produce TCs with novel C2
functional groups, distinct from the usual carbamoyl or acetyl
groups (Figure S5).
Various oxidoreductases were identified in these BGCs,
including FAD-dependent hydroxylases, FAD-dependent ring
cleavage/rearrangement oxygenases, other FAD-dependent
oxygenases, cytochrome P450 enzymes, and SDR family
ketoreductases (Figure 1). On average, each BGC encoded
2.6 FAD-dependent oxygenases, underscoring their critical role
in TC natural products biosynthesis. Notably, BGCs encoding
FAD-dependent ring-cleavage/rearrangement oxygenases, ho-
mologous to MtmOIV from the mithramycin pathway, are
predicted to yield TCs with oxidatively rearranged scaf-
folds.4,35,36 Methyltransferases were also abundant, averaging
2.3 per BGC. Most BGCs are also rich in glycosyltransferases
(GTs), with an average of 2.9 GTs per BGC. The strain with
the highest GT count is Amycolatopsis xylanica, containing 14
GTs. Radical SAM enzymes and halogenases were identified in
some BGCs. Notably, certain BGCs appear to encode type I
PKSs and nonribosomal peptide synthetases (NRPSs),
suggesting the potential for producing hybrid TC products.

Identification of Amycolatopsis rubida DSM 44637 as
a New Producer of Mutaxanthenes Featuring a Unique
Xanthene Ring Structure. Mutaxanthenes represent a
unique type within the TC family natural products,
characterized by their rare 12,12a-dihydro-1H-benzo[b]-
xanthene scaffold.39−41 Notably, we found the mxt gene cluster
in Amycolatopsis rubida DSM 44637, which exhibits significant
similarity to the mtx cluster in Streptomyces setonensis NAK80,
known for mutaxanthenes production, with encoded protein
sequence identity ranging from 19% to 68%.40 Overexpression
of the SARP family regulatory gene mxtR1 activated the
production of two compounds 13 and 14 (Figure S8).
Deletion of the KS-encoding gene mxtA abolished the
production of these compounds, confirming their origin from
the mxt cluster (Figure S8). Subsequent HRMS and NMR
analysis identified as mutaxanthenes A (13) and C (14),
respectively (Figures S35 and S36).39 The mxt cluster encodes
three FAD-dependent oxygenases. MxtO1 and MxtO2 are
predicted to hydroxylate the TC intermediate at C4 and C12a,
respectively, while MxtO3, sharing 56% sequence identity with
MtxO4 from the mtx cluster, is implicated in xanthene ring
formation (Table S5).40 Consistent with findings in the
ΔmtxO4 mutant, deletion of mxtO3 in our study resulted in
the loss of 13 and 14 production and the accumulation of
intermediates 15 and 16 (Figures S8, S37, and S38).
Thus, we identified a new mutaxanthene-producing strain
and confirms the role of the mxt cluster in mutaxanthene
biosynthesis. Based on the product ratios, we infer that the mxt
gene cluster utilizes both acetyl-CoA and propionyl-CoA as
starter units, with a preference for propionyl-CoA (Figure S8).
Prior to this study, the Nocardiopsis sp. FU40 ΔapoS8,
Amycolatopsis orientalis NRRL F3213 and Streptomyces
setonensis NAK80 ΔmtxM2 mutant strains were reported to
produce compounds 13 and 14.39−41
Discovery of a Highly Glycosylated Tetracycline
Natural Product Misiomycin A with an Unusual Starter
Unit from Streptomyces misionensis DSM 40306. Among
the 82 newly identified representative BGCs, approximately
30% encode a KAS III enzyme predicted to synthesize unusual
starter units, and over 70% encode GTs. Notably, 28% of these
BGCs contain five or more GTs, suggesting significant
potential for producing extensively glycosylated TCs. To
date, among known TC natural products, only the biosynthesis
of cervimycin and hainancycline involves five and six GTs,
respectively.9,23,42 To further explore the potential of KAS III
and GT enzymes in generating structurally novel TCs, we
analyzed the mis cluster from Streptomyces misionensis DSM
40306 (Figure 1). This cluster encodes six GTs and two KAS
III enzymes, one of which is predicted to mediate the
formation of an unusual starter unit (Figure 2A, Figure S9, and
Table S6,). These features suggest that this strain has the
potential to produce novel TC compounds with a nonacetyl
starter unit and extensive glycosylation. To obtain the
products, the mis cluster was activated by co-overexpressing
the two cluster-situated SARP family regulatory genes misR1
and misR2, and products misiomycins A−D (4−7) were
detected in the activation strain DSM 40306/misR1R2 (Figure
2B). Further deletion of the KS-encoding gene misA abolished
production of 4−7. In the complementation strain ΔmisA/
misR1R2ABC, the yields of 4−7 were restored to levels
relatively higher than those in the DSM 40306/misR1R2,
possibly due to the use of the strong promoter kasOp* for
misA complementation expression (Figure 2B). These results
confirmed that the mis cluster is responsible for the
biosynthesis of compounds 4−7.
Then, we isolated and characterized the structures of 4−7
based on HRMS and extensive NMR analysis (Figures S28−
S32 and Tables S13 and S14). All four new products share a
common tetracyclic core structure with aromatized C and D
rings, featuring an unusual isobutyryl group at C2. The product
6 is a methylated derivative of 7 at C6. Both 6 and 7 share a
planar structure like that of chelocardin and anhydrotetracy-
cline.43,44 Recently, the configurations at C4a and C12a of
chelocardin were revised to R,R, which is opposite to the
corresponding configurations in anhydrotetracycline (Scheme
1A).45 By comparing computational and experimental ECD
spectra, we determined that the C4a and C12a configurations
of 6 are R,R, consistent with those of chelocardin (Scheme 1A,
Figure S24I,J). Compared to 6, 5 contains an additional D-β-
oliose attached to C8-OH, supported by strong NOE
correlations observed between the anomeric proton and H7/
H8 (Figure S23B). The product 4 has the most complex
chemical structure, with a [M −H]−molecular ion peak at m/
z 1173.5094 (Figure S22N). Compared to 5, 4 features an
additional sugar chain at C12a, composed of five consecutive
deoxysugars. Six characteristic anomeric protons and carbon
signals of the sugar moieties were observed in the 1H NMR
and 13C NMR spectra, supporting the presence of six O-
glycosides in the structure (Figure S22A,B,H−I). Based on
1H−1H COSY, HSQC-TOCSY, and analysis of deoxysugar
biosynthetic genes within the mis cluster, we elucidated the
structure of the sugar moieties (Figures S21 and S22C−G,J−
M). Furthermore, through diagnostic HMBC correlations, we
established the sequence of the sugar moieties connected to
C12a−OH as C12a-O-L-α-rhodinose-D-β-olivose-D-β-amice-
tose-D-α-amicetose-D-β-olivose (Figures S21 and S22E,L).
Additionally, LC-MS/MS analysis of 4 revealed characteristic
fragment peaks corresponding to [M −H]−ions at m/z
1004.46 and m/z 169.05 (Figure S22P). We also observed that
4 could be hydrolyzed in hydrochloric acid to yield 5 and 6
(Figure S22O). These results further support the structure
elucidation of 4.
To gain further insights into the biosynthesis of 4, we
investigated the enzymes involved in its starter unit formation
and glycosylation modifications. Phylogenetic analysis of the
two KAS III proteins MisG and MisJ suggests that MisJ is
likely involved in utilizing the unique starter unit, isobutyryl-
CoA, for biosynthesis (Figure S8). Consistent with our
hypothesis, the production of 5−7 was abolished in the
mutant strain ΔmisJ/misR1R2ABC. Phylogenetic analysis
showed that MisGT6 cluster with C8-O-GTs from other TC
biosynthetic pathways, MisGT5 cluster with C12a-O-GTs
from other TC biosynthetic pathways, while the other four
GTs form a separate clade, suggesting that MisGT6 may
catalyze glycosylation at C8 and MisGT5 may catalyze
glycosylation at C12a (Figure S9). In the overexpression
strain DSM 40306/misR1R2ABC, we generated knockout
mutants for each of the six GTs and analyzed their metabolic
profiles using HPLC-HRMS (Figure 2, Figure S13). This
analysis included determining whether the predicted inter-
mediates 17−20, which contain varying numbers of glycosyl
groups, were produced. In the ΔmisGT6 mutant, 6 and 7 were

The compound 9 possesses a complete tetracyclic structure
with aromatic A, B, and D rings, and a hydroxylated C ring at
the C6 position (Figure 3C). Additionally, an isobutyryl group
is attached at the C2 position. NMR data analysis reveals that 8

fully elucidated through HMBC correlations (Figures S28 and
S33C,D). We successfully obtained single crystals of 8.
Surprisingly, single crystal X-ray diffraction revealed that the
crystal belongs to the P-1 space group (an inversion-symmetric
group), indicating that 8 consists of a pair of enantiomers
(Figure 3C, Figure S33G, and Table S16). The resolved crystal
structure confirmed that it comprises 6R- and 6S-8, with the
two molecules interacting through a hydrogen-bond network
mediated by two methanol molecules in a centrosymmetric
arrangement (Figure S33G). Intriguingly, 8 features a novel
six-membered lactone ring structure (ring A) formed by a C3−
O−C12 linkage, a unique characteristic not previously
observed in TC natural products. We subsequently confirmed
that 8 consists of a pair of enantiomers in an almost 1:1 ratio
through ECD spectral data and chiral column analysis (Figure
S33H). We purified the two optically pure compounds that are
not interconvertible and analyzed their ECD spectra (Figure
S33H). By comparing the experimental ECD data with
computational predictions, we assigned the configurations of
the enantiomers (Figure S33H).
Knockout of the KS-encoding gene varA abolished the
production of 8 and 9 without affecting 1 production, showing
that the polyketide backbones of 8 and 9 are biosynthesized by
the var cluster (Figure 3B). Phylogenetic analysis of the two
KAS III-encoding genes varG and varK in the var cluster
suggests that varK is likely responsible for utilizing the unusual
starter unit isobutyryl-CoA (Figure S9). As anticipated,
knockout of varK resulted in the loss of 8 and 9 production,
while the biosynthesis of 1 remained unaffected (Figure 3B).
Both 8 and 9 lack the C8-OH (D ring), indicating that the
polyketide intermediate underwent C9-ketoreduction prior to
cyclization to form the TC scaffold (Figure 3B). However, the
var cluster lacks a C9-ketoreductase gene (Table S7). We
hypothesize that oxtJ, which encodes the C9-ketoreductase in
the oxt cluster, may catalyze this reduction in the biosynthesis
of 8 and 9. Consistent with this, knockout of oxtJ abolished the
production of 1, 8 and 9 (Figure 3B), confirming its role in
both 1 and varsomycin biosynthesis. Moreover, the var cluster
lacks a C6-methyltransferase gene (Table S7). Knockout of
oxtF resulted in the loss of production of 1, 8 and 9 (Figure
3B), indicating that OxtF is responsible for C6-methylation in
both 1 and varsomycins. In addition to 8, 9 also exhibited no
significant Cotton effect in the ECD spectrum, indicating that
it exists as a racemic mixture of enantiomers (Figure S34F).
These observations suggest that C6-hydroxylation in 8 and 9
may occur via a spontaneous oxidation process. In the
biosynthesis of 1, the FAD-dependent oxygenase OxyS
catalyzes the formation of the C6-R−OH;48 however, no
OxyS homologue is present in the var cluster (Table S7). We
knocked out oxtS (homologue of oxyS), which resulted in the
expected loss of 1 (Figure 3B). Notably, both 8 and 9 were still
produced (Figure 3B), supporting the hypothesis that
nonselective oxidation at C6 occurs spontaneously.
These findings allow us to propose a compelling synergistic
model where the var and oxt clusters collaborate in varsomycin
biosynthesis. Varsomycin biosynthesis utilizes an unusual
isobutyryl starter unit and recruits enzymes from the oxt
cluster, including the C9-ketoreductase OxtJ, C6-methyltrans-
ferase OxtF. The compound 8, featuring an unprecedented
chemical scaffold, is likely derived from the putative
intermediate 21 via a series of reactions, including a Baeyer−
Villiger C−C bond cleavage, ring opening, and cyclization
steps, ultimately forming its unique lactone structure (Figure
3C). To identify the enzyme catalyzing the proposed Baeyer−
Villiger reaction, we knocked out varO4, which encodes a
luciferase family monooxygenase. The ΔvarO4 mutant still
produced compounds 8 and 9 at lower yields, without
accumulating new intermediates (Figure S16). These results
suggest that VarO4 does not catalyze the Baeyer−Villiger
reaction, and the responsible oxygenase remains to be
identified. Notably, the var cluster encodes an abundance of
glycosylation-related enzymes, suggesting its potential to
produce extensively glycosylated compounds in addition to 8
and 9. However, no glycosylated products have been identified
so far, warranting further investigation.
Discovery of Novel Hibarimicins Formed by Dimeri-
zation of Tetracycline Monomers Followed by Exten-
sive Oxidations in Saccharothrix australiensis DSM
43800. FAD-dependent oxygenases not only catalyze the
hydroxylation of polycyclic aromatic polyketide scaffolds but
also mediate complex oxidative transformationssuch as ring
cleavage, contraction, expansion, and rearrangementthat
lead to the formation of novel ring systems.40,48,49 These
reactions significantly enhance the structural complexity and
diversity of aromatic polyketides.7 Notably, FAD-dependent
oxygenases are present in all newly identified and known
representative TC BGCs, underscoring their indispensable role
in the biosynthesis of TC family natural products (Figure 1).
While most BGCs encode 2−3 FAD-dependent oxygenases,
four BGCs harbor five or more, suggesting that their products
are subject to extensive oxidative modifications. The strain
Saccharothrix australiensis DSM 43800 has attracted our
interest due to a large DNA region encoding a type II PKS
for TC scaffold biosynthesis and various tailoring enzymes,
including six FAD-dependent oxygenases, one of which is
predicted to catalyze oxidative ring-cleavage reactions (Figures
1 and4A). In addition, this region encodes several type I PKSs
and an NRPS (Figure 4A). To determine whether these genes
collaborate to produce a single natural product, we individually
inactivated hibA (encoding the KS of the type II PKS), hibI
(encoding a KAS III enzyme), ausA1 (encoding the loading
module of the type I PKS), and ausG (encoding an NRPS
enzyme) (Figure S18). Compared to the wild-type strain, both
ΔhibA and ΔhibI mutants no longer produce compounds 10−
12 or 22−24, while the production of compounds A and B
remains unaffected (Figure 4B). In contrast, the ΔausA1 and
ΔausG mutants still produce 10−12 and 22−24, but are
unable to produce A or B (Figure 4B). These results revealed
that this genomic region harbors two distinct gene clusters: a

We then focused on the hib cluster and its associated products,
compounds 10−12 and 22−24.
We successfully isolated compounds 12 and 22−24 and
determined their structures based on HRMS and NMR data
(Figure 4C, Figures S39−S42, Tables S18−S21). During the
isolation of compounds 10 and 11, we observed that they
spontaneously transformed into compounds 25 and 26,
respectively. The structures of compounds 25 and 26 were
elucidated using HRMS and NMR data. We found that the
HRMS and NMR data of 24 were consistent with those
previously reported for HMP-M1 (= 24), a compound isolated
from the mutant strain Microbispora rosea subsp. hibaria TP-
A0121 (Figure S42A, Table S21).50 This suggests that 24 is a
typical TC compound featuring a butyryl group at C2.
Furthermore, we obtained single crystals of 24. Single crystal
X-ray diffraction data revealed the stereochemical structure of
24, showing that keto-enol tautomerization occurred between
C3, C2, and C13, revising the previously reported structural
interpretation of 24 (Figure 4C, Figure S42G, Table S22).50
The C4a and C12a configurations of 24 are coplanar and β-
oriented, consistent with the configuration of chelocardin at
the same positions.43 Additionally, a comparison of the ECD
spectra of 24 and 6 reveals a close resemblance, indicating that
their C4a and C12a configurations are identical (Figure S31I).
This further supports the aforementioned configuration
analysis of 6 based on computational ECD data. Among
known TC natural products, aside from chelocardin, only 24
and misiomycins (4−6) have been shown to exhibit R,R
configurations at C4a and C12a, while the others either possess
S,S configurations or remain undetermined.9,45 This suggests
that the FAD-dependent oxygenases responsible for hydrox-
ylation at C12a exhibit different stereoselectivity.
While 24 is a TC monomer, the remaining five novel
compounds (12, 22, 23, 25, and 26) are dimers formed by the
intermolecular coupling of TC monomers. These structurally
unique TC natural products are reminiscent of the complex
pseudosymmetric aromatic polyketide hibarimicins (also
known as angelmicins), which were previously isolated and
characterized from Microbispora rosea subsp. hibaria TP-A0121
and its mutant strains;51,52 hence, we also designated them as
members of the hibarimicin-type natural products. While
previously reported hibarimicins (dimeric products) often
undergo extensive glycosylation at C2 and C4,53 we found that
only hibarimicins L (12) and La (22) in our study feature an
interglycosidic linkage at C4′ to a 4-keto-2,3,6-trideoxy-D-
glucose (Figure 4C). By comparing the ECD spectra of 12
with those of chemically synthesized hibarimicinone and atrop-
hibarimicinone reported in the literature,54 we identified that
12 possesses a P configuration for the C9−C9′ axial chirality
(Figure S39G). Compared to 12, the unique feature of 22 is
the presence of a furan ring formed by a C8′−O-C10 linkage.
During the isolation process, we observed that 12 gradually
underwent spontaneous conversion to 22, presumably through
the elimination of a molecule of ethanol (Figure S26). Similar
spontaneous reactions have been previously reported for
hibarimicins.54 Similar to 22, both hibarimicins Ja (25) and
Ka (26) contain a furan ring, with 26 featuring a propoxy
group at C8, as supported by 1H−1H COSY and HMBC
spectral data (Figures S28, S43, and S44). Since 25 and 26 are
spontaneously derived from hibarimicins J (10) and K (11),
respectively, with the loss of a molecule corresponding to the
molecular weight of ethanol during the transformation, we
inferred the structures of 10 and 11, which resemble that of 12,
differing primarily by the absence of the C4′ sugar moiety.
HRMS data show that the [M −H]−molecular ion peak of 23
is at m/z 953.3470 (calcd for m/z 953.3443) (Figure S41G),
suggesting a molecular formula of C48H58O20. The 1D NMR
spectrum of 23 displays only half of the expected signals,
indicating the presence of a centrosymmetric structure (Figure
S41A,B). Compared to 12 and 22, 23 exhibits fewer peripheral
groups. Comparison of the ECD spectra revealed that 23 more
closely resembles HMP-Y1 than atrop-HMP-Y1 (Figure
S41H).54 Thus, the C9−C9′ axial chirality of 23 is also
assigned a P configuration.
To further understand the biosynthetic pathway of these
natural products, we conducted feeding experiments and
demonstrated that the ΔhibA mutant can convert the typical
TC monomer 24 into the dimerized product 23, showing that
24 serves as a biosynthetic precursor for the dimerized
products (Figure S24). The conversion of 24 into the final
dimerized products is likely to involve a series of complex
steps, including dimerization through biphenyl bond for-
mation, oxidative rearrangement of the A and A′ rings,
hydroxylation, ether bond formation, methylation, and
glycosylation. This gene cluster encodes one P450 enzyme,
HibO2, and six FAD-dependent oxidases, HibO1 and HibO3−
O7. Inactivation of hibO2 resulted in the disappearance of
dimerization products 10−12, 22 and 23, while the yield of 24
was dramatically increased (Figure 4B). This indicates that
HibO2 catalyzes the dimerization of 24. Based on sequence
alignment and phylogenetic analysis, HibO1 and HibO7 are
predicted to catalyze hydroxylation at C4 and C12a of 24,
respectively (Figure S23). HibO6 clusters with Baeyer−Villiger
monooxygenases like MtmOIV,35 suggesting it is responsible
for the proposed Baeyer−Villiger oxidation and rearrangement
of the A and A′ rings (Figure S23). HibO3, HibO4, and
HibO5 form a separate clade (with more than 47% sequence
identity among them), indicating their involvement in the
remaining oxidation reactions (Figure S23).
Inactivation of hibO6 resulted in the loss of 10−12, 22 and
23 without significantly affecting the production of 24, and no
new dimerized products were detected (Figure 4B). As
expected, the complementation strain ΔhibO6/hibO6 restored
the production of compounds 10−12, 22, and 23 (Figure
S26). We further constructed a ΔhibO2ΔhibO6 double
knockout mutant, which exhibited a metabolic profile identical
to that of the ΔhibO2 mutant (Figure 4B). This result supports
the role of HibO2 as acting upstream of HibO6, suggesting
that 24 first undergoes dimerization catalyzed by HibO2,
followed by HibO6-mediated oxidative rearrangement of the
dimerized product. Inactivation of hibO3 led to the
disappearance of 10−12, 22 and 23 and the accumulation of
a new product, hibarimicin O3−1 (27) (Figure 4B).
Subsequent NMR analysis elucidated the structural character-
istics of 27, showing that this compound is also a
centrosymmetric molecule (Figure S45). It is hypothesized
that 27 is formed from the dimerization of 24, followed by
oxidation and rearrangement reactions occurring in the A and
A′ rings. Therefore, this structure represents the simplest
dimerization rearrangement product and is proposed to be a
crucial substrate for subsequent modification steps, including
various oxidation reactions. This finding suggests that HibO3
may be responsible for catalyzing the subsequent hydroxylation

We also knocked out hibM3, which encodes a radical SAM
enzyme. However, no hibarimicin-related compounds, except
for 24, were detected in ΔhibM3 (Figure 4B), possibly due to
the accumulation of unstable intermediates. We then
constructed the ΔhibO4ΔhibM3 double mutant strain, in
which 29−31 were no longer produced, while 28 remained
detectable (Figure 4B). These results indicate that HibM3 is
involved in the formation of ethoxy and propoxy groups at the
C7 and C7′ positions. Next, we generated the ΔhibΔaus
mutant strain by simultaneously deleting the hib and aus gene
clusters. As expected, this strain lost the ability to produce all
hibarimicin-related compounds as well as compounds A and B.
We then coexpressed hibO3-hibM2 and hibO3-hibM2-hibM3 in
ΔhibΔaus, obtaining ΔhibΔaus/hibO3M2 and ΔhibΔaus/
hibO3M2M3 strains. Feeding experiments showed that
ΔhibΔaus/hibO3M2 could convert 27 only to 28, while
ΔhibΔaus/hibO3M2M3 converted 27 to both 30 and 31
(Figure S22A). These biotransformation results support that
HibM3 catalyzes the further methylation of the methoxy group
at the C7 and C7′ positions. Furthermore, we overexpressed
HibM3 in E. coli BL21 (DE3) with pDB1282 (Isc operon) and
pBAD43-BtuCEDFB (cobalamin uptake)55 and purified the
protein to homogeneity (Figure S21). In vitro assays showed
that HibM3 catalyzed the conversion of 28 to a small amount
of product 30, as confirmed by HRMS analysis, while boiled
HibM3 did not catalyze this reaction (Figure S22B). Together,
these results confirm that HibM3 catalyzes the sequential
methylation of the methoxy group at the C7 and C7′ positions
during hibarimicin biosynthesis.
Based on these experimental results, we propose a
biosynthetic pathway for 10−12 (Figure 4C, Figure S27).
The pathway starts with butyryl-CoA as the initial unit, leading
to the formation of the tetracyclic intermediate 24. This
intermediate is then transformed into the pseudosymmetric
aromatic polyketides 10−12, 22 and 23 through the actions of
P450 enzyme HibO2, four FAD-dependent oxygenases
(HibO6, HibO3, HibO4, and HibO5), and other tailoring
enzymes. Methyltransferases HibM2 and HibM1 are predicted
to methylate the hydroxyl groups at C7/C7′ and C8/C8′,
respectively. The radical SAM enzyme HibM3 converts the
resulting methoxy groups into ethoxy and propoxy groups. The
putative GT HibGT1 is proposed to mediate the glycosylation
modification at C4′−OH.
Recently, a study cloned and heterologously expressed the
hbm cluster from Microbispora rosea subsp. hibaria TP-A0121,
confirming its capability to biosynthesize hibarimicin B and its
derivatives.56 The hib cluster we identified resembles the hbm
cluster, with sequence identities between homologous proteins
ranging from 42% to 82% (Table S9). Notably, the hbm cluster
encodes additional genes for sugar biosynthesis and glyco-
sylation, accounting for the more extensive glycosylation in its
products. In their study, they constructed ΔhbmO6 (homol-
ogous to hibO3) and ΔhbmO8 (homologous to hibO5)
mutants and analyzed their metabolites. Consistent with our
findings, HbmO8 is likely responsible for C6′−OH formation.
However, they proposed that HbmO6 catalyzes C8/C8′−OH
formation, whereas our analysis of ΔhibO3 and ΔhibO4
suggests that HibO3 (homologue of HbmO6) is responsible
for C7/C7′−OH formation. Among the 13 hibarimicin-type
compounds we identified, compounds 10, 23, and 27 were also
identified in their study.56
Our findings revealed a novel and intriguing BGC, the hib
cluster, which is divided into two regions by another BGC and
produces structurally unique TC natural products, including
hibarimicins J−L and related compounds. Investigation of its
biosynthetic pathway uncovered several fascinating oxygenases
involved in constructing dimeric TC structures with distinctive
ring skeletons, laying the groundwork for further exploration of
their catalytic mechanisms. Hibarimicins rank among the most
complex and largest aromatic polyketides produced by type II
PKS identified to date.

■CONCLUSIONS
In summary, we developed a precise strategy for discovering
TC BGCs based on specific cyclase signatures, leading to the
identification of 82 representative gene clusters with the
potential to produce structurally diverse TC natural products
(Figure 1, Figure S4). From these, we uncovered three groups
of novel natural productsmisiomycins, varsomycins, and
hibarimicins J−Land revealed their biosynthetic pathways.
These compounds exhibit a variety of unique structural
features, including unusual starter units that form distinct C2
groups, diverse aromatic hydroxylations with varying stereo-
chemistry, oxidative rearrangements of the A-ring, dimerization
of TC monomers, and complex glycosylation patterns.
Notably, we found that varsomycin biosynthesis involves the
coordinated action of both the var and oxt clusters, and we
identified the biosynthetic pathway of hibarimicins J−L, which
undergoes a series of complex oxidative modifications
involving seven oxygenases. The compounds 4 and 12/22
are among the most highly modified TCs identified to date,
while compound 8 features an unprecedented chemical
scaffold. Some of the compounds exhibit potent activity
against drug-resistant Gram-positive bacteria and anticancer
properties. Our work significantly expands the chemical
diversity of the TC family natural products, uncovers a wealth
of intriguing biosynthetic enzymes, and highlights the potential
of these gene clusters for producing bioactive novel structures.
This will further facilitate the discovery, engineering and
development of new TC-based therapeutics.
