Daldipyrenones A−C: Caged [6,6,6,6,6] Polyketides Derived from an
Endolichenic Fungus Daldinia pyrenaica 047188

ABSTRACT: Daldipyrenones A−C (1−3), three unprecedented
caged xanthone [6,6,6,6,6] polyketides featuring a spiro-azaphilone
unit, were discovered from an endolichenic fungus, Daldinia
pyrenaica 047188. The structures of 1−3 were determined by using
spectroscopic analysis and chemical derivatization. Daldipyrenones
are likely derived by combining a chromane biosynthesis
intermediate, 1-(2,6-dihydroxyphenyl)but-2-en-2-one, and a
spiro-azaphilone, pestafolide A, via radical coupling or Michael addition to form a bicyclo[2.2.2]octane ring. Genome sequencing
of the strain revealed two separate biosynthetic gene clusters responsible for forming two biosynthetic intermediates, suggesting a
proposed biosynthetic pathway. Daldipyrenone A (1) exhibited significant antimelanogenic activity with lower EC50’s than positive
controls and moderate adiponectin-secretion promoting activity.
D
iverse endolichenic fungi reside asymptomatically in
lichen thalli.1 They have gained attention owing to their
potential for producing novel bioactive secondary metabolites
with diverse structures and activities.2 The metabolites
produced from diverse biosynthetic pathways in the fungi
include steroids, quinones, terpenoids, peptides, and xan-
thones, which exhibit potent bioactivity, including anticancer,
antiviral, antibacterial, and antifungal properties.3 Thus,
endolichenic fungi are a valuable source of novel compounds
with structural diversity and pharmacological properties.
Reduced levels of the protein hormone adiponectin are
associated with obesity, diabetes, and inflammation, three
rapidly increasing chronic disorders with severe global health
implications.4 In our continuous effort to discover novel
compounds with adiponectin-secretion-promoting activity,5 we
performed chemical screening on endolichenic fungi using
HPLC-UV-MS profiling, which yielded one strain, Daldinia
pyrenaica 047188. This strain was isolated from a lichen
Myelochroa aurulenta from the Republic of Korea, and it
produced a new class of metabolites with a unique UV profile
(Figure S2), which prompted us to subsequently perform a
large-scale culture, chromatographic separation, and structural
elucidation. Herein, we report the structure elucidation and
comprehensive stereochemical determination of three caged
polyketides, daldipyrenones A−C (1−3), with an unprece-
dented carbon skeleton comprising a xanthone core. Notably,
daldipyrenone A not only promoted adiponectin biosynthesis
during adipogenesis in human bone marrow mesenchymal
stem cells (hBM-MSCs) but also inhibited melanogenesis in
human melanonoma MNT-1 cells.

molecular formula of 1 was deduced to be C25H28O9 based on
high-resolution electrospray ionization mass spectrometry
(HRESIMS) data (12 unsaturations). The

HSQC NMR data (Table S1) identified the presence of two
secondary methyl groups, one tertiary methyl group, three
diastereotopic sp3 methylenes, six sp3 methines, and three
aromatic methines. Since the above data revealed the presence
of only 24 protons, the last four protons were attributed to
exchangeables. Analysis of the
1H−1H COSY spectrum
enabled one phenolic proton and three alcohol hydroxy
protons to be assigned. Besides the above-mentioned
protonated carbons, two ketones, two oxygenated sp2 carbons,

The complete planar structure of compound 1 was
established by interpreting 1D and 2D NMR spectroscopy
(Table S1). Analysis of 1H−1H COSY together with HSQC
provided three discrete partial structures (Figure 1): (I) ABC
spin system corresponding to C1−C2−C3 of the aromatic ring
(highlighted in red), (II) CH−CH(CH3)−CH corresponding
to C10a−C5(C11)−C6 (highlighted in blue), and (III)
CH(OH)−CH2−CH2−CH(O)−CH3 corresponding to
C18−C19−C20−C21−C22 (highlighted in green). The
partial structures were assembled by interpreting the HMBC
correlations.
First, the hydroxychromone structure was deduced by the
key HMBC correlations of the phenol hydroxy proton (δH
11.72, 4-OH) with C-3 and a ketone (δC 199.2, C-10) along
with the correlations of H-1 and H-2 with the highly
deshielded sp2 carbon (δC 159.6, C-9a). The methine carbon
C-10a in II, next to the methyl-bearing C-5, was evidently a
member of the chromone ring due to HMBC correlations of
H-10a with C-4a. Furthermore, the HMBC correlations of a
hydroxy proton (δH 6.33, 8-OH) with oxygenated quaternary
carbon (C-8a), oxygenated quaternary carbon (δC 73.4, C-8),
tertiary methyl carbon (δC 18.5, C-12), and remaining ketone
carbon (δC 205.8, C-7) suggested that the methyl- and
hydroxy-bearing quaternary carbon (C-8) connects C-8a and
the ketone group. The connection between ketone (C-7) and
C-6 was subsequently established following HMBC correla-
tions of H-5 and H-6 with C-7, forming the 5,8-dimethyl-7-
oxo-xanthone core structure. Partial structure III was clearly a
part of a pyran ring based on the HMBC correlations of 18-
OH (δH = 4.47) and the methyl-bearing oxymethine H-21 with
C-17 (δC 98.4). C-17 of this pyran ring was further directly
connected with the hydroxy-bearing methine carbon C-16 and
the oxygen next to the methylene carbon C-15 in a spiro
arrangement, which was confirmed by HMBC correlations of
the last hydroxy proton (δH 5.02, 16-OH), H-16, and H2-15
with spiroketal carbon C-17. At this point, two nonprotonated
double bond carbons, C-13 and C-14, remained unassigned.
HMBC correlations of H-16 and H2-15 with C-13 and C-14
indicated the presence of another pyran ring comprising a

unsuccessful. Then, 1 was acetylated to obtain a more
hydrophobic compound (1a). 1a was successfully crystallized
in a mixture of CHCl3 and CH3OH (5:1 ratio) after many
attempts with different solvents. X-ray diffraction analysis of 1a
(Figure 2) using a Bruker APEX-III diffractometer with a Mo
Kα (λ = 0.71073 Å) incident beam verified the elucidated
planar structure suggested by NMR data and established the
relative configuration (see the Supporting Information for the
detailed experimental procedures).
Compound 2 had the molecular formula C30H34O10 based
on (+)HRESIMS and NMR data (Table S1). The NMR
spectra of 2 were similar to those of 1, indicating that it was its
analogue. Compared with 1, two methyl groups (δH 1.77/δC
14.2 and 1.74/12.2) attached to double bond carbons (δC
137.7 and 127.8) appeared in place of the exchangeable proton
in 1. NMR analysis revealed that 2 contains a 2-methyl-2-
butenoyl group. Strong ROESY correlations between two
methyl groups and an HMBC correlation of H-16 with the acyl
carbon of the 2-methyl-2-butenoyl group indicated the
attachment of a tigloyl group at C-16 in 2. Compound 3,
also an analogue of 1, had the same molecular formula as 2,
including a tigloyl group. The oxymethine H-18 at δH 4.77
exhibited strong HMBC correlations with the carbonyl carbon
of the tigloyl group and the spiroketal carbon C-17, C-19, and
C-20 of the pyran, indicating attachment of the tigloyl group at
C-18 in 3.
Although the relative configuration of 1 was established by
X-ray crystallographic analysis, ROESY correlations were also
analyzed for comparison (Figure 2b). The relative config-
uration established by ROESY agreed with that determined by
the X-ray analysis (Figure 2). The absolute configuration of 1
was established by comparing its experimental electronic
circular dichroism (ECD) spectrum with the calculated ECD
spectra.6 The calculated conformer P1 and the experimental
conformer have identical negative and positive Cotton effects,
enabling the assignment of the absolute configuration (Figure
3). The stereochemistry of 2 and 3 was analogous to that of 1
based on the NMR and CD spectra.
Compound 1 (30 μM) significantly promoted adiponectin
biosynthesis by 2.85-fold compared with that of the control
during adipogenesis in hBM-MSCs (Figure 4A, Figure S43). In
the concentration−response analysis, the half-maximal effective
concentration (EC50) values of 1 and glibenclamide (GLI)
were determined as 26.3 and 1.3 μM, respectively (Figure 4B).
The adiponectin-secretion-promoting activity of 1 was more
potent than that of aspirin (ASA), although its effect was
weaker compared to antidiabetic GLI. Since antimelanogenic
activity of adiponectin has been reported in several clinical
experiments,7 compound 1 was also evaluated for the
antimelanogenic activity. In result, 1 exhibited significant
antimelanogenic activity in the human melanoma MNT-1 cell
line (Figure 4C). To validate the antimelanogenic effect of 1
on MNT-1 cells, the concentration-dependent activity was
investigated at noncytotoxic concentrations (Figure S42A).
The EC50 values of 1, arbutin (ARB), hydroquinone (HQ),
and kojic acid (KA) were determined as 3.36, 54.49, 4.66, and
66.65 μM, respectively (Figure 4D). The antimelanogenic
activity of 1 was more potent than those of ARB and KA,
which are both widely used as whitening cosmetic ingredients.

It was also as effective as that of HQ. 1 possibly stimulates
adiponectin-secretion by human adipocytes, resulting in
synergistic antimelanogenic activity in human epidermal
melanocytes. In conclusion, 1 has therapeutic potential in
human hyperpigmentation disorders like melanoma as a dual
modulator with both antimelanogenic and adiponectin-
secretion-promoting activities.
Significantly, daldipyrenones A−C (1−3) were found to be
uncommon caged xanthones with a bicyclo[2.2.2]octane ring
featuring an unusual spiro-azaphilone unit in the skeleton. To
gain insights into daldipyrenone biogenesis, the genome of the
daldipyrenone-producing strain Daldinia sp. 047188 was
sequenced. Full genome sequencing of D. pyrenaica and
subsequent BGC analysis using AntiSMASH8 indicated that
compounds 1−3 are likely combined products of two separate
BGCs, clusters 6.1 and 9.2. Cluster 6.1 harbors the genes
homologous to polyketide synthase ChrA (86% identity) and
ketoreductase partner ChrB (85% identity), thus suggesting 1-
(2,6-dihydroxyphenyl)but-2-en-1-one (PBEO) synthesis.9
Conversely, cluster 9.2 was predicted to produce the
azaphilone-type metabolite because it contains genes homol-
ogous to polyketide synthase CHGG_10027 (66% identity)
and monooxygenase CHGG_10021 (66% identity) from the
cochliodons BGC.10 Accordingly, identifying two BGCs
potentially responsible for producing 1−3 allowed us to
propose their biosynthetic pathways (Figure 5). Retro-
biosynthetic analysis indicates that the daldipyrenone scaffolds
of 1−3 are likely formed by radical coupling or Michael
addition between PBEO (precursor A) and hydroxylated 5-
dehydropestafolide A (precursor B) together with subsequent
nucleophilic addition and dehydration via keto−enol tautome-
rization (Figure 5). The precursor A would be derived from
cluster 6.1 while the precursor B containing an azaphilone
moiety is likely to be produced by cluster 9.2. The enzymes
encoded by ORF0353 and ORF0357 together are predicted to
function as polyketide synthases based on their sequence
homology to LovF (94%) and CHGG_10027 (66%) and
consequently produce the 3-methylorcinaldehyde polyketide
core branched with 6-hydroxy-2-heptanone. Aromatic ring
oxidation by monooxygenase ORF0351, homologous to
CHGG_10021 (66% identity), and subsequent spiroketal
formation would create the diene-containing spiro-azaphilone
scaffold. Whether the spiroketal formation is catalyzed by an
enzyme needs to be determined, as cluster 9.1 contains several
genes with unknown functions (ORF0348 and ORF0350).
Further hydroxylation in the spiroketal moiety by a
cytochrome P450 oxidase encoded by ORF0355 followed by
the reduction of diene to monoene in the azaphilone moiety
would generate precursor B, completing the proposed
biosynthesis of 1−3. Interestingly, this biosynthesis is
completely unrelated to the formation of the well-known
caged xanthones produced by plants in the genus Garcinia.11
