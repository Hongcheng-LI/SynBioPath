ABSTRACT: Oxabornyl polyenes represent a unique group of
polyketides characterized by a central polyene core flanked by a
conserved oxabornyl moiety and a structurally diverse oxygen
heterocyclic ring. They are widely distributed in fungi and possess
a variety of biological activities. Due to the significant spatial
separation between the two stereogenic ring systems, it is difficult to
establish their overall relative configurations. Here, we isolated three
oxabornyl polyenes, prugosenes A1−A3 (1−3), from Talaromyces sp.
JNU18266-01. Although these compounds were first reported from
Penicillium rugulosum, their overall relative and absolute config-
urations remained unassigned. By employing ozonolysis in
combination with ECD calculations, we were able to establish their
absolute configurations, and additionally obtained seven new
chemical derivatives (4−10). Notably, through NMR data analysis and quantum chemical calculations, we achieved the structural
revision of prugosene A2. Furthermore, prugosenes A1−A3 exhibited potent antiviral activity against the respiratory syncytial virus,
with compound 1 displaying an IC50 value of 6.3 μM. Our study thus provides a valuable reference for absolute configuration
assignment of oxabornyl polyene compounds.
■INTRODUCTION
Oxabornyl polyenes are a class of fungi-specific polyketides,
characterized by a long polyene moiety in the middle, flanked
by a conserved oxabornyl unit of oxabicyclo[2.2.1]heptane and
a structurally diverse oxygen-containing heterocyclic ring. The
earliest documented occurrence of these compounds from
fungi dates back to 2005, when shimalactone A was isolated
from Emericella variecolor.1 Subsequently, prugosenes2 and
ukulactones,3,4 which feature a linear pentaene unit ending
with an oxabicyclo[2.2.1]heptane and a dihydropyran, were
identified. These compounds exhibit potent inhibitory activity
against the NADH-fumarate reductase system targeted for
treating helminthiasis.5−7 After that, analogues with similar
structures including wortmannilactones,8−11 varilactones,10
and emericelactones12 were obtained from diverse fungi.
To date, a total of 28 natural oxabornyl polyenes have been
reported (Figure S1). All these compounds possess a
conserved oxabornyl structure at one end, but differ in the
length of the polyene moiety and the structure of the second
heterocyclic ring at the other end (Figures 1 and S1). Since
these compounds usually contain two distantly separated
stereoclusters (more than five atoms between the two
stereoclusters), designated as cluster A and cluster B here
(Figure 1), their diastereomers may also possess apparently
identical 1H/13C NMR data.13 This will make it difficult to
assign their overall relative configurations by NMR data
comparison.
On the other hand, determining the absolute configurations
of oxabornyl polyenes de novo is a challenging task, despite the
ease in defining the relative configurations within cluster A and
cluster B.2−4,8,14 Since cluster A and cluster B are entirely
segregated from each other in the molecule, there still exist
four possible stereoisomers (two pairs of enantiomers).
However, some studies have only calculated the electronic
circular dichroism (ECD) for one pair of enantiomers.9,11,15
Although Yang and co-workers considered both pairs of
enantiomers when studying emericelactones A−D,12 unfortu-
nately the ECD calculations failed to distinguish the four
stereoisomers. Therefore, additional specific rotation calcu-
lations are required to determine their absolute configurations.
Since the specific rotation values may strongly depend on
wavelength, temperature, solvent, and concentration, errors
frequently occur in both measured and calculated values.16,17

Examples are known for which optical rotation calculations to
determine the absolute configuration of natural products
proved unreliable.18,19 In a recent study of varilactones,10 Li
and co-workers first used the modified Mosher’s method to
determine the absolute configuration of cluster A after
reduction of the C-3 carbonyl and then established the
absolute configurations of cluster B by ECD calculation.
However, the esterification process using Mosher’s reagents
may encounter limitations due to steric hindrance and might
not be applicable to all oxabornyl polyenes.
Prugosenes A1−A3 were first isolated from the sponge-
derived marine fungus Penicillium rugulosum, but their overall
relative and absolute configurations remained unassigned.2
Here, we reisolated prugosenes A1−A3 (1−3) from
Talaromyces sp. JNU18266-01 and established their full
absolute configurations by ozonolysis in combination with
ECD calculations. Additionally, we revised the C-18
configuration of prugosene A2. Furthermore, we investigated
the antiviral activities of compounds 1−3 against the
respiratory syncytial virus and found that 1 exhibited potent
antiviral activity. This study offers a valuable avenue for
determining the absolute configuration of this class of
polyketides.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
During our exploration for bioactive metabolites derived from
fungi, we successfully isolated three compounds (1−3) from
Talaromyces sp. JNU18266-01. The 1H and 13C NMR data
(Table 1) obtained in CD3OD exhibited an exact match with
the reported data for prugosenes A1−A3, which were
previously isolated from P. rugulosum,2 a fungus closely related
to Talaromyces at the phylogenetic level. Furthermore, it is
noteworthy that known oxabornyl polyenes possess a
conserved oxabicyclo[2.2.1]heptane moiety (Figure S1),
implying their formation through a conserved biosynthetic
machinery. Based on these compelling observations, we
confidently postulate that compounds 1−3 are indeed identical
to prugosenes A1−A3. Despite the assignment of relative
configurations for clusters A and B within prugosenes A1−A3,
their overall relative and absolute configurations remain
unknown due to significant spatial separation between the
two stereoclusters. In this study, we aimed to elucidate the
relative and absolute configurations of compounds 1−3 by
employing ozonolysis in combination with ECD calculations.
Compound 1 was obtained as a yellow oil, and its molecular
formula was established as C28H36O5 from the HR-ESI-MS
(m/z 435.2537 [M + H+] −H2O, calcd for C28H35O4,
435.2535), indicating the presence of 11 degrees of
unsaturation. The 1H and 13C NMR data of 1 (Tables 1 and
S1) recorded in CD3OD were in good agreement with those
reported for prugosene A1 isolated from P. rugulosum,
suggesting that both compounds possess the same planar
structure.2 Further analysis of the one-dimensional (1D) and
two-dimensional (2D) NMR data (COSY, HSQC, and
HMBC) confirmed the planar structure of compound 1,
which consists of a linear pentaene connecting an
oxabicyclo[2.2.1]heptane and a dihydropyran (Figures 1 and
S2). The double-bond configurations of the pentaene unit in 1
were assigned as 7E, 9E, 11E, 13E, and 15E based on the
coupling constants (J7,8 = 15.0 Hz, J9,10 = 14.5 Hz, J11,12 = 14.5
Hz, J13,14 = 14.4 Hz) and the key NOESY correlations
(between H-14 and H3-25, between H-15 and H-17).
Furthermore, the NOESY data of 1 matched well with those
described in the literature, enabling the assignment of the
relative configurations in clusters A and B (Figures 1 and S2−
S4). However, determining the overall relative and absolute
configuration of 1 will be challenging due to the significant
spatial separation between the two stereoclusters (more than
five atoms).
Next, we turned our attention to establish the absolute
configuration of 1. Compound 1 exists as an oil, and its long
polyene chain structure makes obtaining a crystal difficult.
Although the relative configurations of cluster A and cluster B
were determined, there still remained four possible stereo-
isomers for compound 1. Furthermore, the experimental ECD
spectrum of 1 only shows a strong negative Cotton effect (CE)
at 230 nm and a weak positive CE around 307 nm (Figure S7).
Thus, it is not feasible to directly use ECD calculation to
distinguish between the four stereoisomers. According to the
literature,1,10 we first used modified Mosher’s method20 to
establish the absolute configuration of the oxabicyclo[2.2.1]-
heptane unit in 1. Treatment of 1 with NaBH4 yields the
reduced products 4 (Table S4 and Figures S53−S59) and 5
(Table S5 and Figures S60−S66), and the orientation of the 3-
OH in 4 and 5 was determined based on coupling constants
and ROESY correlations (Figure S8a). Despite numerous
attempts, we were unable to obtain the MTPA esters to
establish the absolute configuration of the oxabicyclo[2.2.1]-
heptane ring.
Compound 1 was then degraded by ozonolysis to obtain
fragments containing either the oxabicyclo[2.2.1]heptane unit
or a dihydropyran ring.21 After purification and structural
identification (Figures 1 and S8b), three ozonides, 6 (Table S6
and Figures S67−S73), 7 (Table S7 and Figures S74−S80),
and 8 (Table S8 and Figures S81−S87), were obtained,
confirming the presence of a pentaene unit in 1. The double-
bond configurations of the pentaene unit in 1 as trans were
further supported by the coupling constants (J7,8 = 15.7 Hz,
J9,10 = 14.8 Hz, J11,12 = 15.2 Hz) observed in the 1H NMR data
of 7 (Table S7 and Figure S74). To further confirm the relative
configuration of the dihydropyran ring (cluster B), we
performed quantum chemical NMR calculations on ozonolysis
product 8. Theoretical calculations of 13C NMR data of a pair
of possible epimers, (4S*,7R*,8S*)-8 and (4S*,7S*,8S*)-8,
were carried out using the GIAO method at the mPW1PW91/
6-31+G(d,p) level in Gaussian09. Based on the mean absolute
error (MAE) evaluation and DP4+ probability (Figure S13),
the relative configuration of compound 8 was determined as
4S*, 7S*, 8S*, which was consistent with the deduction from
the ROESY experiment. Finally, through a comparison of the
experimental and calculated ECD spectra, the absolute
configuration of the oxabicyclo[2.2.1]heptane moiety in 6
and the dihydropyran moiety in 8 was determined to be 2S,

Compound 2 was also obtained as a yellow oil. The
molecular formula of 2 was determined to be C28H36O5

Analysis of the 1D and 2D NMR data of 2 confirmed that its
planar structure is identical to that of prugosene A2 (Figures 1
and S2). However, our analysis suggests that the published
relative configuration of the dihydropyran ring in prugosene
A2 may be incorrect.2 In the original report on prugosene A2,
H-17 and H3-22 were determined to have the same orientation
based on the NOESY correlation between them.2 The authors
also claimed that H3-24 should have the same orientation with
H-17 and H3-22 because no cross-peak was observed between
H-15 and H3-24 in the NOESY spectrum.2 However,
determining the orientation of H3-24 solely from the absence
of a cross-peak between H-15/H3-24 is insufficient, and
additional evidence is required for accurate determination. To
solve this problem, we performed NMR spectroscopy of 2 in
DMSO-d6. We observed ROESY correlations between H-17
and 18-OH/H3-22 (Table S11 and Figure S105), indicating
that H-17, 18-OH, and H3-22 are on the same side of the
dihydropyran ring, while H3-24 is on the opposite side (Figure
S5). Thus, we determined the relative configuration of the
dihydropyran ring in 2 to be 17S*, 18R*, 21S*.

Compound 3 was also obtained as a yellow oil. Its molecular
formula was established as C28H36O5 by HR-ESI-MS (m/z
435.2533 [M + H+] −H2O, calcd for C28H35O4, 435.2535),

Compound 3 (Tables 1 and S3) shared the same spectroscopic
data as prugosene A3,2 indicating that they shared the same
planar structure, as well as the relative configurations of cluster
A and cluster B, which was supported by detailed analyses of
the 1H−1H COSY, HSQC, HMBC, and ROESY data (Figures
1, S2, and S6).
To determine the absolute configuration of 3, ozonolysis was
employed, and three ozonides were obtained (Figures 1 and
S8b). These ozonides were identified as 6 (Table S6 and
Figures S67−S73), 7 (Table S7 and Figure S74), and 10

analysis of NMR data and ECD spectra. As with 1 and 2,
the double-bond configurations of the pentaene unit in 3 were
assigned as all E, based on the key ROESY correlations
combined with the coupling constants of 3 (Table S3 and
Figure S2) and 7 (Table S7). The relative configuration of 10
was further confirmed by quantum chemical NMR calculations

established as 2S, 4R, 5S, 6S, 17R, 18R, 21S on the basis of the
ECD calculation results of compounds 6 (Figures 2a and S10)
and 10 (Figures 2d and S22).

Compounds 1−3 were tested for their anti-respiratory
syncytial virus (RSV) activities using the cytopathic effect
(CPE) reduction assay. Respiratory syncytial virus is a
common respiratory virus that poses a serious threat to infants
and children, causing tens of thousands of deaths each year.28
While antiviral drugs like ribavirin and palivizumab have been
approved by the FDA for RSV treatment, concerns about their
efficacy and safety have been raised.28,29 Therefore, the search
for effective anti-RSV drugs is of utmost importance. As shown
in Table 2 and Figure S106, compounds 1−3 displayed
powerful antiviral activity against RSV, compounds 1 and 2
exhibited comparable anti-RSV activities to the positive control
ribavirin, with IC50 values of 6.3 and 12 μM, respectively, and 3
showed moderate anti-RSV activity with an IC50 value of 25
μM.
Although prugosenes A1−A3 were originally isolated from
the fungus P. rugulosum,2 it is not surprising that we reisolated
them from Talaromyces sp. considering the close phylogenetic
relationship between Penicillium and Talaromyces.30 The
structure of the oxabicyclo[2.2.1]heptane moiety in oxabornyl
polyenes is conserved (Figure S1). This suggests that there
exists a conserved biosynthetic machinery for the biosynthesis
of oxabornyl polyenes in fungi.24−27 Although there are
significant differences in the magnitudes of the specific rotation
for compounds 1 and 2 (−48.5, +49.5) and prugosenes A1 and
A2 (−12, +3),2 it is likely that the compounds 1−3 isolated in
the present study are the same compounds as prugosenes A1−
A3. However, we could not rule out the possibility that they
are stereoisomers containing cluster A and/or cluster B with
inverted configurations (Figure 1).
It is interesting to note that 2 and 3 have identical absolute
configurations in cluster A and different configurations in
cluster B. However, they exhibit almost identical experimental
ECD spectra (Figure S7), suggesting that the absolute
configurations of oxabornyl polyenes cannot be determined
directly by the ECD calculations. The modified Mosher’s
method has been successfully applied to determine the
absolute configuration of the oxabicyclo[2.2.1]heptane in
varilactones.10 However, this method failed to determine the
absolute configuration of 1. This is probably caused by a steric
hindrance of the polyene chain or the adjacent methyl groups
when 3-OH reacts with Mosher’s reagents. Thus, the absolute
configurations of each stereocluster of compounds 1−3 should
be determined separately.
The reaction of olefins with ozone is one of the common
methods for the cleavage of carbon−carbon double bonds, and
the ozone attack is electrophilic in nature.31 Here, 1−3 were
subjected to ozonolysis under controlled conditions, thereby
separating the two stereoclusters into two fragments without
affecting the double bonds in the dihydropyran ring. Since the
double bonds of the polyene have more electron density, it is
unsurprising that ozone is more reactive toward them.
Cleavage primarily occurred at the central double bonds
rather than the terminal C7−C8 and C15−C16, an
observation that can be explained by steric hindrance, as was
also observed in the ozonolysis of other compounds.32,33
Therefore, ozonolysis combined with ECD calculations should
be an effective approach to establish the absolute config-
urations of oxabornyl polyenes.
■EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
General Experimental Procedures. Optical rotations were
measured on a JASCO P1020 digital polarimeter (JASCO Interna-
tional Co. Ltd.). UV data were recorded using a JASCO V-550 UV/
vis spectrometer. ECD spectra were recorded on a Chirascan-plus
qCD spectrometer (Applied Photophysics Ltd.) at room temperature.
IR data were recorded on a JASCO FT/IR-480 plus spectrometer.
NMR spectra were acquired with Bruker AV 400 and Bruker AV 600
spectrometers (Bruker BioSpin Group) using the solvent signals
(CD3OD: δH 3.30/δC 49.0; CD3CN: δH 1.94/δC 118.26; CDCl3: δH
7.26/δC 77.0; DMSO-d6: δH 2.50/δC 39.5) as internal standards. ESI-
MS spectra were acquired on a Bruker Amazon SL mass spectrometer
(Bruker Daltonic Int.). HR-ESI-MS spectra were obtained on a
Waters Synapt G2 TOF mass spectrometer (Waters Corporation).
Column chromatography (CC) was carried out on silica gel (200−
300 mesh) (Qingdao Haiyang Chemical Group Corporation). TLC
was performed on precoated silica gel plates (SGF254, 0.2 mm, Yantai
Chemical Industry Research Institute). Analytical HPLC was
performed on a Dionex HPLC system equipped with an Ultimate

Fungal Material. The fungus Talaromyces sp. JNU18266-01 was
isolated from the air environment of Jinan University, Guangzhou,
China (longitude: 113.34215, latitude: 23.13292) in January 2018. A
90 mm Petri dish containing 20 mL of potato glucose agar (PDA) was
placed in an air environment. To inhibit bacterial growth,
chloramphenicol (100 mg/L) was added to the PDA. After 7 days,
the formed fungal colonies were transferred to new Petri dishes with
PDA for purification. Guaranteed pure strains were transferred to
PDA agar slants for molecular identification and chemical research.
The strain was identified as Talaromyces sp. based on its
morphological characteristics and gene sequence analysis. The
ribosomal internal transcribed spacer (ITS) and the 5.8S rRNA
gene sequences (ITS1-5.8S-ITS2) of the strain have been deposited at
GenBank as OR781530. The strain was cultured on a PDA slant at 4
°C and was deposited at the Institute of Traditional Chinese
Medicine and Natural Products, Jinan University.

temperature overnight. The reaction mixture was concentrated under
reduced pressure and then further purified by semipreparative HPLC

Antiviral Assay. The human larynx epidermoid carcinoma cell
line (HEp-2, ATCC CCL-23) was maintained in Dulbecco’s modified

(FBS, Biological Industries) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere
supplied with 5% CO2. Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV A2, ATCC
VR-1540) was propagated in HEp-2 cells. At 2−3 days after the viral
infection, the virus suspension on the cells was harvested and stored at
−80 °C until use. For the CPE assay, HEp-2 cells were seeded in 96-
well culture plates at a density of 1.2 × 104 cells per well and
incubated overnight. The cells were then infected with 100TCID50 of
RSV and treated with compounds at different concentrations.
Ribavirin (Sigma) was used as a positive control. At 72 h after the
viral infection, the RSV-induced CPE was observed and scored under
a microscope.
