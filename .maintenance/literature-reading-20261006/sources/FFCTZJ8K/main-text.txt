Radical S‑Adenosylmethionine Sulfurtransferase MybB Catalyzed
Formation of the 4‑Thiazolidinone Core in Mycobacidin Represents
an Intersection between Primary and Secondary Metabolism

ABSTRACT: Mycobacidin is an antitubercular antibiotic structur-
ally composed of a sulfur-containing 4-thiazolidinone ring, yet its
biosynthesis including the mechanism of sulfur incorporation has
remained an open question since its discovery in 1952. In this study,
the mycobacidin biosynthetic gene cluster is identified from soil-
dwelling Streptomyces, and the corresponding biosynthetic pathway
starting with 7-oxoheptanoate is characterized. The radical SAM
enzyme MybB catalyzes two sulfur insertion reactions, thereby
bridging C7 and C10 to complete the 4-thiazolidinone heterocycle as
the final step in mycobacidin maturation. MybB is a homologue of
biotin synthase, and in both biosynthetic pathways, the bridging
sulfur originates from the degradation of an enzymatic auxiliary
[Fe2S2] cluster. Introduction of the two C−S bonds during 4-
thiazolidinone cyclization is shown to take place in a fixed sequence with the terminal C10−S bond generated first followed by the
C7−S bond. MybB thus represents a generalization of biotin synthase activity that contributes to the maturation of not only primary
but also secondary metabolites via sequential sulfur insertion reactions to yield sulfur containing heterocycles.
■INTRODUCTION
Mycobacidin (1), also known as actithiazic acid, cinnamonin
and acidomycin, is an antitubercular agent produced by soil-
dwelling Streptomyces.1,2 This metabolite exhibits selective
antibiotic activity against Mycobacterium turberculosis (TB)
while exhibiting no significant activity against other mycobac-
teria as well as Gram-positive and Gram-negative micro-
organisms in vitro.1,3 Therefore, mycobacidin has drawn
particular attention from the biomedical and pharmaceutical
communities due to its promise as a lead compound for the
development of new antitubercular agents with a narrow
spectrum.
The chemical structure of mycobacidin (1, Figure 1A) is
comprised of a heptanoate chain linked to an unusual 4-
thiazolidinone core (2), in which a sulfur center connects C7
and C10 to form a sulfur bridge in a heterocyclic assembly.
Since its discovery in 1952,1,2 the enzymology and biosynthetic
mechanism behind introduction of this intramolecular sulfur
linkage has remained an enigma as the gene cluster responsible
for mycobacidin biosynthesis had not yet been identified.
However, mycobacidin was previously shown to inhibit the
cellular production of biotin (vitamin B7, 4) in Mycobacterium
turberculosis,4 which is a primary metabolite that plays critical
roles in the tricarboxylic acid cycle, membrane lipid biosyn-
thesis and amino acid metabolism.5 The specific target of 1 was
later found to be the biotin synthase (BioB),6,7 a radical S-
adenosylmethionine (SAM) enzyme that catalyzes tetrahy-
drothiophene cyclization via a radical-mediated sulfur cross-
bridging reaction in biotin biosynthesis (3 →4, Figure
1B).8−19 The inhibition of biotin biosynthesis by mycobacidin
(1) may thus arise from its structural resemblance to biotin (4)
allowing it to act perhaps as a competitive inhibitor for M.
tuberculosis biotin synthase (MtBioB) thereby accounting for

■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Mycobacidin Biosynthetic Gene Cluster. In order to
test this hypothesis, the genome of the mycobacidin producing
strain Streptomyces virginiae ISP-50941,2 was analyzed and a
total of 25 genes annotated as encoding putative radical SAM
enzymes20−26 were identified. Considering that the nitrogen in
the 4-thiazolidinone moiety (2) may also need to be
introduced during the assembly of 2, eight of the above
genes, each coclustered with an aminotransferase gene, were
selected as possible candidates responsible for constructing the
core (2) in mycobacidin (1). Each of these genes was cloned
into a pET-28b(+) vector and overexpressed in E. coli together
with the auxiliary plasmid pDB1282, which facilitates Fe−S
cluster reconstitution in vivo.27 After aerobic protein
purification by affinity chromatography, the Fe−S clusters of
these purified His6-tagged radical SAM enzymes were further
reconstituted with Fe(NH4)2(SO4)2 and Na2S under anaerobic
conditions (see Supporting Information). Each enzyme (100
μM) was then incubated with chemically synthesized 7-
aminoacetoheptanoate (8, 1 mM), 2 mM dithiothreitol, 1 mM
SAM, 1 mM Na2S, and 1 mM sodium dithionite in 50 mM 4-
(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid (HEPES)
buffer (pH 7.5) for 6 h at room temperature anaerobically in
a glovebox.10 The reaction mixtures were then deproteinized
by centrifugal filtration through a YM-10 membrane and the
filtrates were analyzed by LC-MS. Among the eight candidates,
only one (rSAM13) was found to catalyze the conversion of 8
to a product with a molecular weight (calcd for C9H15NO3S
[M + H]+ 218.0845, found 218.0832) and retention time
consistent with those of the standard 1 prepared according to a
reported procedure6 (Figure 2C).
The flanking regions of rsam13 were then analyzed in order
to determine whether it may be part of a gene cluster
associated with mycobacidin biosynthesis. Four additional
genes were identified forming a gene cluster (myb) including
orf-1 as well as mybC both annotated as pyridoxal 5′-phosphate
(PLP) dependent aminotransferases, mybD as an ATP-grasp
protein, and orf1 as a potential acetyl transferase (Figure 2A).
The rsam13 gene was renamed mybB accordingly. Based on the
annotated functions of these genes, a plausible mycobacidin
biosynthetic pathway could be proposed starting with the
aliphatic precursor 7-oxoheptanoate (6, Figure 2B), which may
be derived from the reduction of pimeloyl-CoA or pimeloyl
acyl carrier protein (5) during lipid metabolism. This
chemistry is well-documented in living systems. For example,
biosynthesis of lysine in Saccharomyces cerevisiae via the α-
aminoadipate pathway involves reduction of a thioacyl
enzyme−substrate adduct by NADPH to yield α-aminoadipate
semialdehyde.28 The terminal aldehyde of 6 may then be
reductively aminated in a reaction catalyzed by one of the PLP-
dependent transaminases (i.e., the orf-1 gene product or
MybC) to produce 7-aminoheptanoate (7). Subsequent
acetylation of 7 catalyzed by MybD or the orf1 gene product
will generate 8, which serves as the substrate for the sulfur
cross-bridging reaction to afford 1.
Mycobacidin Biosynthetic Pathway. The activity of the
myb encoded gene products was investigated using hetero-
logously expressed and purified proteins. The genes orf-1,
mybC, mybD and orf1 were each cloned into a pET-28b(+)
vector, overexpressed in E. coli and purified as the N-terminal
His6-tagged proteins for in vitro characterization. When 10 μM
MybC was incubated with 1 mM chemically synthesized 6, 0.2
mM dithiothreitol, 0.2 mM PLP and 2 mM SAM in 50 mM
HEPES buffer (pH 7.5) at 37 °C for 3 h, a new product was
observed by LC-MS. This product had both an MS profile
(calcd for C7H15NO2 [M + H]+ 146.1176, found 146.1171)
and retention time identical to those of a synthetic standard of
7 (Figure 2D). Besides SAM, L-lysine could also serve as an
amino donor, while the remaining 19 common amino acids
could not (Figure S8). No new product formation was
observed when the orf-1 gene product was incubated with 6.
These results showed that MybC could catalyze the amination
of 6 to 7 as an initial step in the proposed mycobacidin
biosynthetic pathway.

ase, it was originally hypothesized to catalyze N-acetylation of
7 to 8; however, incubation of 50 μM orf1 gene product with 1

Origin of the Bridging Sulfur in Mycobacidin. Having
established the biosynthetic pathway of mycobacidin, efforts
were next directed at investigating the origin of the newly
incorporated sulfide and the mechanism of the MybB catalyzed
reaction. As depicted in Figure 3, nonpeptide based thiolation
reactions catalyzed by radical SAM enzymes are not common
in natural product biosynthesis. Besides BioB,8−19 only a
handful of cases are known in primary metabolism including
LipA from lipoic acid (13) biosynthesis (11 →12, Figure
3A),27,29−33 the ribosome methylthiolation reaction catalyzed
by RimO (14 →15, Figure 3B),34−37 and tRNA modification
catalyzed by MiaB (16 →17, Figure 3C).37−41 To date, ThnL
is the only example found in secondary metabolism where it
catalyzes C−S bond formation between 18 and pantetheine
during the biosynthesis of thienamycin (20, Figure 3D).42
The origin of the incorporated sulfide in biotin biosynthesis
has been demonstrated to be a [Fe2S2] auxiliary cluster in BioB
from E. coli (referred to as Type I biotin synthase);14,16,43
however, a [Fe4S5] auxiliary cluster was shown to serve a
similar role with the reaction catalyzed by BioB from obligate
anaerobes such as Blautia obeum (a Type II biotin synthase).44
Similarly, LipA utilizes a [Fe4S4] auxiliary cluster as the sulfur
donor,30,31 whereas RimO and MiaB serve as methylthiolases
that also employ an auxiliary [Fe4S4] or [Fe3S4] cluster to
coordinate either a methylthiolate or equivalent generated
from a pentasulfide bridge as the thiol donor.37,45,46 In all
cases, an auxiliary iron−sulfur cluster functions either as the
sulfur donor or plays an important role in sulfur transfer.
To gain more insights into MybB catalysis, the structure of
MybB was predicted using AlphaFold 3.0Beta47 and is shown
aligned with that of E. coli BioB in Figure 4C. Combining the
structural model with the sequence alignment of MybB and E.
coli BioB implied that MybB is a Type I biotin synthase
containing a canonical radical SAM [Fe4S4] binding motif
CxxxCxxC composed of residues Cys65, Cys69 and Cys72, as
well as four additional ligands projected to coordinate an
auxiliary [Fe2S2] cluster (Arg272, Cys109, Cys142 and
Cys202).11 Indeed, iron and sulfide titration of reconstituted
MybB revealed an iron content of 6.0 ± 0.1 mol per mole
protein monomer and a sulfide content of 7.1 ± 0.5 mol per
mole protein monomer, indicating that MybB coordinates an
auxiliary [Fe2S2] cluster in addition to the catalytic [Fe4S4]
radical SAM cluster. The presence of an [Fe2S2] cluster was
also supported by the characteristic absorbance at 452 nm48 of
the as-isolated MybB (Figure 4B). This is analogous to the
[Fe2S2]-type BioB from E. coli, with which MybB shares 37%
sequence identity. Furthermore, single point mutation of a
predicted binding residue associated with either cluster (i.e.,
C65A or C109A) led to inactive mutant enzymes indicating
the importance of both clusters for MybB catalysis (Figure S6).
In order to identify the source of the bridging sulfur, MybB
was first reconstituted with natural abundance Na2S and
incubated with 8 in the presence of 1 mM Na2
34S; however,
enrichment of 1 with 34S was not detected (Figure S7). This
observation argued against free sulfide or bisulfide as the
immediate sulfur donor and instead suggested that the sulfur
may be derived from the auxiliary [Fe2S2] cluster mimicking
the chemistry of E. coli BioB (Figure 4A). In order to test this
hypothesis, the Fe−S clusters of MybB were first disrupted by
treatment with EDTA, resulting in decolorization of the
otherwise brown MybB protein solution. After removal of
EDTA using an EconoPac 10 DG column, the iron−sulfur
clusters were reconstituted under anaerobic conditions using
sodium sulfide (natural abundance or 34S-enriched), dithio-
threitol and Fe(NH4)2(SO4)2 (Figure S5). The reconstituted
MybB was then incubated as described earlier with 8, SAM,
dithiothreitol and sodium dithionite for 6 h at room
temperature; however, in neither case was production of 1
observed. Addition of 1 mM sodium sulfide to the incubation
mixture did not change the experimental outcome. The loss of
enzyme activity may be attributed to partial denaturation of
MybB in the presence of EDTA consistent with the observed
color change of the reconstituted versus the wild-type enzyme.

performed in the presence of the chaperone protein IscU,
which is an Fe−S scaffold protein that has been shown to

Incorporation of sulfur from the MybB auxiliary [Fe2S2]
cluster would result in its degradation, which can be monitored
at the characteristic 452 nm absorbance of [Fe2S2]2+
clusters.10,14 As shown in Figure 4E, the absorbance at 452
nm of the MybB incubation decreased over time (0−120 min),
whereas the difference spectrum between the unreacted
control and MybB assay showed gradual formation of a peak
consistent with a [Fe2S2]2+ cluster (Figure 4F). A positive
control was also performed using the E. coli BioB (Figure S12),
which has been established to catalyze the formation of biotin
from dethiobiotin by transferring a sulfur from its auxiliary
[Fe2S2] cluster.43 This control demonstrated similar changes in
the absorption spectra as observed in the MybB assay. Taken
together, the above results are consistent with the bridging
sulfur in the 4-thizaolidinone moiety arising from the auxiliary
[Fe2S2] cluster of MybB similar to the case of E. coli BioB.14,43
The myb Cluster Is Bifunctional. The similarity in
sequence alignment, iron−sulfur cluster content, and the
catalytic activity of S. virginiae MybB and E. coli BioB strongly
suggests that they are homologous enzymes and may be
functionally interchangeable (Figure 5A). Therefore, dethio-
biotin (3) was chemically synthesized and incubated with
reconstituted MybB under the aforementioned conditions. LC-
MS analysis of the resulting reaction mixture indeed showed
the production of biotin (4) having the same exact mass (calcd
for C10H16N2O3S [M + H]+ 245.0954, found 245.0945) and
retention time as the commercial standard (Figure 5B). This
result demonstrated that MybB not only catalyzes 4-
thiozolidinone ring formation in 1 but can also act as a biotin
synthase. Moreover, mycobacidin showed an inhibitory effect
on MybB acting as a biotin synthase resulting in a significant
reduction in biotin formation following a 6 h incubation of 3 in
the presence of 10-fold 1 (Figure 5B). As mentioned above,
mycobacidin was previously shown to be inhibitory against M.
tuberculosis biotin biosynthesis both in vivo and in vitro7 and a
competitive inhibitor for E. coli BioB in vitro.6 However,
mycobacidin has no effect on E. coli growth as its accumulation
in E. coli is about 30-fold less compared to that in M.
tuberculosis.6 Thus, the inhibitory effect of mycobacidin toward
biotin biosynthesis appears to be species specific (e.g., M.

The above results also indicated that the myb cluster itself
may be bifunctional given the similarities between the
biosynthetic pathways for biotin and mycobacidin. As depicted
in Figure 5A, biotin maturation5 is initiated upon PLP-
dependent BioF catalyzed incorporation of an
L-alanyl
fragment to pimeloyl-CoA or a pimeloyl-acyl carrier protein
(ACP) precursor (21) to form 7-keto-8-amino pelargonic acid
(KAPA, 22). This is followed by PLP-dependent BioA
mediated transamination to generate 7,8-diamino pelargonic
acid (DAPA, 23). The ATP-grasp BioD then catalyzes urea
cross-linkage to form two new amide bonds producing
dethiobiotin (3). Subsequent BioB-catalyzed sulfur insertion
completes biotin biosynthesis.
In order to determine whether MybC and MybD could
replace BioA and BioD (28% and 30% sequence identity,
respectively), DAPA (23) was chemically prepared (1 mM)
and incubated with MybD and ATP in the presence of
NaHCO3 resulting in the formation of dethiobiotin (3) (calcd
for C10H18N2O3 [M + H]+ 215.1390, found 215.1387) (Figure
5C). Examining the reverse of PLP-dependent transamination,
a new peak was detected with a molecular weight consistent
with that of KAPA (22) (calcd for C9H17NO3 [M + H]+
188.1281, found 188.1276) when 1 mM DAPA (23) was
incubated with MybC and 2-ketoglutarate (Figure 5C). These
results demonstrated that MybC, MybD and MybB have dual
functions allowing for maturation of both mycobacidin and
biotin. Moreover, only MybB was identified when the genome
of S. virginiae ISP-5094 was probed using the sequence of the
E. coli BioB. This suggests that no other biotin synthase besides
MybB exists in the genome of S. virginiae ISP-5094. Likewise, a
similar analysis using E. coli BioA and BioD as anchors for the
BLAST analysis only identified MybC and MybD as
homologous enzymes. These results suggest that the myb
cluster is also responsible for the biosynthesis of biotin in this
strain. Indeed, analysis of fermentation media from cultures of
S. virginiae ISP-5094 demonstrated the production of biotin
(see Figure S9). While the orf-1 gene product may be the BioF
equivalent in biotin biosynthesis, no production of 22 was
observed upon incubation of this protein with 21 generated in
situ using pimelic acid and BioW, which is the pimeloyl-CoA
synthetase found in the biotin biosynthetic pathway of Bacillus
subtilis strain 168.53
Mechanism of MybB. Having established the function of
MybB and its role in both mycobacidin and biotin biosyn-
thesis, efforts were directed at characterizing its catalytic
mechanism. Like other radical SAM enzymes, the catalytic
cycle of MybB is expected to begin with SAM coordinating the
catalytic (CAT) [Fe4S4]1+ cluster followed by reductive
cleavage of the C5′−S bond to generate methionine and a
highly reactive 5′-adenosyl radical (5′-dAdo•, 24) or its
equivalent.22,24,54 The 5′-dAdo• radical (24) could then
abstract a hydrogen atom from either C7 or C10 of 8 (Figure
6A, route a or b) to give the corresponding substrate radical
(25 or 26) followed by radical addition to a sulfide in the
auxiliary [Fe2S2] cluster to generate the first C−S bond in 28
or 29, respectively. The second half-reaction could proceed in
an analogous manner leading to the second C−S bond
formation between the remaining C10 or C7 and the initially
formed thiol group to complete the cyclization of the 4-
thiazolidinone ring (Figure 6A, 28 →30 →1 or 29 →31 →
1). This mechanism requires the reduction of two molecules of
SAM per completed reaction consistent with the four-electron
oxidation of 8 to 1 and the measured stoichiometry (27/1) of
2.2 ± 0.3 (see Supporting Information and Figure S11). In
order to determine whether C−S bond formation takes place
in a fixed sequence, the two hypothetical intermediates with
either a C7 (32) or C10 (33) thiol were synthesized (Figure
6B) and incubated with 100 μM reconstituted MybB, 1 mM
SAM, 2 mM dithiothreitol and 1 mM sodium dithionite
anaerobically at room temperature for 6 h. While no
mycobacidin was detected with 32, formation of 1 was
observed when 33 was supplied as the substrate (Figure 6C).
Thus, the 4-thiazolidinone cyclization catalyzed by MybB
proceeds via formation of the C10−S bond prior to the C7−S
bond.
■CONCLUSIONS
Mycobacidin is an antitubercular antibiotic whose enzymatic
target is the radical SAM sulfurtransferase BioB critical to the
biosynthesis of biotin in mycobacteria.6,7 Construction of the
characteristic thiazolidinone ring of mycobacidin proceeds via
two sequential sulfur insertion reactions catalyzed by the
radical SAM enzyme MybB. However, MybB is itself a
homologue of BioB and can also catalyze the oxidative
sulfuration of dethiobiotin (3) to yield biotin (4) as well.
Moreover, the remaining enzymes in the myb gene cluster are
not only responsible for the biosynthesis of mycobacidin in
Streptomyces but likely also the biosynthesis of biotin.
Consequently, Streptomyces possesses a dual function bio-
synthetic gene cluster that simultaneously catalyzes the
production of a primary metabolite (biotin) as well as a
secondary metabolite (mycobacidin) that inhibits the corre-
sponding pathway in other organisms. This suggests a
previously unrecognized evolutionary adaptation of biotin
