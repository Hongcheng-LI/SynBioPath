ABSTRACT: The bacillamides are a class of indole alkaloids
produced by the Bacillus genus that possess significant antialgal
activity. Incorporation of fluorine into the bacillamides was carried
out using a precursor-directed biosynthesis approach, with 4-, 5-,
and 6-fluorotryptophan added to growing cultures of Bacillus
atrophaeus IMG-11. This yielded the corresponding fluorinated
analogues of bacillamides A and C, in addition to new derivatives
of the related metabolite N-acetyltryptamine, thus demonstrating a
degree of plasticity in the bacillamide biosynthetic pathway. The
bacillamide derivatives were tested for activity against bloom-
forming algae, which revealed that fluorination could improve the
antialgal activity of these compounds in a site-specific manner, with
fluorination at the 6-position consistently resulting in improved activity.
N
ature continues to serve as a crucial reservoir to meet the
demands of the health, pharmaceutical, and chemical
industries, partly due to the production of a large repertoire of
valuable secondary metabolites by plants and microorganisms.1
Natural products and their derivatives account for the majority
of small-molecule drugs approved from 1981 to 2019,2 with
microorganisms playing a particularly important role. Among
microorganisms, the Bacillus genus is known to produce a wide
range of useful secondary metabolites, including antibiotics,
anticancer agents, plant growth promoters, pigments, and
antiphytoplankton compounds.3
Bacillus atrophaeus, formerly known as Bacillus globigii and
Bacillus subtilis var. niger, is one of the oldest known members
of this genus and derives its name “atrophaeus” from its
production of a blackish-brown pigment.4 In addition to this
pigment, this species is also known to produce antimicrobial
compounds such as bacteriocin and subtilosin A, the antialgal
bacillamides, the antio-omycete compound trioxsalen, as well
as a range of biosurfactant proteins.5,6 Inactivated spores of B.
atrophaeus have also been shown to be an effective vehicle and
adjuvant for rabies, anthrax, tetanus, and Clostridium
perfringens vaccines.6 B. atrophaeus endospores have also
been utilized in plant protection assays, and some of its strains
are used as biocontrol agents, bioindicators, and for the
evaluation of water and sewage treatment systems.6
Phytoplankton blooms caused by various algae and
cyanobacteria are responsible for tremendous harm to humans
and wildlife. They account for massive economic losses in the
fishing industry, can halt recreational activities in affected areas,
and can deteriorate the quality of drinking water.7−9 Some of
the cyanobacteria responsible for harmful blooms produce
cancer-promoting cyanotoxins that can also cause neuro- and
liver toxicity in humans.8−11 Numerous strategies have been
devised for the control of algal and cyanobacterial blooms.
These strategies include physical interventions (flocculants and
clays),12,13 chemical antialgal agents,14,15 and the use of
algicidal microorganisms (viruses and bacteria).16,17 In the
search for eco-friendly and species-specific agents to inhibit the
growth of these algae and cyanobacteria, the bacillamides were
discovered.18
Bacillamides are alkaloids containing a characteristic
thiazole-tryptamide moiety, the first of which was reported
from a Thermoactinomyces strain (TM-64).19 This was followed
by bacillamide A (1) in 2003, which displayed inhibitory
activity against the harmful dinoflagellate Cochlodinium
polykrikoides;18 bacillamides B and C (2) in 2007, isolated
from a Bahamian hypersaline microbial mat;20 and neo-
bacillamide A in 2013.21 The bacillamides have highly selective
activity against some eukaryotic microalgae and cyanobacteria
and were therefore thought to be potentially more environ-
mentally friendly than other antialgal natural products.18,22

their exact mechanism of action has not been studied in detail,
it was observed that treatment of cyanobacteria with
bacillamides results in the distortion of cell shape and the
reduction and collapse of gas vesicles.22
The biosynthesis of bacillamides has been described to take
place by a unique pathway involving a nonribosomal peptide
synthetase (NRPS) containing three modules, in addition to an
L-amino acid decarboxylase (AADC).21 The first NRPS
module activates alanine, while the second activates L-cysteine
and is also responsible for producing a thiazoline derivative,
which is then oxidized to AlaCysthiazole. The AADC
decarboxylates tryptophan into tryptamine, which is added to
the molecule by the third NRPS module.23−25
Precursor-directed biosynthesis is commonly used to
produce derivatives of useful natural products,26 in order to
produce derivatives with altered potency, solubility, and
metabolic properties.27 By taking advantage of the natural
promiscuity of enzymes, precursor-directed biosynthesis allows
the addition of multiple non-native substrates that can be taken
up by the relevant enzymatic domains.28,29 Both wild-type and
genetically engineered microorganisms can be used for the
incorporation of non-natural precursors to synthesize new
pharmacologically active compounds.30−32 However, there are
no reported studies on the use of precursor-directed
biosynthesis for the synthesis of bacillamide analogues;
previous studies concentrating on these compounds have
focused on derivative production via synthesis.22,33,34 In this
work, fluorinated bacillamide derivatives were obtained by
employing a precursor-directed biosynthesis approach in which
various fluoro-tryptophans were used as precursor molecules
for the synthesis of fluorinated derivatives from B. atrophaeus
IMG2−11 (Figure 1). These were then tested for their activity
against two of the most well-known red tide-forming algae,
Karenia brevis and Skeletonema grethae.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
A bacterial strain possessing significant antimicrobial activity
was isolated from the grasslands of Inner Mongolia, People’s
Republic of China. The strain was designated as IMG2−11 and
identified as B. atrophaeus using 16S rDNA analysis. Large-
scale culture and fractionation using sequential solvent−
solvent partition, solid-phase extraction, and reversed phase
HPLC led to the isolation of several natural products. A
combination of
1H NMR,
13C NMR, and HRMS and a
comparison of experimental data with the literature were used
to identify these compounds as four iturins (A2, A4, A6, and
A8, Supporting Information), in addition to a small suite of
tryptophan derivatives, including bacillamides A (1) and C (2)
(Supporting Information). Our attention was particularly
drawn to the bacillamides due to their potent algicidal
properties.22
Initial studies focused on the growth of the bacterium and
the production of compounds of interest. The bacterium was
inoculated in liquid media, and bacterial growth was
monitored, along with the levels of bacillamide A (1),
bacillamide C (2), and N-acetyltryptamine (3), to determine
the optimum time for the addition of fluoro-tryptophans for
maximal production of bacillamides. The bacterium reached
the stationary phase at 48 h, while bacillamide C (2)
production started at around 12 h and peaked at around 72
h. N-Acetyltryptamine production also started around 12 h,
but the levels remained approximately half of those of
bacillamide C (2). Bacillamide A (1) production started
later, at around 48 h, and remained low through the
experiment (Figure 2).

production studies, precursor-directed biosynthesis experi-
ments were conducted using fluorinated precursors. Fluori-

These results suggested that precursor-directed biosynthesis
is a viable method for producing fluorinated bacillamide
derivatives. Thus, large-scale precursor-directed biosynthesis
was carried out, with the bacterium grown in a modified ISP-4
medium and fluorinated tryptophans (4-, 5-, and 6-fluoro-
tryptophan) added to separate cultures (50 mg/L) after 12 h
of inoculation. After 7 days, growth was halted, and the
biomass and media were separated by centrifugation. The
media were extracted with EtOAc and dried under vacuum to
yield a crude organic extract; HPLC showed a similar mixture
of compounds to the original small-scale studies (Supporting
Information). A combination of solid-phase extraction and
semipreparative HPLC was used to isolate the target
compounds. The isolated metabolites included N-acetyltrypt-
amine (3) and its fluorinated analogues (4−6), bacillamide C
(2) and its fluorinated derivatives (8−10), and bacillamide A
(1) with its corresponding analogues (11−13) (Figure 4).
While insufficient material was available to carry out large-scale
studies with 7-fluoro-tryptophan, a small amount of 7-fluoro-
N-acetyltryptamine (7) was isolated from the initial small-scale
culture (not shown).
The purified metabolites were identified using HRMS, 1H,
and
19F NMR and by comparison with the available
literature.20 A molecular formula of C18H19FN4O2S was
confirmed for all the fluoro-bacillamide C derivatives (8−10)
by HRMS analysis. The 1H NMR for all of the bacillamide C
derivatives showed similar resonances to the parent compound,
except for those in the aromatic region. For example, in
compound 8, the 1H NMR spectrum lacked the H-4 proton
resonance, while significant shifts were observed in the
remaining aromatic signals: 6.71 (dd, J = 11.5, 7.9 Hz), 7.02
(td, J = 7.9, 5.1 Hz), and 7.17 (dd, J = 8.1 Hz). In addition to
the changes in chemical shift, distinctive
1H−19F coupling
constants confirming the presence of fluorine in the ring
system were observed in both proton and fluorine spectra,
consistent with those observed in similar ring systems (Table
1).40,41 Similar changes were observed in the spectra for the
other fluorinated derivatives. Compounds 8−10 showed single
fluorine resonances at 124.47, 125.68, and 122.47 ppm,
respectively. The chemical shifts of the 19F NMR resonances
matched closely with those of the fluoro-tryptophan
precursors, with Δδ 0.21, 1.51, and 0.07 ppm for the 4-fluoro,
5-fluoro, and 6-fluoro derivatives, respectively. The absolute
configuration of bacillamide C (2) was confirmed by
comparison of optical rotation with literature values; on
biosynthetic grounds, we propose that the fluorinated
derivatives share a common configuration.
For N-acetyltryptamine and bacillamide A derivatives (4−6
and 11−13), a similar pattern was observed. HRMS confirmed
the molecular formulas of all N-acetyltryptamine derivatives as
C12H13FN2O and those of the bacillamide A derivatives as
C16H14FN3O2S. 1H NMR for all these compounds confirmed
the presence of the same tryptamine units as in the parent
compounds, though differences were observed in the aromatic

analogues (Tables S1 and S2). 19F NMR for the fluorinated
bacillamides and N-acetyltryptamines confirmed the presence
of a single fluorine atom at the 4-, 5-, or 6-position. The
amounts of 7-fluoro-N-acetyltryptamine (7) and 5-fluoro-

The compounds were subjected to antiphytoplankton
testing using the red tide-forming dinoflagellate K. brevis and
the diatom S. grethae (formerly Skeletonema costatum). The
bioassay revealed not only that fluorination improves the
activity of these compounds but also that site-specific
fluorination has a differential effect on the bioactivity of
these compounds (Table 2). Fluorination at the 4-position had
no effect on the antialgal activity of the bacillamides, while
fluorination at the 5-position did not have a strong effect on
activity: 5F-bacillamide A (12) was only weakly active. In
contrast, fluorination at the 6-position consistently resulted in
improved potency, with 6F-N-acetyltryptamine (6) possessing
good activity against S. grethae and 6F-bacillamide-C in
particular (10) being active against K. brevis. These results
were in agreement with a previous study conducted by Churro
et al., where halogenation not only improved the activity of
bacillamides but also increased their spectrum of activity.22
Both K. brevis and S. grethae are infamous for causing
harmful algal blooms. K. brevis is known for causing the Florida
red tides that are responsible for harmful effects not only to
marine organisms (owing to the production of brevetoxin) but
also to public health. These disastrous effects coupled with
economic losses make this organism one of the most harmful
marine algae.42−44 Similarly, S. grethae is one of the most
dominant species responsible for causing red tides across the
world, including costal and fresh waters of China as well as the
South China Sea.45−47
These results show that site-specific fluorination can make a
drastic difference to a compound’s bioactivity, and it can be
achieved using simple but effective methods like precursor-
directed biosynthesis. Overall, our studies suggest that
precursor-directed biosynthesis remains a convenient strategy
for obtaining unique derivatives of natural products in
sufficient yields for preliminary testing. Competition with
natural substrates remains a problem, and for follow-up studies
on the bacillamides it might be preferable to use either
mutasynthetic or synthetic approaches.
■EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
General Experimental Procedures. UV−vis spectra were
recorded on a Hitachi UH5300 spectrophotometer (Hitachi,
Japan). NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker Advance III 400
spectrometer and a Bruker Advance III 600 spectrometer (Bruker,
USA) with tetramethylsilane as an internal reference. HR-ESI MS
spectra were obtained from a Bruker microTOF-Q II mass
spectrometer and a Thermo Fisher Q Exactive HF mass spectrometer.
Methanol-d4 and dimethyl sulfoxide-d6 (Cambridge Isotope Lab.,
USA) were used in NMR experiments. HPLC analysis was performed
on an Agilent 1260 series HPLC system equipped with a G1311B
Quaternary pump, a G1315D photodiode array detector, a G1316C
thermostat equipped column compartment, and a G1329B
autosampler (Agilent Technologies, USA) using a reversed-phase
Agilent Pursuit XRS-C18 column (3.5 μm, 4.6 mm × 150 mm; Agilent
Technologies, USA). Semipreparative HPLC separation was carried
out on a Shimadzu LC-20AR series HPLC instrument (Shimazdu,
Japan) equipped with a UV detector and a reversed-phase C18 column
(Agilent Pursuit XRS -C18, 5 μm, 9.4 × 250 mm). Column
chromatography was performed using 45−75 μm silica gel
(Qingdaodingkang, Qingdao, China) and Diaion HP20 macroporous
resin (Mitsubishi, Japan). Thin-layer chromatography (TLC) was
performed on precoated sheets of silica gel 60 F254 (EMD Millipore
Corp., Germany). Commercially available reagents and solvents were
used in this study (Concord Tianjin, China). Fluorinated reagents
were purchased from BLD Pharmatech Ltd. Shanghai., China.
Bacterial Strain. The bacterial strain IMG2−11 was isolated from a
soil sample collected from Inner Mongolian Grasslands (Inner
Mongolia Autonomous Region, Peoples Republic of China) in May
2018 at GPS coordinates GPS: N 41°22′50″, E 111°11′18″. The
strain was identified as B. atrophaeus by 16S rDNA sequence analysis,
and the sequence has been deposited in GeneBank with accession
number OR855979. DNA isolation, amplification, and sequence
analysis were carried out by using an adapted version of protocols
described elsewhere.48
Bacterial Growth and Production of Metabolites of Interest.
To monitor bacterial growth and evaluate the production levels of
metabolites of interest, the bacterial solution (OD = 1) was inoculated
into 100 mL of modified ISP-4 medium (see Supporting Information)
and grown at 30 °C. Starting from 12 h postinoculation, a 5 mL
aliquot was taken from the flask after every 24 h, and the OD was
measured at 585 nm. Samples were extracted with equal volumes of
ethyl acetate, and then the samples were analyzed by HPLC. Peak
areas were measured for bacillamide A (1), bacillamide C (2), and N-
acetyltryptamine (3) for quantification.
Precursor-Directed Biosynthesis (Small-Scale). For small-
scale cultures, a bacterial suspension (OD = 1) was inoculated into
two flasks containing 100 mL of modified ISP-4 medium each (casein
(10 g), wheat flour (10 g), K2HPO4 (1.0 g), MgSO4·5H2O (1.0 g),
NaCl (1.0 g), (NH4)2SO4 (2.0 g), CaCO3 (2.0 g), FeSO4·5H2O
(0.001 g), MnCl2·7H2O (0.001 g), and ZnSO4 (0.001 g), pH = 7.2).
The fermentation was carried out at 30 °C with a shaking speed of
200 rpm. Filter-sterilized fluorinated tryptophans (5 mg/100 mL)
were added at 0 h in flask 1 and after 12 h in flask 2. After 7 days, the
media was extracted with equal volumes of ethyl acetate and dried,
and HPLC analysis was performed.
Precursor-Directed Biosynthesis (Large Scale). The media
composition used for precursor-directed biosynthesis was the same as
that for the small-scale cultures (see Supporting Information).
Fermentation was carried out at 30 °C with a shaking speed of 200
rpm in 250 mL (10 flasks for each fluoro-tryptophan) conical flasks
each containing 100 mL of modified ISP-4. Filter-sterilized 4-, 5-, and
6-fluoro-tryptophan (5 mg/100 mL) were added after 12 h of
incubation. After 7 days of cultivation, all culture flasks for each of the
fluorinated precursors were combined and extracted with equal
volumes (1000 mL) of ethyl acetate (EtOAc) three times. The solvent

Phytoplankton. Strain CCMP 2228 of K. brevis and strain
CCMP775 of S. grethae were obtained from the National Center for
Marine Algae and Microbiota, Maine, USA, and cultured in L1 media
formulated with natural seawater from the Gulf of Maine. Cultures
were maintained in an environmental chamber under a 12:12 light−
dark cycle, at 24 °C with irradiance of 30−45 μmol m−2 s−1. Growth
was approximated using in vivo chlorophyll a (chl a) fluorescence (ex,
430 nm; em, 665 nm) using FlexStation 3 by Molecular Devices LLC,
San Jose, USA.
Antiphytoplankton Bioassay. To determine the effect of
fluorination position on the bioactivity of test compounds against
dinoflagellates (K. brevis) and diatoms (S. grethae), 200 μL of
midexponential phase phytoplankton (∼1 × 105 cells/mL) was added
to each well of a 96-well plate. Phytoplankton were subjected to no
addition (negative control), addition of 10 μL of water/DMSO carrier
solvent, or the addition of 10 μL of test compound to generate final
well concentrations of 200 μM, 20 μM, 2 μM, 200 nM, 20 nM, 2 nM,
200 pM, 20 pM, or 2 pM. All tests were conducted in triplicate.
Tralopyril (Janssen Pharmaceuticals, Belgium) and cycloheximide
(MP Biomedicals, USA) were used as positive controls. The chl a
fluorescence was measured immediately after exposure, at 24, 48, and
72 h. IC50 values for each test compound against K. brevis were
calculated from chl a measurements at 72 h via an asymmetrical,
sigmoidal, 5 pt IC50 curve regression using Prism Graphpad version
10.0. Due to the nature of S. grethae growth (aggregations of various
cell numbers), the change in chl a fluorescence over 72 h for each well
was used for IC50 calculations.
