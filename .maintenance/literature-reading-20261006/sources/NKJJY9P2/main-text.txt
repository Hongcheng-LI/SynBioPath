Fungal RiPPs Side Chain Macrocyclization Catalyzed by Copper-
Dependent DUF3328 Enzyme

ABSTRACT: Fungal enzymes containing domain of unknown function (DUF) 3328 have been implicated in the oxidative
maturation of ribosomally synthesized and post-translationally modified peptides (RiPPs). We report here the functional
characterization of one such enzyme, AprY, involved in the biosynthesis of RiPP asperipin-2a. Biochemical reconstitution of AprY
showed the enzyme catalyzes two consecutive C−O cross-linking reactions between tyrosines and beta-carbons of residues in the
core hexapeptide. The side-chain macrocyclization activities of AprY are copper- and oxygen-dependent and do not require a leader
peptide sequence.
R
ibosomally synthesized and post-translationally modified
peptides (RiPPs) are a large family of natural products
with diverse structures and potent biological activities.1−3
Macrocyclization of the linear RiPPs precursor is a post-
translational modification (PTM) that enhances metabolic
stability and target binding affinity.4 In addition to head-to-tail
macrolactamization, side-chain cyclization through C−C, C−
O, C−N, or C−S bond is a prominent feature of RiPPs.5 The
enzymology of radical-mediated cyclization in RiPPs biosyn-
thesis has been studied intensively, including aryl couplings
catalyzed by cytochrome P450s;6−8 macrocyclization at an
unactivated C(sp3)−H bond by radical S-adenosylmethionine
enzymes (rSAMs);9−13 and copper-dependent burpitide
cyclases from plant RiPPs pathways.14−17 The discovery and
characterization of these PTM enzymes have greatly
accelerated the mining of new RiPPs from bacteria and plants.
In sharp contrast, our understanding of oxidative macro-
cyclization enzymes in fungal RiPPs has lagged behind
significantly.18 As a result, only five RiPPs belonging to the
dikaritin class (containing a macrocyclic ether bond) have
been identified, including ustiloxins,19 phomopsins,20 epichloë-
cyclins,21 asperipin-2a (2),22 and victorins23 (Figure 1A). The
genes encoding precursor peptides of dikaritin RiPPs parallel
those of Kex2-processed repeat proteins (KEPs) that are
widely present in fungi:24 it contains multiple repeats of core
peptides flanked by Kex2 protease cleavage sites (KR or RR)
(Figure 1B). A differentiating feature of dikaritin RiPPs
biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) from other non-RiPP
KEPs is the presence of one or more genes that encode
proteins with domain of unknown function (DUF) 3328.22,25
The colocalization of DUF3328 enzymes with KEP-like
precursor peptides has enabled prediction of thousands of
RiPPs BGCs in sequenced fungal genomes, with ∼25% of all
KEPs being predicted to be dikaritin RiPPs.24 Previous
heterologous expression and genetic knockout studies
suggested that DUF3328 enzymes, which have low sequence
identities between each other, catalyze a variety of oxidative
PTMs, including side-chain macrocyclization,25,26 chlorina-
tion,23,27 desaturation,27 and others. Despite the implicated
central roles, no successful in vitro reconstitution of a
DUF3328 enzyme from a RiPPs BGC has been achieved to
date. This remains the main obstacle in studying and mining
fungal RiPPs biosynthesis.
In this study, we chose DUF3328-containing AprY from
asperipin-2a (2) BGC as a model enzyme. The apr BGC from
Aspergillus flavus encodes a precursor peptide AprA, AprY, a
short-chain dehydrogenase (SDR) AprR, and a MFS trans-
porter AprT (Figure 1C). Previous work showed when the
entire apr BGC was heterologously expressed, 2-derived from
the hexapeptide Phe1-Tyr2-Tyr3-Thr4-Gly5-Tyr6 (FYYTGY)
was produced.26 2 contain two Cβ−O cross-links between
Phe1-Tyr3 and Tyr3-Tyr6, as well as the α-hydroxy group in
Phe1 instead of a N-terminal amine. While AprY is proposed to
catalyze the macrocyclization reactions, understanding the
timing of AprY during PTM of AprA and the order of the two
macrocyclization reactions requires biochemical analysis with
functional AprY.
We recently developed a refolding method to reconstitute
the function of a non-RiPPs DUF3328 protein ApnU, and we
showed it is a copper-dependent halogenase.28 The E. coli
expression-refolding procedure was adopted for obtaining
active AprY (see the Supporting Information). Briefly,
truncated AprY (NΔ77-AprY-CHis6, named AprY in the
following) was purified under denaturing condition and
refolded using serial dialysis processes in the presence of
arginine and glutathione redox pair (GSH and GSSG) (Figure
S1). The N-His6 tagged AprR was expressed and purified from
E. coli BL21(DE3) (Figure S1). To identify the substrate of

hexapeptide FYYTGY (1), the hexapeptide flanked by KR
sequences (KRFYYTGYKR), the full repeat which includes
both FYYTGY and a N-terminal nonapeptide (NAET-
PEDLD), as well as only the nonapeptide. 1 was incubated
with AprY, AprR, NADPH, ascorbate, and a first-row divalent
transition metal in MOPS buffer (pH 7.0), followed by LC−
QTOF analysis. Gratifyingly, in the presence of Cu2+, 1 was
completely converted into two products with molecular
weights (MW) of 809 and 808 (Figures 2A and 2B). MS/
MS analysis suggested that both products are side chain
cyclized peptides (Figures S2 and S3). Testing the other three
peptide substrates with the same assay conditions did not lead
to any cyclized product (Figure S4).
The in vitro reaction was scaled up with AprY and AprR and
both products were isolated for NMR characterization (Figure
2C). The MW 809 product was verified to be 2 (Table
S3),26,29 while the MW 808 product 3 was found to be the α-
amino analogue of 2 (Table S4). The macrocyclization activity
of AprY requires molecular oxygen and ascorbate as the
oxidant and reductant, respectively (Figure 2D). Therefore,
AprY can catalyze two oxidative macrocyclization reactions
directly on the hexapeptide FYYTGY without requiring a
leader peptide. During bacterial RiPPs maturation, leader
peptides fused to the core peptides are often essential for
recognition by PTM enzymes. One prominent example is the
binding of leader peptides to RiPP recognition element (RRE)
present in PTM enzymes.30,31
We then conducted reactions using each enzyme separately
(Figure 2C). While there is no modification of 1 with only
AprR present, new products emerged in the reaction with only
AprY. Time-course analysis showed that 1 was first converted
to 4 (MW 810), followed by conversion to 5 (MW 825)
(Figure 2E). MS/MS analysis (Figure S5) and NMR
characterization (Table S5) revealed 4 contains a single
Tyr3-Tyr6 cross-link compared to 1. 4 is therefore the product
of the first macrocyclization step catalyzed by AprY.
MS/MS analysis of 5 compared to 2 revealed a similar
fragmentation pattern except with a +16 Da modification at
Phe1 (Figure S6). NMR characterization of this compound
was unsuccessful due to difficulties in achieving sufficient
purity. To test if 5 is a precursor of 2, 1 was first incubated
with AprY for an hour to accumulate 5, followed by the
addition of AprR and NADPH (Figures 2C and S7). This
resulted in the conversion of 5 into 2, which can also be
observed when NaBH4 was added to effect the chemical
reduction. These results indicate the α-hydroxyl in 2 is most
likely reduced from an α-ketone as in 7. We propose 5 is the
hydrated form of 7, and the two can equilibrate in aqueous
buffer due to the electron-withdrawing effect of the amide
carbonyl (Figure 3), consistent with known chemical character
of α-keto amides.32,33 These results therefore pinpoint the role
of AprY in catalyzing two macrocyclization reactions during
which the α-amino group in Phe1 is oxidized into an α-ketone
in 7, while AprR can stereoselectively reduce the α-ketone to
an α-hydroxyl group to give 2.
To probe the origin of the α-oxygen atom in 2, assays with
AprY and AprR were performed in H2
18O, and the products
were analyzed by LC−QTOF (Figure S8). The observed +2
Da increase in the MW of 2 indicates the incorporation of one
18O atom, while no MW increase in 3 and 4 was detected.
These results are consistent with the proposal of α-ketone 7 as
an intermediate to 2, which must derive from the hydrolysis of
an imine intermediate (6) (Figure 3). We propose that the α-
amino product 3 is a result of the imine reduction of 6 by AprR
during the one-pot enzymatic assay. Such activity of SDR to
reduce both ketone and imine substrates has been reported.34
Compound 3 was not observed in the stepwise assay (see
Figure 2C (spectra vi and vii), and Figure S7), this is because 6
is completely hydrolyzed to 7 (and 5) prior to addition of
AprR and NADPH.
We performed AprY assays in D2O to probe the mechanism
of oxidative cyclization. One mechanism to form the Cβ−O−
Tyr bond is through a Michael-type addition by the attack of
phenolic alcohol to Cβ of α,β-unsaturated intermediate
followed by Cα protonation. This mechanism is established
in the biosynthesis of lanthipeptides, during which side chain
thiol of Cys attacks Cβ of dehydroalanine or dehydrobutyrine
residues.35 Alternatively, cyclization may proceed through a
radical recombination mechanism as proposed for rSAMs
involved in Cα−S bond formation during sactipeptide
biosynthesis.36,37 From the D2O experiment, no mass change
was observed in either 3 or 4, while +1 Da was observed in 2
and 5 (Figure S8). The absence of proton exchange in 4
indicates the cyclization between Tyr3-Tyr6 most likely
proceeds via radical recombination (see Figures 3 and S9).

between Phe1 and Tyr3 goes through a radical intermediate

from 4 in a second cyclization step. We propose that a

reaction that is in competition with imine hydrolysis to form
ketone 7. Lastly, 7 is reduced by AprR to give 2 as the final
product.
Conserved among the DUF3328 protein family, AprY
contains two HXXHC motifs that can serve as the putative
copper binding center via the predicted square planar
geometry of the four imidazole side chains (Figures S11 and
S12). Copper binding was quantified by inductively coupled
plasma mass spectroscopy (ICP−MS) to be 2.2 ± 0.6 per
active site (Figures S13 and S14). Mutagenesis of each
histidine in HXXHC led to the complete loss of activity,
indicating the importance of the complete coordination
environment for activity (Figure S15). No ligand-to-metal
charge transfer (LMCT) band from UV−vis spectroscopy
(Figure S16) was observed, indicative of AprY having a type II
binuclear copper center, as characterized for ApnU.28 In
addition to two cysteines from the two HXXHC motifs, AprY
contains two cysteine residues (C237 and C265) that are
highly conserved among the DUF3328 enzymes (Figure S11).
From disulfide mapping of ApnU, these two cysteines are
involved in intermolecular disulfide bond formation to form a
covalent dimer.28 Nonreducing SDS−PAGE analysis of
refolded AprY revealed a distribution of oligomeric states
with the dominant dimer as the only active species (Figure
S17). The active dimeric AprY has a protein mass consistent
with the formation of four disulfide bonds, two intermolecular
and two intramolecular, within each dimer (8.64 Da loss;
Figure S18). The addition of reductants such as DTT
abolished AprY activity, likely due to the disruption of the
intermolecular disulfide cross-links (Figure S19). Mutation of
each cysteine residue disrupted dimer formation and abolished
activity (Figure S15). Overall, AprY displays many similar
biochemical features to those characterized for ApnU, which
may be shared among the entire DUF3328 family.
In summary, we characterized AprY to be the prototypical
DUF3328 enzyme catalyzing copper-dependent side chain
macrocyclization during the maturation of fungal dikaritin
RiPPs. Our methodology to obtain functional AprY should be
generalizable to other DUF3328 proteins found prevalently
among fungal BGCs. Ours findings can be useful in the
exploration of uncharacterized fungal RiPPs pathways, as well
as in the generation of cyclic peptides.
