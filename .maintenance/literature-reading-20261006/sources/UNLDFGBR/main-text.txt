ABSTRACT: In recent years, genome mining in cyanobacteria has
revealed abundant gene clusters related to natural product biosynthesis.
However, only a few terpene synthases (TSs) have been identified from
this bacterial phylum. Pfam profiles, such as PF03936 and PF19086,
which are frequently used for TS retrieval, are built from plant,
bacterial, and fungal TSs. Herein, we constructed a new hidden Markov
model (HMM) specific to bacterial TSs on the basis of 110 bacterial
TSs experimentally validated in recent years. Using this model, we
identified a pair of diterpene synthases, Cpt11 (class II TS) and Cts11
(class I TS), in the cyanobacterium Scytonema tolypothrichoides. In vitro
experiments demonstrated that Cpt11 catalyzes the formation of syn-
copalyl diphosphate from geranylgeranyl diphosphate and that Cts11
subsequently converts syn-copalyl diphosphate into a rare 6,6,7-tricyclic
diterpene alcohol. Its biosynthesis was established through isotope
labeling experiments, which revealed a unique sequence of a 1,6-proton shift and ring expansion to a seven-membered ring. We
solved the crystal structure of Cts11 at a resolution of 1.76 Å. Additionally, via site-directed mutagenesis experiments, we identified
two amino acid residues whose exchanges affected the formation of the original diterpene alcohol, leading to the formation of two
new compounds: a 6,6,7-tricyclic diterpene hydrocarbon and another 6,6,6-tricyclic diterpene alcohol. A BLAST search revealed
several sequences that shared over 70% identity with Cts11 from cyanobacteria that could produce diverse diterpenes. This study
demonstrates the potential for cyanobacteria to produce unprecedented terpenoids and lays the foundation for studying the
physiological activities of terpenoids in cyanobacteria.
■INTRODUCTION
Terpenes are the largest group of natural products, constituting
over 80,000 compounds that are isolated mainly from plants
and fungi.1 The structural diversity of terpenoid skeletons is
due mainly to the diversity of terpene synthases (TSs) involved
in their formation. TSs are distinguished by two classes, classes
I and II, depending on the chemical strategy used for initial
carbocation formation. Class I TSs generate an allyl
carbocation by dissociating diphosphate from the correspond-
ing isoprenoid diphosphate. On the other hand, class II TSs
generate a carbocation via protonation of a terminal olefin or
epoxide.2
Cyanobacteria are well-known producers of natural prod-
ucts, many of which display bioactivities with potential
pharmaceutical applications, such as the peptides patellamides
and the macrolides cryptophycins.3 Cyanobacteria have also
been found to produce terpenoids (Figure 1). Some marine
cyanobacteria can produce isoprene,4 and the volatile
methylated monoterpene 2-methylisoborneol and the trisnor
sesquiterpene geosmin have also been found in cyanobac-
teria.5,6 In addition, several bioactive diterpenoids have been
identified in cyanobacteria. For example, the potent anti-
inflammatory meroterpenoid, tolypodiol, was first discovered
in 1996 from the terrestrial cyanobacterium Tolypothrix
nodosa.7 A novel extracellular meroterpenoid metabolite,
noscomin, with an uncommon diterpenoid skeleton, was
isolated from the strain Nostoc commune and exhibits
antibacterial activity.8 Two abietane diterpenoids isolated
from Microcoleous lacustris also showed some antibacterial
properties.9 Recently, the cembrene-type diterpenoids kagim-
minols A and B were isolated from the marine cyanobacterium
Okeania sp. that showed selective growth-inhibitory activity
against the causative agent of human African trypanosomia-
sis.10 However, little is known about the TSs responsible for
the biosynthesis of these terpenoids. Indeed, only a few TSs

In the context of these facts, we constructed a bacterial TS
hidden Markov model (HMM) profile based on experimen-
tally validated TS sequences to mine as-yet unidentified
terpene synthase candidates. Using this new model, we
identified a pair of diterpene synthases (DTSs), Cpt11 and
Cts11, in the genome of the cyanobacterium Scytonema
tolypothrichoides VB-61278. In vitro experiments demonstrated
that Cpt11 catalyzes the cyclization of geranylgeranyl
diphosphate (GGPP, 1) to form syn-copalyl diphosphate
(syn-CPP, 2) and that Cts11 subsequently catalyzes the
cyclization of 2 to form the new diterpene alcohol
tolypothrichol (3) with a rare 6−6−7 tricyclic skeleton.
Isotope labeling experiments in conjunction with DFT
calculations established the reaction mechanism of the
cyclization catalyzed by Cts11. In addition, we solved the
crystal structure of the class I TS Cts11 with a high resolution
of 1.76 Å, thereby identifying key residues in the active site by
modeling and structure-based site-directed mutagenesis experi-
ments.
A BLAST search of Cts11 in the NCBI database revealed a
series of cyanobacterial homologues with significant identity.
Subsequent in vitro experiments demonstrated that these
homologues utilize syn-CPP or ent-CPP to produce diterpene
products other than compound 3. The present study highlights
the potential of cyanobacteria in the synthesis of structurally
diverse terpenoids.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Genome Mining Reveals Two Diterpenoid Biosyn-
thesis-Related Gene Clusters. Owing to the low sequence
similarity among bacterial class I TSs, retrieving new TSs via
sequence alignment is challenging. However, highly conserved
metal-binding motifs (DDXXD and NSE/DTE) have been
found in essentially all known bacterial TSs. As an alternative
to sequence alignment methods, a search method based on an
HMM, such as the Pfam searcher, has been used as a
preliminary approach for retrieving TSs.15 The frequently used
Pfam profiles associated with TSs include PF03936 (terpene
synthase family, metal binding domain), PF19086 (terpene
synthase family 2, C-terminal metal binding) and PF01397
(terpene synthase, N-terminal domain).16,17 Among them,
PF03936 and PF01397 were built from plant TSs, whereas
PF19086 was built from bacterial and fungal TSs (http://pfam.
xfam.org/). Considering that a model constructed only from
bacterial TS sequences as the training set might be more
effective for retrieving bacterial TSs, we decided to construct a
new Pfam profile for bacteria.
First, we collected 110 experimentally validated bacterial
TSs for building the Pfam model via HMMER 3.3.2 (Figure
S1A). Compared to the Pfam model PF03936 based on plant-
derived TSs and PF19086 based on bacterial and fungal TSs,
the conserved metal-binding domain, including the aspartate-
rich motif and NSE motif, was more clearly captured in the
new model. In addition to these two motifs, the RY motif, a
highly conserved motif that is important for the binding of the
substrate and the Mg2+ cofactor, was also identified (Figure
S1B). Then, to identify the TS candidates from the
cyanobacterial genome resource, 3,213,600 cyanobacterial
protein sequences were downloaded from the NCBI database
as the local database. At the same time, PF03936 and PF19086
were also used to search the same cyanobacterial protein
database. The results showed that the E value for the new
model was 10−266, while the E values for PF19086 and
PF03936 were 10−108 and 10−9, respectively (Figure S1C).
Upon searching with the new model, 166 sequences were
retrieved, 143 of which had E values smaller than the inclusion
threshold (10−3). In comparison, PF19086 and PF03936
retrieved 180 and 173 sequences, respectively, with 141 and 56
sequences having E values smaller than the inclusion threshold
(10−3). Thus, the new model did not show a significant
difference in the number of retrieved sequences compared to
the other two models. Additionally, the new model yielded a
lower E value than PF19086 and PF03936, but this was
unsurprising given that the bacterial sequences collected to
construct our search model were used as a training set to
detect bacterial sequences from genomic data.
After removing duplicate sequences and those lacking the
conserved metal-binding motif, a total of 44 sequences were
ultimately selected as candidate sequences. The resulting 44
candidate sequences were subjected to a phylogenetic analysis
alongside experimentally validated TS sequences, leading to
the exclusion of sequences showing greater than 30% similarity
to known sequences according to multiple alignments (Figure
S2). Finally, four sequences, cts10 (accession number
MBP0009865.1),

Functional Characterization of the DTSs Cpt11 and
Cts11. To confirm this hypothesis, we performed heterologous
expression of codon-optimized cpt11 and cts11 genes in E. coli
BL21 (DE3) and conducted in vitro reactions using GGPP as a
substrate. To enable tracing of the Cpt11 product, the sample
was treated with calf intestinal phosphatase (CIP) to generate
the corresponding alcohol. When GGPP was incubated with
Cpt11 followed by CIP treatment, the alcohol 2’ was detected
besides geranylgeraniol (GGOH, 1’) formed from remaining
starting material, and when GGPP was incubated with both
Cpt11 and Cts11, compound 3 was detected (Figure 2B). No
conversion was observed upon incubation of GGPP with
Cts11 and CIP dephosphorylation. Both compounds 2’ and 3
were isolated and structurally characterized via NMR spec-
troscopy (Figures 2C, and S26−S34, Tables S6 and S7). A
comparison of the NMR data to literature data18 identified 2’
as (+)-syn-copalol ([α]D
25 = +17°, c 0.03, CH2Cl2; lit. [α]D
24 =
+17.7°, c 2.39, CHCl3).18 Thus, cpt11 encodes a syn-CPP
synthase (syn-CPPS). Extensive 1D and 2D NMR analyses
were used to determine the structure of 3, for which we suggest
the name tolypothrichol, as a novel diterpene alcohol with a
6,6,7-tricyclic ring system (Figures 2C, S10, and S28−S34,
Tables S6 and S7).
Given the structures of 2 and 3, we proposed a cyclization
pathway from 1 to 3 (Scheme 1). The cyclization reaction
from 1 to 2 catalyzed by Cpt11 follows the generally accepted
mechanism, starting with a protonation of the C14 = C15
double bond, followed by two cyclization steps and
deprotonation to 2. The second cyclization mediated by
Cts11 may start with the ionization of syn-CPP (2) to A.
Carbocation A is cyclized by the attack of C19 on the Re-face
of C3 to yield carbocation B.19 Subsequently, a rare 1,6-proton
shift from C8 to C1 leads to the generation of carbocation C
that undergoes a ring expansion to D. A final quenching with
water through attack at C3 yields 3.
Isotopic Labeling Experiments for the Cyclization
Reaction. To gain insight into the cyclization mechanism, we
performed a series of isotopic labeling experiments (Table S4).
The 1,6-proton shift from B to C was confirmed with the
conversion of (1-13C,8-2H)GGPP obtained from GPP,
(1-13C)IPP,20 and (E)- or (Z)-(4-13C,4-2H)IPP21 in con-
junction with GGPP synthase (GGPPS) from Streptomyces
cyaneofuscatus20 (Figure S18). 13C NMR analysis revealed a
triplet for C1 (13C−2H spin coupling) in the experiment with
(Z)-(4-13C,4-2H)IPP, but not with (E)-(4-13C,4-2H)IPP,
revealing specific migration of the proton derived from 4-HZ
of IPP (Δδ = −0.5 ppm,1JC,D = 19.2 Hz, Scheme 1, Figure
3A). Such a long-range 1,6-proton shift is rare in the
biosynthesis of terpenoids, but has been experimentally
validated before in the biosynthesis of cleistantha-8,12-diene
that is formed from CPP by HvKSL4 from Hordeum vulgare.22
The sequential conversion of (2-13C)GGPP20
and
(19-13C)GGPP (enzymatically prepared with GGPPS from
(15-13C)FPP23 and IPP) into 3 by Cpt11 and Cts11 further
confirmed the origin of C2 and C19 in compound 3 (Figure
3B). Labeling experiments using (16-13C)GGPP or
(17-13C)GGPP (likewise prepared from (12-13C)FPP23 and
(9-13C)GPP,24 respectively) were used to determine the
relative positions of C16 and C17 of 3 (Figure S19). The
incubation of (14-13C)GGPP (obtained from (10-13C)FPP23)
in a deuterium oxide buffer revealed that protonation of GGPP
at the Si face of C14 initiates the cyclization by Cpt11 (Figure
S20). DFT calculations for the Cts11-catalyzed reaction
cascade revealed an overall exergonic process via low activation
barriers. The highest reaction barrier was found for the 1,6-
proton shift starting from B (+12.1 kcal/mol), and the
calculations demonstrated that the secondary cation C is
indeed not an intermediate, but instead D is directly formed
from B (Figures S24 and S25).
To date, class II DTSs have been reported in both plants and
bacteria. For example, ent-CPP synthase (ent-CPPS) is
involved in synthesizing the intermediate toward gibberellins,
an important class of plant hormones, whereas syn-CPP
synthase (syn-CPPS) is involved in the biosynthesis of the
intermediate toward the phytoalexin momilactone.25 In
bacteria, only a few class II DTSs have been functionally
characterized, and their products represent three types of
skeletons: the labdane, the clerodane, and the halimane
skeleton.26 Enzymes that form the labdane skeleton are the
most common and include ent-CPPSs, such as Orf2 and
PtmT2, and (+)-CPP synthases, such as RmnB and CldB.27−29
Clerodane synthases include Cyc1 and Haur_2145, which
produce terpentedienyl diphosphate and (+)-kolavenyl diphos-
phate, respectively.30,31 Rv3377c from Mycobacterium tuber-
culosis is the only enzyme that forms the halimane skeleton,
producing tuberculosinyl diphosphate.32 Thus, Cpt11 is the
first syn-CPPS identified in bacteria, and Cpt11 and Cts11 are
the first pair of cyanobacterial DTSs with catalytic activity
detected. To evaluate the pair of DTSs, the amino acid
sequences of Cpt11-Cts11 and other class I and class II TSs
were aligned and subjected to phylogenetic analysis (Figure
S5; see the figure legend for more information).

Crystal Structure and Catalytic Mechanism of Cts11.
To gain further insights into the mechanism of the unique
reaction cascade catalyzed by Cts11, the enzyme was
crystallized for an X-ray structure analysis (Figure S6),
providing the crystal structure of the apo form with a
resolution of 1.76 Å (PDB ID: 9KGB). The overall structure
revealed a monomeric state in the crystal that reflects the
observation of monomers also in solution (Figure S6). Cts11
exhibits the typical α-helical fold of class I TSs, comprising 11
core α-helices (A to K) and five short α-helices (α1 to α5)
(Figure 4A). The highly conserved DDxxD and NSE motifs of
class I TSs are located on helices C and H, respectively.
To examine the catalytic mechanism of Cts11, the amino
acid residues around the active site were further analyzed.
Given the high structural similarity with the previously
reported class I TS Sat1646, the position of magnesium ions
inside the active site was inferred from their position in the
crystal structure of Sat1646 (Figure S7).33 Three magnesium
ions are present in the active site of Cts11, including two Mg2+
binding to D93 and D94 from the 93DDITE97 motif, as well as
Mg2+ binding to N264, D265, S268, R271, and E272 from the
264NDIRSFERE272 motif (Figure S8). By comparing the
residues around the active sites of Sat1646, we inferred that
the active site pocket of Cts11 is composed of F90, Y339,
V220, M63, M67, T70, I260, and M331. The substrate syn-
CPP was then docked into the active site of Cts11 by docking
simulation (Figure 4B), and the position of the substrate
matched well with the predicted active site. The diphosphate
moiety of syn-CPP is coordinated by the three Mg2+ ions
(Figure S9). Furthermore, previous studies have established
that bacterial class I TSs can utilize an induced-fit mechanism
for substrate binding and ionization, involving the ″effector
triad″ which includes the pyrophosphate sensor, the linker, and
the effector residues located at the G1/2 helix-break motif.34
Consistently, we identified the pyrophosphate sensor R261, the
linker S214, and the effector residue I215 in Cts11 (Figure S9).
However, a distinction is that its sensor residue R261 is located

According to the results of a GC−MS analysis, the F90A
variant lost the ability to form 3, but produced compound 4
instead (Figure 5A). The M63A variant showed a reduced
production of 3 with a shift to the main product 5. Compound
isolation and NMR-based structure elucidation revealed the
structure of syn-pimara-1,7(8)-diene33 for 4 (Figures S10, S35
and S36, Tables S6 and S7), while 5 was identified as syn-
isopimara-1,7(8)-diene33 with a different configuration at C3
(Figures S10, S37 and S38, Tables S6 and S7). The formation
of compound 5 is understandable by deprotonation of
intermediate B, whereas 4 requires a stereochemical change
in the cyclization of A with Si face attack at C3, resulting in the
direct precursor E (Scheme 1). This configurational inversion
suggests that F90 is essential for maintaining the spatial
arrangement of the methyl and vinyl groups at C3 and fixes the
attack of C19 on the Re face of C3 to yield B, promoting the
1,6-proton shift from C8 to C1 during the biosynthesis of 3.
The M63A exchange enlarges the active site pocket and allows
the vinyl chain with the C1 = C2 double bond to be more
flexible. This flexibility results in C1 being able to either accept
or not accept the proton from C8. This may explain why the
M63A variant simultaneously produces both compounds 3 and
5. The Y339A variant did not catalyze the formation of any
diterpene, indicating that an aromatic residue may be crucial
for initial carbocation stabilization. M67A and M331A had
little effect on the reaction, with the production of 3 being
similar to that by the native enzyme, suggesting that M67 and
M331 have little involvement in catalysis.
Control of an Active Site Water in the Formation of
the Diterpene Alcohol 3. To date, six protein structures of
class I TSs involved in diterpene biosynthesis utilizing
precyclized diphosphate intermediates have been reported,
including Rv3378c (3WQL),36 Sat1646 (7E4O),33 Stt4548
(7E4M),33 LrdC (6OH6),37 BjKS (4W4R),38 and AgAS (3S9
V) (Figure S11).39 For the following analysis, the plant enzyme
AgAS and Rv3378c that has a unique function acting as a
prenyltransferase in the biosynthesis of 1-tuberculosinyladeno-
sine40 were excluded. For Cts11 and the other four
characterized enzymes a good alignment of almost all active
site residues was observed, only for Y339 of Cts11 and the
corresponding residues in the other four enzymes large
conformational differences were found (Figure S12A). In
Cts11, Y339 forms hydrogen bridges with T70 and the main
chain carbonyl group of G66 (Figures 4B and S12B) slightly
turning Y339 away from the active site, which may enlarge the
space in the active site for water entry. This unique structural
feature of Cts11 coincides with the fact that only Cts11, but
none of the other enzymes for which a crystal structure has
been reported, forms a diterpene alcohol as the main product.
In agreement with this hypothesis, disruption of the
hydrogen bridge in the Y339F variant resulted in the enhanced
production of the hydrocarbons syn-isopimara-1,7(8)-diene
(5) and a new compound (6), coinciding with a reduced
formation of 3 (Figure S13). NMR-based structure elucidation
revealed that compound 6 shares the same 6,6,7-tricyclic
skeleton as compound 3, with the difference that the hydroxyl
group at C3 is replaced by a double bond (Figures S10, S39−
S45, Tables S6 and S7). The formation of 6 can be explained
by the direct deprotonation of C3 in intermediate D rather
than quenching by water (Scheme 1). This finding is
explainable because the introduced F339 can turn toward the

■CONCLUSION
Using a newly constructed bacterial Pfam model, we identified
a pair of diterpene synthases, Cpt11 and Cts11, from the
cyanobacterium Scytonema tolypothrichoides. This pair of
diterpene synthases converts GGPP first into the intermediate
syn-CPP and then into the rare 6,6,7-tricyclic diterpene 3,
identifying the first bacterial syn-CPP synthase and a unique
type I DTS. The detailed elucidation of the biosynthetic
pathway for 3 through isotopic labeling experiments and DFT
calculations deepens our understanding the formation of the
seven-membered ring. Interestingly, oxidized derivatives of ent-
3 produced through ent-CPP are known from the plant
Siegesbeckia pubescens48 that will likely be formed via the
enantiomeric series of cationic intermediates as described here
for 3. So far, a very different biosynthetic proposal lacking
experimental or computational evidence was available for these
plant-derived materials.48 The crystal structure of Cts11 gave
insights into active site residues. Disruption of a hydrogen
bonding network between Y339 and the main chain carbonyl
oxygen of G66 that is unique to Cts11 among the so far
crystallized labdane-related type I DTSs showed a major
influence on diterpene alcohol formation. The structural
information gained here will be a basis for further engineering
of diterpene synthases that can produce more diverse,
unnatural diterpenes. Various diterpenes produced by
diterpene synthase homologues in different cyanobacteria
species also demonstrate the potential of cyanobacteria to
create a valuable class of terpenoids.
