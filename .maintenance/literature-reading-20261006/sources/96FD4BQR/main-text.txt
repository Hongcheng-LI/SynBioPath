ABSTRACT: The limonoids are a family of tetranortriterpenoids that
share a steroid-like ring system with a conserved 3′-substituted furan
moiety. The family exhibits a high degree of structural diversity arising
from extensive oxidations and skeletal rearrangements. This account
chronicles the evolution of our chemoenzymatic strategy toward various
limonoids from two feedstock terpenes, sclareol and sclareolide. An
initial route starting from sclareol successfully delivered the limonoid
tetracyclic skeleton but was deemed to lack the versatility to access more
oxidized family members. A second-generation strategy that com-
menced from sclareolide was conceived to allow convergent access to
gedunin, Corey’s protolimonoid, nimbocinol and azadiradione. Finally,
the strategy was further extended to the synthesis of two ring-seco
limonoids, methyl angolensate and mexicanolide.
■INTRODUCTION
The limonoid natural products are a family of tetranor-
triterpenoid bearing immense structural diversity and a broad
range of bioactivities.1 The term limonoid itself was derived
from limonin (1, Figure 1A),2 which was the first characterized
citrus bitter principle3a in 1960 after a long period of
investigation to determine what accounts for the lingering
bitterness in navel orange juice.3 With more than 300 family
members isolated to date, limonoids are widely found in the
leaves, stem bark and seeds of Rutaceae and Meliaceae
plants.1c,f In addition to being the bitter principles of citrus
fruits, ongoing studies have also revealed the remarkable
biological activities of limonoids, including insecticidal,
antibacterial, antifungal, antimalarial, anticancer, and antiviral
activities.1,4
The structural complexity of limonoids originated from their
highly oxygenated skeletons and their propensity to undergo
extensive skeletal rearrangements. Heasley proposed that
limonoids can be classified into four subcategories: ring-intact
limonoid, degraded limonoid, ring-seco limonoid, and highly
oxidatively modified limonoids based on their structural
features.1e Ring-intact limonoids, as suggested by its name,
share an intact 6/6/6/5 tetracyclic all-carbon framework. In
contrast, degraded limonoids typically refer to family members
with missing A and B rings. Ring-seco limonoids consist of
members that have undergone C−C cleavage on any of their
four rings. For instance, D ring-seco limonoid gedunin (2)
shows oxidative fission between C16 and C17 and C ring-seco
limonoid nimbolide (4) exhibits C12−C13 bond cleavage.
Finally, highly oxidatively modified limonoids are those that
have undergone extensive ring cleavage and additional skeletal
rearrangements.
The biosynthesis of limonoids remained elusive for decades
until the complete biosynthetic pathways of azadirone and
khihadalactone A were revealed by Sattely and co-workers,5a
which served as a landmark for understanding the origins and
divergence of these structurally intriguing triterpenes from
genetic and enzymatic perspectives. This work revealed that
most limonoids are generated in nature from an initial
epoxidation of squalene. Subsequent cyclization, oxidation
and a series of methyl and hydride shifts lead to the
protolimonoid melianol, which further diversifies into more
complex members of the family.5
The structural intrigue of limonoids along with their notable
bioactivities have drawn enormous attention from the synthetic
community,6 and extensive efforts have been put in the past
three decades into the total syntheses of intricate limonoids
ever since Corey’s first synthesis of azadiradione (1) in 1989.6a
A landmark synthesis of azadirachtin was accomplished by Ley
and co-workers in 2007,6b,c which represents a triumph for
modern organic chemistry in solving the synthetic conundrum
of one of the most highly oxygenated limonoids. The first total
synthesis of limonin was achieved by Yamashita and co-

As alluded above, synthetic efforts on the limonoids to date
have placed a strong emphasis on ring-seco and highly modified
limonoids. In fact, only two synthetic approaches toward ring
intact limonoids have been reported (Figure 1B), and both
came from the Corey group.6a,7 While oxidation state can often
be used as a proxy for structural complexity and, by extension,
synthetic difficulties presented by a given target,8 constructing
less oxygenated ring-intact limonoids still presents a unique
challenge in assembling an all-carbon tetracyclic ring system
with multiple quaternary centers, particularly on the B and C
rings, with few to no functional handles. Previously, our group
disclosed a convergent de novo synthesis of ring D-seco
limonoid gedunin (2),6p featuring the use of enzymatic
oxidation and modern transition-metal-catalyzed transforma-
tions for accessing two synthons with similar complexity. This
full account chronicles the evolution of our chemoenzymatic
strategy for the synthesis of ring-intact and seco-limonoids,
starting with a first-generation linear approach, which was
subsequently revised to a second-generation convergent
coupling approach. During the course of this work, the
syntheses of four limonoids, namely Corey’s protolimonoid,
azadiradione, mexicanolide and methyl angolensate were
accomplished.
In the context of ring intact limonoids, Corey’s use of
polyene cyclization was a seminal approach to efficiently build
the ABC ring system.6a,7 While this strategy was successfully
applied in the synthesis of a hypothetical protolimonoid and
azadiradione, it suffers from the need for lengthy manipulations
for the D ring construction. As a result, the synthesis of Corey’s
protolimonoid and azadiradione could only be accomplished in
20 and 27 steps, respectively. Our initial design for synthesizing
intact limonoids was inspired by a previous report from our
group in 2020,9 wherein sclareol was converted to tricyclic acid
8 in 6 steps and submitted to divergent functionalization to
complete the synthesis of taondiol, chevalone A, decaturin E
and stypodiol. Noting the exact structural mapping of 8 onto
the A/B/C-ring of intact limonoids, we postulated that 8
would be a viable starting point for the synthesis (Figure 2A).
Specifically, Corey’s protolimonoid (6) was deemed accessible
via radical cyclization from ketone 7, which in turn would be
prepared by appending a furan-containing fragment to 8.
Additionally, we proposed that the route could be adjusted
accordingly to prepare various ring-seco limonoids by
incorporating additional oxidative modifications.

conditions were employed to elicit chemoselective enyne
cyclization to construct the desired tetracyclic framework,
which also left a vinyl stannane motif on the D ring.
Transmetalation in the presence of Cu(OAc)2 converted this
motif to the corresponding vinyl acetate.13 Finally, ester
hydrolysis under mild basic conditions afforded 3-deoxypro-
tolimonoid (19), concluding our model study for proto-
limonoid synthesis.
At this stage, we anticipated that Corey’s protolimonoid
would be attainable if we were to start with 8 instead of 11 and
protect the C3 alcohol before implementing the rest of the
sequence for D-ring formation. However, when considering its
applicability to more complex limonoids, the strategy was
deemed too linear and would lead to less-than-ideal step count.
Furthermore, while the tricyclic acid 8 can be concisely
prepared from sclareol, it lacks functional handle on its B- and/
or C-ring, which would prohibit synthetic access to more
oxidized ring-seco limonoids. At this time, our lab had
successfully developed a concise synthesis of gedunin (2) by
performing biocatalytic hydroxylation on known enone 20,
followed by fragment coupling with iodide 22 for a stepwise C-
ring formation (Figure 3A).6p The high structural similarity of
gedunin to Corey’s protolimonoid and azadiradione led us to
believe that the strategy can be readily adapted in the synthesis
of these intact limonoids (Figure 3B). Furthermore, the
convergent nature of the synthesis was anticipated to provide a
lower step count, while offering the added versatility and
modularity to access additional family members. Retrosyntheti-
cally, both azadiradione (1) and Corey’s protolimonoid (6)
could be traced back to a common intermediate 24 by
conducting hydrogen-atom-transfer (HAT)-based Giese cou-
pling14 and oxidation state manipulations. In turn, 24 was
envisioned to arise from convergent coupling of fragment 23
and enone 21.

with the use of Brown’s kinetic resolution method to establish
the chiral quaternary center at C13 (limonoid numbering),15
allowing the preparation of enantioenriched 25 in two steps.

In light of our success in applying Krische’s hydrogen

to prepare 33. Our effort began with the TBS-protected diol
35a, which was obtained in two steps from 3-furfuryl alcohol

displacement of the secondary alcohol with an allyl

would construct the desired carbon skeleton of 33. However,
both Keck-type radical allylation and Lewis acid-induced
Mukaiyama-type Keck allylation only led to a complex mixture
of products.19 We next explored an alternative sequence
featuring allylation on the secondary alcohol, followed by RCM
to give 37, which would be coaxed to undergo a [1,2]-Wittig
rearrangement.20 However, attempts to effect this rearrange-
ment on 37 failed to deliver the desired product. Based on
Aggarwal’s precedent,21 we also investigated the borylative
displacement of the secondary alcohol but only degradation of
35b was observed. Other approaches, such as halogenation of
the secondary alcohol, homologation of the corresponding
ketone via van Leusen reaction or via Wittig reaction using
methoxymethylenetriphenylphosphine22 also proved fruitless.
All of these approaches were also explored with the MOM-
protected counterpart of 35a, but no improvements were
observed.
As an alternative, we sought to apply Hoveyda’s Ag/Cu-
mediated asymmetric conjugate addition23 on 43 (Figure 4B)
and trapping the enolate intermediate with TES group, which
established the desired stereochemistry of the quaternary
center while also setting the stage for Pd-mediated coupling
between the silyl enol ether and 3-bromofuran.24 Unfortu-
nately, we did not observe any cross-coupled product 40 after
multiple attempts. We attributed this failure to the challenging
nature of the cross-coupling owing to the steric hindrance of
the neighboring quaternary center. The highly sensitive nature
of enol ether 42, which was prone to hydrolyze, may also have
negative influence on the coupling.
At this juncture, we became aware of Yang’s synthesis of
haperforin G and were inspired to explore an interrupted Heck
approach to construct 33.6g,25 As the C17 stereochemistry was
shown by Yang to be crucial for influencing the stererochem-
ical outcome of the quaternary center formation, a route
featuring asymmetric isopropenyl Michael addition, Z-vinyl
iodide formation and hydroxylation was designed (Figure 4C).

Based on the above considerations, we sought for an
alternative sequence of transformations for the B-ring cleavage.
Pioneering studies by Suárez and co-workers have shown that
when converted to the corresponding alkoxy radical,37
secondary alcohol adjacent to a quaternary center can undergo
selective β-cleavage to favor the formation of the more
substituted carbon radical product. Our exploration thus
commenced with applying Suarez fragmentation on 7-
deacetylgedunin (73, Figure 5B). While we have previously
reported the synthesis of this compound, initial explorations of
its oxidative fragmentation were done with material obtained
from commercial neem oil, as it allowed us to obtain more
than 1 g quantity in a single pass (see Supporting Information
for details). Surprisingly, though no desired product was
observed in the fragmentation, a degraded side product, epoxy-
pyroangolensolide 74 was isolated instead. We speculated that
the alkoxy radical was generated initially upon exposure of the
hypoiodite intermediate to light, effecting β-scission to cleave
the B-ring. However, the resulting radical species did not
recombine with iodine and instead underwent elimination with
concomitant cleavage of the C9−C10 bond, likely due to the
resonance-stabilized nature of the resulting allylic radical on
the A-ring. The A-ring fragment possibly underwent further
degradation as we were not able to isolate any potential
byproduct of interest. Even though desired B-ring cleavage did
not occur, the observed outcome was still encouraging as it
suggests that the alkoxy radical generation and β-scission have
proceeded successfully. To suppress the C9−C10 fragmenta-
tion pathway, we decided to investigate the β-fragmentation
strategy on deoxydeacetylgedunin (75, Scheme 4A), reasoning
that the resulting C8 allylic radical from 75 will be more long-
lived to undergo successful trapping with iodine. To our
delight, the B-ring-cleaved product 76 was obtained in an
acceptable yield after carefully screening various combinations
of oxidant, base and solvent (see Table S6 for details). Pinnick
oxidation of 76 and methylation delivered methyl ester 77,
therewith establishing the carbon skeleton of B ring-seco
limonoids.
Initial efforts to manipulate the oxidation state at C1 by
regioselective epoxidation were unexpectedly challenging.
Various epoxidation conditions resulted in exclusive reaction
at the exo methylene or degradation (see Table S7 for
details).6e,38 To resolve this issue, we decided to epoxidize the
A-ring of 75 prior to Suarez fragmentation (Scheme 4B).
Subjection of 75 to mild epoxidation condition successfully
delivered the desired product 79 in high yield and with
complete regio- and facial selectivity. The epoxyketone moiety
remained intact after the B-ring cleavage and the following
oxidation, which provided methyl ester 72.39 Fortuitously,
reacting 72 with SmI2 or in situ generated NaSePh40 not only
effected epoxide opening, but also the formation of methyl
angolensate (67) by in situ intramolecular oxa-Michael
addition from the newly formed C1 hydroxyl group to the
unsaturated lactone without any detection of 70.

■CONCLUSION
Herein, we reported the evolution of our synthetic strategy
toward ring intact and B-ring seco limonoids, resulting in the
synthesis of four limonoids, namely Corey’s protolimonoid (11
steps LLS), azadiradione (15 steps LLS), methyl angolensate
(14 steps LLS) and mexicanolide (14 steps LLS). A fully linear
first-generation approach that commenced from sclareol
successfully established the intact limonoid skeleton. However,
the suboptimal modularity of the route prompted us to
investigate a convergent coupling approach, which was first
applied to the synthesis of gedunin. To adapt the approach to
intact limonoids, we investigated a number of approaches to
prepare a highly substituted cyclopentene fragment. Ulti-
mately, the desired fragment was successfully obtained through
the use of an interrupted Heck reaction and the intended
coupling was successfully carried out en route to Corey’s
protolimonoid and azadiradione. To further expand the utility
of our approach, we leveraged the C7 alcohol on a gedunin-
type intermediate as a functional handle to gain access to B-
seco limonoids via β-cleavage of the corresponding alkoxy
radical. Divergent functionalization of an epoxyketone
intermediate obtained from this sequence afforded methyl
angolensate and mexicanolide. Further application of this idea
to synthesize cipadonoid B was investigated, but failed to
deliver the desired natural product. This work especially
highlights the utility of a biocatalytically prepared hydrox-
yenone building block (21) in the convergent synthesis of
several terpenoid natural products. The strategy is also
noteworthy for its modularitya simple switch of the iodide
coupling partner allowed access to different limonoid frame-
worksand its straightforward combination with HAT-based
Giese cyclization to construct highly congested internal rings
of fused polycycles. Further applications of this strategy to
other limonoids and labdane-containing terpenoids are on-
going.
