A Stereoselective Decarboxylative Aromatase/Cyclase Directs the
Biosynthesis of an Axially Chiral Biphenyl Framework in Fasamycin
Kai Jiang, Cheng Zhu, Xiaoli Yan, Guangjun Li, Zhi Lin, Zixin Deng, Shukun Luo,* and Xudong Qu*

Supporting Information
ABSTRACT: Aromatic polyketides are an important class of natural products with various bioactivities, and their structural
diversity arises from modifications to their aromatic frameworks. In this study, we identify a stereoselective aromatase/cyclase
(ARO/CYC) FasU, which is responsible for forming the axial chiral biphenyl framework in fasamycin. FasU catalyzes sequential
decarboxylation and cyclization/aromatization with strict S-stereospecificity on a previously unidentified biosynthetic intermediate.
Through crystal structure analysis and site-directed mutagenesis, we reveal the enzyme’s substrate binding mode, stereospecificity,
and the key residues involved in catalysis. This discovery introduces a novel class of ARO/CYC enzymes in type II polyketide
biosynthesis, advancing the development of biocatalysts for chiral aromatic polyketides.
A
romatic polyketides are a large class of natural products
with diverse biological activities.1−3 Their biosynthesis is
primarily orchestrated by type II polyketide synthases (PKSs),
wherein the minimal PKS complex assembles polyketide chains
that are subsequently cyclized by aromatase/cyclase (ARO/
CYC) enzymes to form basic aromatic frameworks.4−7 These
frameworks act as templates for further post-PKS modifica-
tions, resulting in the intricate structural diversity of
polyketides.1,8 However, despite the range of postmodifica-
tions, the basic aromatic frameworks have exhibited very
limited structural variability, with only eight distinct planar
conjugated structures characterized to date (Figure 1).9
Therefore, discovering new ARO/CYCs capable of catalyzing
novel cyclization patterns is essential for enhancing the
structural and functional diversity of aromatic polyketides.
Fasamycin (FAS) and its congeners are a group of aromatic
polyketides that exhibit potent activity against methicillin-
resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and vancomycin-
resistant Enterococcus faecalis (VRE).10−27 Unlike typical planar
aromatic structures, FAS features a unique axially chiral
biphenyl framework (Figure 1). Recent studies have elucidated
the formation of the phenyldimethylanthrone (PDA) core of
FAS, which occurs through tetracyclization catalyzed by a
TcmI-like ARO/CYC (FasL) in conjunction with a non-
essential helper ARO/CYC (FasD) (Figure 2).9 However, the
mechanism underlying the formation of the axially chiral
biphenyl structure, termed the E-ring, remains unclear. In this
study, we present a novel cyclization mechanism, revealed
through both in vivo and in vitro studies, which identifies the
first stereoselective decarboxylative ARO/CYC responsible for
E-ring formation in FAS.
The biosynthesis of the PDA core involves a minimal PKS
(FasA, FasB, and FasC), a methyltransferase (FasT), a
tetracyclic ARO/CYC (FasL), and a helper ARO/CYC
(FasD) (Figure 2).9,12 FasR, FasS, and FasU are the remaining
proteins that putatively encode ARO/CYCs and are likely to
catalyze E-ring formation in FAS. FasS and FasU are members
of the antibiotic biosynthesis monooxygenase (ABM) super-
family (cl46813).9 In a previous report, the deletions of forS
and forU (homologues to fasS and fasU, respectively) in
another FAS gene cluster resulted in a reduction or complete
loss of FAS production, indicating their important roles in E-
ring formation.16
To confirm this, we overexpressed FasS and FasU in
Escherichia coli and evaluated their cyclization activity on the
substrate PDA. However, neither enzyme shows activity
(Figure 3A, traces I, II). Similarly, FasR did not catalyze the
expected reaction (Figure 3A, trace III), suggesting that PDA is
not the direct substrate for cyclization. Since PDA is derived
from the decarboxylation of a hypothesized intermediate 19
(Figure 2B), we speculated that this intermediate might be the
true substrate.
The putative intermediate 1 containing a highly unstable β-
keto acid moiety cannot be detected in the fermentation broth.
Therefore, an in vitro biosynthesis was employed to obtain it.
The ARO/CYCs (Abx(+)C, Abx(+)D) and methyltransferase
(Abx(+)M) from the (+)-anthrabenzoxocinone (ABX) bio-
synthetic pathway exhibit higher activity than their homo-
logues from the FAS pathway (FasD, FasL, and FasT).9 Thus,
these three enzymes, along with the minimal PKS (FasA, FasB,
and FasC), malonyl-CoA ligase (MatB), acyltransferase
(FabD), and S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) were combined
to constitute a one-pot biosynthetic reaction. Consistent with
previous results,9 this reaction can produce PDA under the
assay condition (37 °C, pH 8.0).

Since β-keto acids can be stabilized by maintaining them in
their carboxylate form and at low temperatures, we optimized
the temperature and pH of the reaction (Figure 3A, traces IV−
VI, Figure S1). To our delight, at pH 8.5, two new compounds
were observed, one of which exhibited the expected molecular
weight and UV absorption characteristic of presumed 1. The
other compound was identified as fasamycin F (FAS-F, Figure
3A),16 which features a carboxyl group at C2 and is believed to
have spontaneously cyclized from 1. The yield of 1 can be
further increased when the reaction is conducted at a lower
temperature (12 °C) (Figure 3A, trace VI, Figure S1), while it
can rapidly convert to PDA at 37 °C via spontaneous
decarboxylation.
We next optimized the concentration of each enzyme
component in the reaction system through systematic titration
to increase the yield of 1 by more than five times. To further
enhance the signal in the NMR carbon spectrum, we
introduced [1,2,3-13C3] malonyl-CoA into the reaction.
HRMS analysis confirmed a 26 Da mass shift of 1, indicating
that the polyketide carbon chain comprises 26 carbon atoms,
one more carbon atom than PDA (Figure 3D). Additionally,
the 13C NMR analysis of the purified 13C-1 revealed a new
carbon signal for the carboxyl group at 172.06 ppm compared

ppm splits into a doublet, adhering to the 2n+1 rule, suggesting
that only one carbon atom is coupled to the observed carbon.
Apart from 26-Me-C (δ = 21.74 ppm), which can split into a
doublet, only the terminal carboxyl carbon can exhibit a similar
pattern. Therefore, based on the combination of in vitro
experimental results, the spontaneous cyclization of 1 to FAS-
F, isotopic labeling outcomes, and 13C NMR spectra, we can
conclude that 1 is the product containing the PDA core with a
terminal carboxyl group.
Next, we investigated whether 1 could serve as a substrate
for E-ring formation. When FasS and FasR were introduced
into the reaction system, neither enzyme converted 1 into FAS
(Figure S2). However, upon addition of FasU, both 1 and
PDA disappeared, yielding only FAS (Figure 3B, trace II). This
result was reproducible when purified 1 was used as a substrate
(Figure 3B, traces III−IV), and FasU exhibited a kcat/KM value
of 60.33 × 10−3 min−1 μM−1 at 12 °C (Figure S3). Therefore,
it is confirmed that 1 is the substrate for FasU. To validate
these findings in vivo, we inserted fasU into the cosmid
pJTU103,9 which harbors the (+)-ABX biosynthetic genes
responsible for 1 biosynthesis, and expressed the engineered
cosmid in Streptomyces coelicolor M1154.28 As anticipated, the
resulting strain also produced FAS (Figure 3C, trace I).
Phylogenetic analysis revealed that FasU belongs to a novel
category of ARO/CYC, with no apparent relation to previously
reported ARO/CYC enzymes (Figure S4). Additionally, other
than the artifact racemic products such as formicapyridine A,16
streptovertidines A and B,15 all the fasamycin-like products
have been identified as the S-configuration,10−27 suggesting
that FasU has S-stereoselectivity.
To understand the catalytic mechanisms of FasU, we
determined its crystal structure at a resolution of 2.01 Å
(Figure 4A, Table S1). FasU forms a compact homodimer,
with each subunit comprising three α-helices (α1, α2, and α3)
and five β-sheets (β1, β2, β3, β4, and β5) (Figure 4B, Figure
S5). The active site is situated within the cavity of each subunit
and accessible through a wide entrance defined by the β1−3
strands and α1−3 helices (Figure 4A). Due to the instability of
1, we attempted to use PDA as the substrate for FasU to obtain
complex structures; however, this was unsuccessful. Con-
sequently, we performed docking of 1 into FasU, followed by
molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to optimize the
resulting complex structures (Figure 4C). The simulations
revealed minimal conformational changes with RMSD of 1.641
Å, suggesting that FasU does not require significant structural
rearrangements to bind substrates and catalyze reactions.
In the catalytic cavity, T63 and D61 form hydrogen bonds
with the C1 hydroxyl and C3 carbonyl groups of 1 (Figure 4C,
Figures S6 and S7), bringing the C2 of 1 into proximity with
C19 (3.5 Å). This arrangement facilitates the attack of the
decarboxylated C2 enolate on the C19 carbonyl carbon,

thereby completing the cyclization. This binding conformation
exhibits similarities to the α/β-hydrolase-type tomato methyl
ketone synthase 1 (MKS1), which utilizes T18 and H243 for
substrate binding and decarboxylation activity.29 The D-ring of
1 partially occupies the hydrophobic binding pocket of FasU,
with the C26 methyl group oriented inward, while the
hydrophilic C21 and C23 hydroxyl groups are exposed to
the solvent. This orientation secures the D-ring to the “Sa” face
of the E-ring (Figure 4D), conferring the axial chirality
selectivity in FasU. Given that the hydrophobic pocket lacks
residues capable of anchoring the D-ring’s hydroxyl groups, we
speculate that FasU’s stereospecificity arises from hydrophobic
repulsion between the pocket and the C21 and C23 hydroxyl
groups. To validate this assumption, we performed a 50 ns MD
simulation. As expected, among the 2.5 × 107 generated
conformations, the D-ring remained fixed on the “Sa” face in
100% of cases (Figure S8).
We next conducted a mutagenesis study to confirm the
importance of these residues in the FasU catalysis. As
anticipated, mutations T63A and D61N resulted in a complete
loss of catalytic activity (Figure 4E and Figure S6). D61 and
T63 are positioned close enough to form a general acid−base
pair. D61 is believed to transfer a proton to T63, which
subsequently protonates the C3-ketone, facilitating the
decarboxylation process of 1 (Figure 4E). The resulting
enolate undergoes cyclization and aromatization to form the E-
ring. In this mechanism, the catalytic role of D61 is analogous
to that of D146 in ZhuI, which catalyzes the cyclization/
aromatization of the first ring in R1128.30 Interestingly, the
decarboxylase MKS1 also contains a threonine (T18) at the
position of T63, where it forms a hydrogen bond interaction
with the carboxylate group of the substrate.29 However, MSK1
utilizes a histidine (H243) to catalyze decarboxylation via an
acid−base mechanism. In FasU, H35 is located near D61
(1.863 Å), but its role in catalysis was excluded, as H35Q
retained 42% of the cyclization activity (Figure S6). These
findings established the pivotal role of D61 and T63 in
coordinating and catalyzing the E-ring cyclization.
In conclusion, we have successfully elucidated the unique
biosynthetic mechanism responsible for the formation of the
axial chiral biphenyl structure in FAS. FasU has been identified
as the first ARO/CYC catalyzing decarboxylative cyclization
and controlling axial chirality in the biosynthesis of an aromatic
polyketide framework. The discovery of this cyclization
mechanism not only provides insights into the structural
diversity of aromatic polyketides but also highlights the
potential for engineering new ARO/CYCs to create poly-
ketides with axially chiral frameworks and biological activities.
