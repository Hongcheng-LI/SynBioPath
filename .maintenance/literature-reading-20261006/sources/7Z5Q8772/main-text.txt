Structure and Mechanism of the Azomycin Biosynthetic Enzyme
RohQ That Catalyzes a Spontaneous Cyclodehydration
Zi-Wang Wei, Phillip Daniel-Ivad, Li Zhang, and Katherine S. Ryan*

ABSTRACT: RohQ from the azomycin biosynthetic pathway catalyzes a spontaneous cyclodehydration to form 2-aminoimidazole.
Here we report the structure and mechanism of RohQ and use a serendipitously bound imidazole to pinpoint active site residues.
We propose that catalysis occurs at the dimeric interface using two key aspartic acid residues for proton transfer steps to accelerate 3
× 104-fold intramolecular cyclization of a guanidino group and aldehyde, releasing water. Our work expands our understanding of de
novo emerged enzymes and provides the first structural and mechanistic view of a yet-unexplored protein family.
B
iochemical reactions are typically enzyme-catalyzed. In
primary metabolism, almost all transformations are
enzyme-catalyzed1 with one known exception being the
spontaneous step converting glutamic-γ-semialdehyde to
pyrroline-5-carboxylate in proline biosynthesis (Figure 1A),
for which no enzyme has been discovered.2 However,
spontaneous chemistry is more common in secondary
metabolism. For example, in the biosynthetic pathway to
betalain pigments, dopaquinone and 4,5-secodopa sponta-
neously cyclize to cyclo-dopa and betalamic acid, respectively.
The two then condense spontaneously to the aldimine
betanidin (Figure 1B).3−5 Another example is the siderophore
acinetobactin, formed via spontaneous isomerization (Figure
1C).6 Occasionally, enzymes are later linked to reactions
previously proposed to occur spontaneously.7,8 For example,
the long-presumed spontaneous transformation of (7S)-
salutaridinol-7-O-acetate to thebaine was later demonstrated
to be catalyzed by an enzyme, thebaine synthase THS (Figure
1D).7 Indeed, discovery of the enzymes that catalyze
spontaneous reactions can unearth de novo enzymes −i.e.
enzymes that evolve from protein families with no apparent
connections to the reactions. For example, chalcone isomerase
lacks similarity to other enzymes and catalyzes the otherwise
spontaneous intramolecular cyclization of chalcone into
naringenin, with the enzyme controlling the stereochemical
outcome to give the (S)-isomer (Figure 1E).9
The naturally occurring alkaloid antibiotic azomycin (1) was
discovered in the 1950s,10 and its structure features a
nitroimidazole pharmacophore that inspired the synthesis of
a wide suite of clinically important nitroimidazole drugs
(Figure 2A).11−15 The core feature of the molecules is the
nitroimidazole, which forms reduction products (i.e., reactive
radical species) like the nitro radical anion under reduction
potentials in cells that can inhibit DNA synthesis and oxidize
the existing DNA to induce strand breakage, eventually leading
to cell death.12,13,15 Biosynthetic studies on azomycin revealed
it derives from the intermediate 2-aminoimidazole (2), in turn
derived from arginine via oxygen-, pyridoxal-phosphate-
dependent chemistry.16−19 Our group previously identified
the long-elusive gene cluster (roh) for 1 and reconstituted the
biosynthetic pathway in vitro (Figure 2B).20,21 One key finding
is that the spontaneous conversion of the retro-aldol cleavage
product guanidinoacetaldehyde (3) to 2 is catalyzed by a
cyclodehydratase, RohQ.20 All but one of the predicted roh
gene clusters include a rohQ gene (Figure S1), supporting the
important role of RohQ in the azomycin biosynthetic pathway.
However, the rate acceleration and catalytic mechanism for
this enzyme are obscure. The evolutionary origin of RohQ is
also enigmatic, as Pfam search places this enzyme into a
domain family of unknown function (DUF6190) with fewer
than 200 proteins. BLAST searches give otherwise ∼390
nonredundant sequences that are primarily RohQ-like
(DUF6190-kind) and display no detectable sequence homol-
ogy to other proteins, implying the uniqueness and de novo
emergence of RohQ. Sequence similarity network (SSN) and
genomic neighborhood analyses furthermore indicate that
proteins within this DUF6190 family are closely related,
sharing a highly similar roh gene cluster context (Figures S1
and S2).
To provide insights into the de novo emergence of RohQ as a
catalyst, we identified a homologous enzyme encoded in an
azomycin gene cluster from Pseudomonas brassicacearum DF41
(accession number: WP_025211970.1; Figure S1) and
demonstrated that recombinant PbrRohQ (called RohQ
here) possesses the same cyclodehydration activity in vitro
(Figure S3−S4).20 Using enzymes (RohP + RohR) at low
temperatures and long reaction times to preferentially
accumulate 3, we measured the kinetic parameter of RohQ-
catalyzed cyclodehydration of 3 at 25 °C at pH 7.4 (Figure
S5), and obtained a kcat = 0.59 ± 0.03 s−1. We determined the

We then solved the 2.10 Å crystal structure of RohQ (Table
S2 and Figure 3A), finding that RohQ forms a homodimer
with an asymmetric unit consisting of 5 protomer copies
(Figure S7). Size-exclusion chromatography analysis supports
that RohQ is a dimer in solution (Figure S8). Each protomer
contains nine short α-helices and two β-strands that comprise a
β−α−α−β motif where the two β-strands align with each
other, forming a cap covering the gap between the two α-
helices (α3 and α4, Figure S9 and Figure 3B). These two α-
helices align against their counterparts in the partner protomer
at the dimer interface where hydrogen bonds and π-
interactions are observed. On the other end of the dimeric
interface, two Cys residues (C92/C92′) form a disulfide bond,
presumably contributing to the dimer stability (Figure S10).
RohQ shows structural similarity to ribonuclease toxin proteins
from PIN-domain-containing VapC (virulence associated
protein C) family (Figure S11) but forms a distinct dimer
interface by holding dimeric interactions in different parts of
their tertiary structures (Figure S12). RohQ-like proteins lack
sequence homology with VapC family proteins, as reflected by
their poor relatedness in the SSN analysis (Figure S2).
During structural refinement of the RohQ structures, we
observed electron density consistent with imidazole within the
dimeric interface of the two β−α−α−β motifs (Figure S10),
which likely copurified with His6-tagged RohQ during gradient
imidazole elution. This trapped imidazole appears in multiple
diffraction data sets of RohQ crystals (Figure S13), and we

the α3 and α4 helices of the RohQ β−α−α−β motifs surround
the imidazole, forming a largely hydrophilic binding pocket.
Four Asp residues (D51/D51′ and D70/D70′) are oriented
toward the imidazole, with D70/D70′ forming hydrogen
bonds with the heteroatoms of imidazole and the interoxygen-
atom distance and geometry between D51/D51′ indicating H-
bonding interactions (Figure 3B). The imidazole ring forms
parallel-displaced π-π interactions between two Tyr residues
(Y66/Y66′) (Figure 3B). Assuming this imidazole-anchoring
site represents the active site, the surrounding Asp and Tyr
residues presumably play key roles in catalysis.
To probe the potential active site of RohQ, we targeted a
number of residues for site-directed mutagenesis (Table S3
and Figure S14) and assayed the resulting variants toward in
situ generated 3 (from RohP + RohR coupled assay) (Figure
S3), followed by LC-ESI-MS quantification of the production
of 2 (m/z [M + H]+ = 84) (Figure S15 and Figure 3C).
Variants D51A or D70A showed complete loss of activity,
confirming the essential roles of D51 and D70 for catalysis.
Similarly, variants D51N, D70N, D51H, or D70H had
abolished enzyme activity (Figure 3C), further supporting
the indispensable catalytic roles of these two Asp residues and
suggesting that both H-bonding interactions and acid−base
chemistry are employed by these aspartate residues during
catalysis, reminiscent of the Asp dyad in aspartate proteases
like HIV-1 protease (PDB: 1KZK), albeit for an entirely
unrelated reaction.22 Variant Y66A, in the same pocket,
reduced the activity by 85%, whereas Y66F showed marginal
activity loss compared to RohQ-wildtype (WT) (Figure 3C),
suggesting that Y66 participates in catalysis by forming π-
interactions with the guanidino group of the substrate and/or
with the aromatic ring system of the intermediate/product.
Furthermore, these results identify the likely active site pocket
(Figure 3B). To rule out the possible involvement of another
pocket, we also targeted residues at several other sites for
mutagenesis (Figure S10) and obtained one expressed variant
Q81A, which gave wildtype-like levels of product formation
(Figure 3C), indicating that this distinct pocket is not involved
in the catalysis.

To further explore the active site pocket, we targeted more
residues within 8 Å of the imidazole center (Figure 3D) and
generated three additional variants. W55A lost all enzyme
activity; M69A reduced the activity by 80%; and R59A had
marginal activity loss (Figure 3C). In this expanded pocket, the
indole NH of W55 forms a hydrogen bond with the side chain
of D70′ in the partner protomer (Figure 3B,D). Alanine
substitution at W55 would disrupt this H-bonding interaction,
thereby impairing the catalytic activity by indirectly affecting
D70′. The indole side chain of W55 also π-stacks with H73’ in
the partner protomer (Figure 3B,D), presumably helping
stabilize the dimeric interface. Residue M69 has a hydrophobic
side chain and mutation of it could potentially break the space-
filling properties required for the neighborhood. For residue
R59 that is at the pocket outer edge, alanine substitution
resulted in a lower yield of soluble protein (Figure S14) but
little effect on the activity, suggesting that this residue may play
a structural role in stabilizing the dimer interface (Figure
3C,D). To rule out disruption to the dimeric assembly of
RohQ caused by mutations, we conducted size-exclusion
chromatography of a subset of the variants and demonstrated
that D51A, D70A, Y66A, Y66F, W55A, and M59A retained the
same elution profile as RohQ-WT (Figure S16).
Based on the site-directed mutagenesis results together with
active site structural details, we propose that the chemistry for
conversion of 3 to 2 takes place at the dimer interface wherein
four Asp residues (D51/D51′ and D70/D70′) play the
essential roles in catalysis, with two Tyr residues (Y66/Y66′)
anchoring the substrate via π interactions, and other residues
such as W55 and M69 contribute by stabilizing the active site
pocket. Following binding of compound 3 in the active site
(Figure S17), Asp70′ abstracts a proton from the positively
charged guanidino group, forming hydrogen bonds, while
Asp70 forms hydrogen bonds with the other side of the
guanidino group (Figure 4). The aldehyde carbonyl is then
protonated likely from the proton held between two inner Asp
residues (D51/D51′). Intramolecular nucleophilic attack of the
terminal −NH2 of the guanidino group onto the protonated
carbonyl carbon proceeds to form the 5-membered ring
intermediate (4), followed by a proton transfer from the now
positively charged −NH2
+ to the exocyclic −OH group that is
possibly mediated by a water molecule from the aqueous
environment within the enzyme. After the elimination of H2O
from the 5-membered ring, Asp51 can deprotonate the
methylene group to drive the aromatic ring formation. The
resultant product abstracts a proton from Asp70′, becoming a
2-aminoimidazolium ion (pKa ≈8.5),23,24 thereby regenerating
the enzyme active site.
We note that the slowness of a spontaneous biosynthetic
transformation might lead to the overaccumulation of
precursors and intermediates,8 the formation of undesired
byproducts,7 or inefficient production of vital secondary
metabolites. In azomycin biosynthesis, one of the pathway
intermediates is the key aldehyde 3. Although this guanidine-
containing aldehyde is intrinsically reactive and can undergo
spontaneous cyclodehydration to 2, an extended reaction
duration (>12 h) does not achieve the full conversion (Figures
S3 and S18). RohQ, however, can effectively convert 3 to 2 −
as signified by a kcat/kuncat ratio up to 4 orders of magnitude
over the uncatalyzed reaction at pH 7.4 −thereby contributing
to preventing the accumulation of the aldehyde intermediate
and increasing the production of azomycin.19,20 Interestingly,
in the biosynthetic pathway to the octapeptide, fusaoctaxin B,
the intermediate 3 was also reported.25 There, however, the
cyclization product of 3 was intercepted by a two-electron-
oxidation process followed by a linearization step to give
guanidinoacetic acid which serves as the starter unit for
fusaoctaxin B (Figure S19). The authors proposed an
uncharacterized endogenous oxidoreductase might catalyze a
two-electron oxidation of the cyclized product 4. In azomycin
pathway, however, RohQ handles the intermediate 3 very
differently, further highlighting the important role of RohQ in
azomycin biosynthesis.
Our work provides mechanistic details and kinetic
information for the RohQ-catalyzed cyclodehydration of 3 to
2 in the pathway to the nitroimidazole antibiotic azomycin.
Our work also offers a structural basis for possible rational
design of the cyclodehydratases in natural product biosyn-
thesis. Future work on engineering microbes for enhanced
production of azomycin or other nitroimidazole derivatives
could exploit RohQ to this end. Furthermore, our work
expands the repertoire of de novo emerged enzymes for
spontaneous chemistry.
