ABSTRACT: Tetrapetalones make up a unique class of pentake-
tide ansamycins that feature a tetracyclic skeleton and exhibit
potent inhibitory activities against soybean lipoxygenase. However,
a detailed biosynthetic route to tetrapetalones has not been
published. Herein we report the activation of the tetrapetalones’
biosynthetic gene cluster (tpt) in Streptomyces sp. S10 by promoter
engineering along with constitutive expression of pathway-specific
regulator genes, leading to the discovery of seven new derivatives,
tetrapetalones E−K (2−8), and the known tetrapetalone A (1). In
vivo gene deletion experiments and heterologous expression of the minimized tpt cluster in Streptomyces albus J1074 suggest that the
tetracyclic system of tetrapetalones is probably formed spontaneously, and the regioselective glycosylation of tetrapetalones at the C-
9 hydroxy group with D-rhamnose or D-rhodinose was catalyzed by the glycosyltransferase Tpt14.
A
nsamycins make up a family of macrolactams with diverse
bioactivities and are characterized by the presence of a
rigid aromatic chromophore connected to a polyketide chain.
According to the polyketide chain length, known ansamycins
are classified into several subgroups, and pentaketide
ansamycins are the smallest. Eight different types of
pentaketide ansamycins have been reported, including Q-
1047,1 tetrapetalones,2,3 cebulactams,4 juanlimycins,5 micro-
ansamycins,6 aminoansamycins,7 catellatolactams,8 and shen-
gliangmycins.9 Tetrapetalones represent a unique group of
pentaketide ansamycins, which were first isolated from the
culture broth of Streptomyces sp. USF-4727 and identified as
novel lipoxygenase inhibitors.2,3,10 Tetrapetalone A (1) and its
derivatives were featured by the presence of a highly decorated
tetracyclic skeleton, which presents numerous synthetic
challenges and triggered great interest in total synthesis
studies.11−19 The biosynthesis of tetrapetalones was inves-
tigated by precursor feeding experiments. The results indicated
that tetrapetalones were constructed from 3-amino-5-hydrox-
ybenzoic acid (AHBA), propanoate, butanoate, and glucose.20
Additionally, ansaetherone obtained from strain USF-4727 was
suggested to be biosynthetic precursor of tetrapetalones.21
However, the tetrapetalone biosynthetic gene cluster has not
been reported, and the molecular mechanism by which
Streptomyces synthesizes tetrapetalones remains unclear.
Streptomyces sp. S10 was isolated from the soil collected at
the Nanjing Botanical Garden, China. The genome sequence
analysis of strain S10 revealed the presence of a cryptic
ansamycin gene cluster (tpt, Figure 1a, Table S3, GenBank
accession No. MG603754). The tpt cluster contains ORFs
coding for three type I polyketide synthases (PKSs, tptA-C)
associated with an amide synthase (tptD), a subset of AHBA
biosynthetic genes (tptH-N), ethylmalonyl coenzyme A (CoA)
biosynthetic genes (tpt5−7), several putative redox genes, and
three regulatory genes (tptR1, tpt10, and tptR2). The putative
PKSs within the cluster contain five modules, implying that
pentaketide ansamycins could be the products. Bioinformatics
analysis of the five acyltransferase (AT) domains in TptA-C
demonstrated that the possible extender units for the assembly
of polyketide chain could be AHBA-C3-C3-C3-C3 (Figure
S1), which is consistent with the pentaketide scaffold of
tetrapetalones or cebulactams.3,4 Herein, we report the
activation of the cryptic pentaketide ansamycin gene cluster
(tpt) in Streptomyces sp. S10 and the identification of seven
new ansamycins, tetrapetalones E−K (2−8), along with the
known tetrapetalone A (1). Furthermore, heterologous
expression of the minimized tpt biosynthetic gene cluster in
Streptomyces albus J1074 provided the first insight into the
formation of the tetracyclic skeleton, which may occur
spontaneously.

poorly expressed if at all under our culture conditions.

However, no extra peaks were detected in the metabolites of
the recombinant strains S10-tptR1 and S10-tptR2 compared

Moreover, their production was improved when the medium

To determine the chemical structure of these compounds,
we prepared a 20 L culture of strain SP101 on YMG agar
medium supplied with 50 mg/L AHBA and obtained 2−5.
Tetrapetalone E (2) was determined to possess the molecular
formula C25H31NO9 by HR-ESIMS (m/z 490.2072 [M + H]+,
Figure S35). The 1D- and 2D-NMR data of 2 (Table S5,
Figures S12, S28−S34) revealed that the structure of 2 is
similar to that of tetrapetalone A (1).10 The difference was that
the ethyl group at C-4 and the rhodinosyl moiety at C-9 in 1
were replaced by a methyl group and a rhamnopyranosyl
moiety in 2, respectively. It was supported by the HMBC
correlations from Me-17 (δH 2.36) to C-3, C-4, and C-5 and
from H-9 (δH 5.46) to C-1′. The α-rhamnopyranosyl moiety
was identified on the basis of the broad singlet of H-1′ (δH
5.50), 1H−1H coupling constants (J2′,3′ = 2.6 Hz, J3′,4’ = 9.0 Hz,
and J4′,5′ = 9.1 Hz), ROESY correlations of H-3′/H-5′ and H-
4′/Me-6′, and the 1JCH value (178 Hz) at the anomeric carbon
(Figure S31).24 The absolute configuration of the rhamnose
was assigned as D by methanolysis of 2 (Figure S19) and
comparison of the optical rotation value of methyl α-
rhamnopyranoside ([α]D
20 +74) with that of the reported one
([α]D
20 +61).25 ROESY correlations of H-9/Me-19, Me-19/H-
7, and H-7/Me-17 supported the notion that the relative
configuration of the tetracyclic skeleton in 2 was the same as
that in 1. From a biosynthetic perspective, the tetracyclic
skeleton in 2−5 was formed as in 1, which would not affect its
absolute configuration. Thus, 2 was assigned, as shown in the
chart.
The molecular formula of tetrapetalone F (3) was
determined to be C19H21NO5 on the basis of HR-ESIMS
(m/z 344.1494 [M + H]+, Figure S42). The NMR data (Table
S6, Figures S13, S36−S41) of 3 showed that its planar
structure was the same as the aglycone of 2. Tetrapetalone G
(4) with a quasi-molecular ion at m/z of 358.1651 [M + H]+
was determined to have the molecular formula C20H23NO5
(Figure S49). The planar structure of 4 was determined to be
very similar to that of 3 (Table S7, Figures S14, S43−S48).
The only difference was that the methyl group at C-4 in 3 was
replaced by the ethyl group in 4, which was supported by the
HMBC correlations from Me-18 (δH 1.09) to C-4 and C-17.
Tetrapetalone H (5) was determined to have the molecular
formula C26H33NO9 on the basis of HR-ESIMS (m/z 504.2227
[M + H]+, Figure S56). Detailed NMR analyses revealed that 5
differed from 4 only by the presence of an additional
rhamnopyranosyl moiety at C-9 (Table S8, Figures S15,
S50−S55). The relative configurations of the tetracyclic ring
system in 3−5 were determined to be the same as that in 2 by
NOESY correlations (Figures S13−S15). Considering that 3−
5 were biosynthesized by the same pathway as 2, the absolute
configuration of 3−5 was determined.
The identification of 2−5 from strain SP101 indicated that
the tpt cluster is responsible for the biosynthesis of
tetrapetalones. Recently, we reported that co-overexpression
of the SARP and LuxR family regulatory genes in the
ansamycin gene cluster aas led to the identification of seven
novel pentaketide ansamycins.7 To see if it works for the tpt
cluster, the regulator genes tptR1 and tptR2 were co-
overexpressed in strains S10 WT and SP101 to give the
recombinant strains S10-tptR1R2 and SP102, respectively
(Figure S4). Interestingly, extra peaks (1, 6−8) were identified
in the metabolites of strain SP102 in addition to 2−5 (Figure
1b), although still no tetrapetalones were detected in S10-

The molecular formula of 1 was established as C26H34NO7

The 1D- and 2D-NMR data (Tables S9−S11) of 6−8 revealed
that they were homologues of 1, but differ in the side chain at
C-4. Tetrapetalone I (6) was identified as containing a methyl
group (δC 22.2) at C-4, which was supported by the HMBC
correlations from Me-17 (δH 2.38) to C-3, C-4, and C-5
(Figures S16, S57−S61). For tetrapetalone J (7), a
hydroxymethyl group (δC 60.4) was deduced to be attached
at C-4 based on the HMBC correlations from H-17 (δH 4.78
and 3.82) to C-3 and C-4 (Figures S17, S63−S68).
Tetrapetalone K (8) was determined to have no side chain
at C-4, which was supported by the HMBC correlations from
H-4 (δH 4.99) to C-3 and C-5 (Figures S18, S70−S75). From
a biosynthetic perspective, the absolute configurations of 6−8
should be the same as that of 1. Moreover, the ECD spectra of
1 and 2−8 were similar (Figure S20), which further confirmed
the absolute configurations of 2−8.
Although tetrapetalones with two different sugar units, D-
rhamnose and D-rhodinose, have been characterized, only one
glycosyltransferase gene (tpt14) was identified in the tpt
cluster. To determine whether tpt14 is involved in the
glycosylation of tetrapetalones, we deleted it from SP102
(Figure S5). The generated mutant SP102Δtpt14 abolished
the production of all glycosylated tetrapetalones (1, 2, and 5−
8, Figure 1b), suggesting that glycosyltransferase Tpt14 can
transfer D-rhamnose or D-rhodinose to the C-9 hydroxy group
of tetrapetalones.
To investigate the formation mechanism of the tetracyclic
system of tetrapetalones, the putative tailoring genes, tpt8, tptE,
tpt12, tpt15, and tpt24−25, were individually deleted from
SP101 (Figure S6). However, none of the mutant strains
abolished the production of tetrapetalones except for
SP101ΔtptE (Figure S9). The 3-(3-hydroxyphenyl)propionate
hydroxylase TptE was proposed to be responsible for the C-15
hydroxylation of AHBA during the polyketide chain elongation
as reported for Rif19, deletion of which also interrupted the
biosynthesis of rifamycins.26 Interestingly, deletion of the
cytochrome P450 gene tpt12 significantly increased the
production of 1 and 6 (Figure S9), but complementation of
tpt12 in SP101Δtpt12 did not restore the original metabolites’
profile (Figures S7, S9), indicating that the improved
production of 1 and 6 was not due to the deletion of tpt12.
Since no candidate genes located in the tpt cluster were
involved in the tetracyclic system formation, we proposed that
it may be constructed spontaneously. To verify this
speculation, a minimized tpt cluster (∼52 kb), containing
only the precursor biosynthetic genes (tptH−N for AHBA and
tpt5−7 for ethylmalonyl CoA), PKSs (tptA−C), amide
synthase (tptD), 3-(3-hydroxyphenyl)propionate hydroxylase
(tptE),27 type II TE (tptZ), transporter-related (tpt11, tpt20−
23), and three regulator genes (tptR1, tptR2, and tpt10), was
constructed and cloned into an integrative BAC vector
(Figures 2a, S8). The tptR1 and tptR2 were under the control
of the ermE* promoter. The resulting plasmid was transformed
into Streptomyces albus J1074 to examine if the production of
tetrapetalones can be observed in the heterologous host; one
major extra peak with the same retention time and UV
spectrum as 4 was detected (Figure 2b), suggesting that the
tetracyclic system of tetrapetalones was formed via sponta-
neous intramolecular reactions.
Based on the above data, the biosynthetic pathway of
tetrapetalones was proposed (Figure 3). Three PKSs (TptA−
C) and amide synthase (TptD) are responsible for assembling
the macrocyclic intermediates (protetrapetalones) using
AHBA as the starter unit and methylmalonyl-CoA as the
extender unit. However, due to the substrate tolerance of the
AT domain in module 3, malonyl-CoA and ethylmalonyl-CoA
can also be used as extender units in polyketide chain
elongation. The hydroxylase TptE is responsible for the C-15
hydroxylation of the AHBA moiety as previously reported.26
Then, the hydroquinone protetrapetalones were spontaneously
oxidized to the quinone intermediates by ambient O2, which
may further undergo a nonenzymatic transformation to form
the tetracyclic system, yielding 3, 4, and intermediate IV.
Transfer of D-rhamnose or D-rhodinose to the C-9 hydroxy
group of the aglycones by Tpt14 generates 1, 2, 5, 6, and 8.
Hydroxylation of 6 at C-17 by an unknown oxidase produces
7.
Compounds 1−8 were evaluated for their antimicrobial
activity against Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25923, Pseudomo-
nas aeruginosa PA01, Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium
UK-1 χ8956, and Candida albicans 5314, but none were active.
Additionally, they showed no cytotoxic activities against
SW480 and HCT116 colon cancer cells at a concentration
of 50 μM.
In summary, heterologous expression of the minimized tpt
cluster in S. albus J1074 reveals that the tetracyclic system of
tetrapetalones probably occurs spontaneously. In recent years,
a growing number of natural products involving nonenzymatic
reactions in their biosynthetic pathways have been charac-
terized.28 Previously, Porco et al. have designed a biomimetic
approach to construct the tetracyclic skeleton of tetrapetalones

■EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
General Experimental Procedures. Optical rotation was carried
out using an Anton Paar MCP200 polarimetry. UV spectra were
measured on a Cary100 Bio spectrophotometer. IR spectra (film)
were obtained on a Nicolet iN10 Micro FT-IR microscope (Thermo
Scientific). NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker DRX-400 or
DRX-600 MHz NMR spectrometer in pyridine-d5 or methanol-d4,
using residual solvent signals (δH 7.21 or 3.31 for 1H and δC 123.5 or
49.0 for 13C) as internal references. HR-ESIMS were carried out on an
LTQ-Orbitrap XL. Sephadex LH-20 (25−100 μm; Pharmacia
Biotech) and LiChroprep RP-18 (40−63 μm; Darmstadt) were
used for column chromatography.
Strains and Cultivation. Streptomyces sp. S10 was isolated from
the Nanjing Botanical Garden, China. Bacterial strains and plasmids
used in this study are listed in Table S1. Streptomyces strains were
grown at 30 °C on SFM solid medium (2% D-mannitol, 2% soybean
meal, 1.5% agar, pH 7.2) for conjugation with Escherichia coli (E. coli),
in YMG broth (0.4% yeast extract, 1% malt extract, 0.4% glucose, pH
7.2) for mycelial growth, or on YMG agar medium for fermentation.
All E. coli strains were cultivated in Luria−Bertani broth (LB) or on
LB agar plates at 37 °C.
Construction of the Promoter Replacement Mutant. To
introduce the ermE* promoter in front of the tptA gene through
homologous recombination, an apramycin resistance gene cassette
aac(3)IV-oriT-ermE*p was amplified from pUC18-aac(3)IV-oriT-
ermE*p with the primer pairs of S10OEbenzPKSF and S10OE-
benzPKSR (Table S2). The λ-Red-mediated recombination was used
to introduce the cassette into fosmid XVII-22K, resulting in XVII-
22K-ermE*p, in which the ermE* promoter was inserted in the same
orientation upstream of tptA. The construct XVII-22K-ermE*p was
then electroporated into E. coli ET12567/pUZ8002, which was then
conjugated with strain S10. The exconjugants were selected with
apramycin to give mutant strain SP101, which was verified by PCR
with the primer pairs of S10PKSF and S10PKSR (Table S2, Figure
S3).
Co-overexpression of the Transcriptional Regulatory Genes
tptR1 and tptR2. The transcriptional regulatory genes (tptR1 and
tptR2) co-overexpression plasmid was constructed using a BioBrick
approach.29 The tptR1 and tptR2 genes were individually amplified by
PCR using the genomic DNA of strain S10 as a template with the
primer pairs tptR1bF/R and tptR2bF/R (Table S2). Each of the PCR
products was cloned into pUC-ermE to give pUC-ermE-tptR1 and
pUC-ermE-tptR2, followed by digestion with EcoRI/SpeI and XbaI/
NsiI, respectively, to give ermE-tptR1 and ermE-tptR2 fragments. They
were ligated into EcoRI/NsiI-digested pJTU824 to produce the
construct pJTU824-R1R2 (Figure S4A), which was integrated into
the chromosome of strains S10 and SP101 by conjugation to give the
recombinant strains S10-tptR1R2 and SP102, respectively (Figure
S4B).

biosynthetic genes, the λ-Red-mediated PCR-targeting method was
used. The spectinomycin resistance gene cassette aadA-oriT was
amplified from pIJ773a with the primer pairs listed in Table S2. The
aadA-oriT cassette was introduced into the corresponding fosmid to
replace the target gene, resulting in the construction of plasmids IX-
22JΔtpt8, XIII-8IΔtptE, XIII-8IΔtpt12, XIII-8IΔtpt14, XIII-8IΔtpt15,
and X-20CΔtpt24−25, respectively. The constructs were electro-
porated into E. coli ET12567/pUZ8002, respectively, which were then
individually conjugated with strain SP101 or SP102 to give the
mutants SP101Δtpt8, SP101ΔtptE, SP101Δtpt12, SP101Δtpt15,

Cloning and Heterologous Expression of the tpt Cluster.
The linear PCR product of vector p15Apr amplified using primer
pairs of 15Apr4Fr1-F/R (Table S2) was used to capture a DNA
fragment covering tpt5-tptM from fosmid IV-15C by λ-Red
recombination. The SARP family regulator gene tptR1 was replaced
by PCR targeting with the ampicillin resistance gene bla, which is
amplified from pUC18 using primers of dSARP-Amp-F/R (Table S2).
The resulting plasmid was digested with HindIII and self-ligated to
give Fragment 1 (Fr1). The left and right regions of Fragment 2 (Fr2)
were amplified from fosmid XVII-22K using primers of tptFr2L-F/R
and tptFr2R-F/R (Table S2), followed by digestion with EcoRI/KpnI
and KpnI/SpeI, respectively, which were then co-ligated into the
EcoRI/SpeI sites of p15ACIS. The resulting plasmid was digested with
BsrGI and MfeI and ligated with the 26 kb BsrGI/MfeI-digested
fragment from fosmid XVII-22K to afford p15ACIS-Fr2. To remove
tpt9, Fr2 was cut from p15ACIS-Fr2 by digestion with MfeI/SpeI and
ligated into single-copy integrative vector pBAC to give pBAC-tptFr2-
dtpt9. Fragment 3 (Fr3) was captured from fosmid X-20C by λ-Red
recombination as above. To delete the LAL family regulator gene
tptR2 and additional tailoring genes tpt24−27, the chloramphenicol
resistance gene cat and ampicillin resistance gene bla were amplified
and used to replace them by PCR targeting. The selection marker was
removed by BglII/HindIII digestion and religation to obtain Fr3. To
constitutively express tptR1 and tptR2, the BioBrick part R1R2 from
pJTU824-R1R2 was assembled by the BioBrick strategy with Fr1 and
Fr3. The tpt8 gene in the resulting plasmid was removed by PCR
targeting, PacI digestion, and self-ligation to give pBAC-Fr1&3-R1R2.
Finally, the XhoI/SpeI-digested pBAC-Fr2dtpt9 and XbaI/XhoI-
digested pBAC-Fr1&3-R1R2, each containing partial vector sequen-
ces, were ligated to obtained the plasmid pBAC-tpt (62,402 bp, Figure
S8A). Then, pBAC-tpt was conjugated into Streptomyces albus J1074
using E. coli ET12567/pUZ8002 as a donor to give the recombinant
strain J1074-tpt, which was verified by PCR amplification with
primers listed in Table S2 (Figure S8B).
Fermentation and Isolation. The mutant strains SP101 and
SP102 were individually cultivated on YMG agar medium (20 L for
each) containing 50 mg/L AHBA for 10 days at 30 °C. Each of the
cultures was chopped, diced, and extracted with EtOAc−MeOH−
AcOH (80:15:5, v/v/v) overnight at room temperature three times.
The extract was partitioned between H2O and EtOAc. Then, the
EtOAc extract was concentrated and further partitioned with
petroleum ether and MeOH to give the MeOH extract. The
MeOH extract of strain SP101 was subjected to column
chromatography (CC) over RP-18 (140 g) eluted with 30%, 50%,
60%, 70%, 80%, and 100% MeOH (200 mL for each gradient) to give
7 fractions: Fr.A−G. Fr.C was purified by semipreparative HPLC
(ZORBAX Eclipse XDB-C18, 9.4 × 250 mm, 4 mL/min, UV 340 nm)
eluted with 23% acetonitrile to afford 2 (tR = 8.5 min, 85 mg), 3 (tR =
9.0 min, 64 mg), and 4 (tR = 10.2 min, 26 mg), respectively. Fr.D
(180 mg) was purified by Sephadex LH-20 (120 g) eluted with
MeOH to obtain Fr.D1−D6. Fr.D2 (23 mg) was purified by
semipreparative HPLC eluted with 23% CH3CN to afford 5 (tR = 9.6
min, 4.0 mg). The MeOH extract of strain SP102 was subjected to
Sephadex LH-20 (120 g) eluted with MeOH, followed by CC over
RP-18 (80 g, 20% CH3CN, 1 L) to afford 4 fractions: Fr.1−4. Fr.1
(36 mg) and Fr.2 (64 mg) were individually purified by semi-
preparative HPLC eluted with 23% CH3CN to afford 7 (tR = 9.2 min,
4 mg) and 8 (tR = 10.4 min, 5.5 mg). Fr.3 (18 mg) was purified by
semipreparative HPLC eluted with 30% CH3CN to afford 6 (tR = 9.0
min, 5 mg). Fr.4 (24 mg) was purified by semipreparative HPLC
eluted with 30% CH3CN to afford 1 (tR = 12.7 min, 7 mg).
