       20   Enzymes are the molecular machines of life, and a key property that governs their function is
       21    substrate specificity—the ability of an enzyme to recognize and selectively act on particular
       22    substrates. This specificity originates from the three-dimensional (3D) structure of the enzyme active
       23     site and complicated transition state of the reaction1,2. Many enzymes can promiscuously catalyze
       24    reactions or act on substrates beyond those for which they were originally evolved1,3-5. However,
       25    millions of known enzymes  still lack reliable substrate specificity information, impeding their
       26    practical applications and comprehensive understanding of the biocatalytic diversity in nature.
       27    Herein, we developed a cross-attention-empowered SE(3)-equivariant graph neural network       28    architecture named EZSpecificity for predictingARTICLEenzyme substrate specificity, which was trained on
       29   a comprehensive tailor-made database of enzyme-substrate interactions at sequence and structural
       30    levels. EZSpecificity outperformed the existing machine learning models for enzyme substrate
       31    specificity prediction, as demonstrated by both an unknown substrate and enzyme database and
       32    seven proof-of-concept protein families. Experimental validation with eight halogenases and 78
       33    substrates revealed that EZSpecificity achieved a 91.7% accuracy in identifying the single potential
       34    reactive substrate, significantly higher than that of the state-of-the-art model ESP (58.3%).
       35    EZSpecificity represents a general machine learning model for accurate prediction of substrate
       36    specificity for enzymes related to fundamental and applied research in biology and medicine.
       37   Over the past few years, a number of machine learning tools have been developed to predict enzyme
       38    substrate  specificity,  but they met with  limited  success and mainly  concentrated on developing
       39    computational tools specific to particular protein families6-12. Popular enzyme function prediction tools such
       40    as CLEAN13, ProteInfer14, and DeepECTransformer15 struggle to distinguish enzyme reactivity and
       41    substrate specificity within same EC numbers, which is a central challenge for biocatalysis. Enzyme
       42    Substrate Prediction (ESP) ESP utilized graph neural networks (GNN) to encode various metabolites for
       43   numerous proteins 8. However, ESP faced challenges in covering all native/non-native enzyme-substrate
       44    pairs in a genome-scale metabolic model due to the limited number of collected substrates (~1.3k).
       45    Fingerprint- and sequence-based embeddings have since achieved more promising results8,13,16,17, though
       46   compound–protein  interaction (CPI) methods  still struggle  to accurately capture substrate–enzyme
       47    recognition 7. This reflects the importance of catalytic residue identity, spatial arrangement, and long-rangeACCELERATED       48    coupling in determining specificity18-20. Although the frameworks such as ESP, CPI, and ALDELE21

        4   To address the above-mentioned limitations, we developed a general deep learning model named
        5    EZSpecificity for enzyme specificity prediction, which is empowered by structural information and cross
        6    attention-assisted GNN. We  first  built a high-quality curated enzyme-substrate  interaction  dataset
        7    (ESIbank) encompassing a wide range of natural/non-natural substrates, substitutions and substrate-enzyme
        8   complex information at the sequence, structure, and interaction level. We then designed an SE(3)-
        9    equivariant GNN-based encoder to capture the micro-environment of each atom in the catalytic active site,
       10    followed by a cross-attention process aimed at empowering interactions between the substrates and the
       11   enzymes. To validate the versatility and accuracy of EZSpecificity, we extensively performed in silico
       12    experiments for various unknown enzymes and substrates combinations as shown for six typical protein
       13    families. Lastly, we challenged EZSpecificity to annotate substrate landscape for an in-house collected
       14    halogenase database (in total 386 enzymes, 449 substrates) followed by experimental validation.       15    Results and Discussion        PREVIEW
       16   The architecture of EZSpecificity
       17    Existing machine learning models for enzyme specificity prediction mainly concentrated on extracting
       18    features from one-dimensional protein sequences and two-dimensional molecular graphs, ignoring the 3D
       19    nature of the substrate binding process (Table S1). Additionally, these models reduced the substrate and
       20   enzyme to two separate embeddings before concatenation steps, which likely limited their ability to capture
       21   complex interactions between the enzyme and substrate22. To address these challenges, we developed a
       22   deep learning model named EZSpecificity that integrates sequence data, 3D enzyme-substrate complex
       23    structures, and active  site environment to predict enzyme-substrate specificity. The architecture of
       24    EZSpecificity is shown in Figure 1. The full-length amino acid representations produced by ESM-2, one
       25    of the most powerful self-supervised sequence representation encoders, was integrated into EZSpecificity       26     at the sequence level. Considering that theARTICLEsubstrate binding pocket is crucial for determining enzyme
       27    function, especially specificity, we represented the binding pocket as a graph G = (V, E) where each node
       28  V is an atom from either substrate or enzyme. These nodes are connected by edges in E to their k-nearest
       29   atom neighbor in the structure. To maintain the encoding process’s invariant to E(3) transformations,
       30   drawing inspiration from E(n)-Equivariant Graph Neural Networks (EGNN)23, the binding pocket is
       31   modeled using an SE(3)-equivariant GNN. Additionally, we kept the gate open for flexibly integrating other
       32    fingerprints at the atom, residue, or overall level into the framework. The above design allowed the model
       33    to incorporate essential features, thereby enhancing the generalizability of EZSpecificity. Importantly,
       34    EZSpecificity is designed to predict enzyme substrate specificity, but it does not currently support reliable
       35    prediction of chemo-, regio-, or stereoselectivity due to limitations in molecular representation and the
       36    resolution of the encoding strategy.
       37   To capture the complex enzyme-substrate interaction in the active site environment, two cross-attention
       38    layers were employed to generate the blended representation of the enzyme-substrate pair. The cross-
       39    attention layers can emphasize the amino acids and atoms crucial for enzyme-substrate specificity, unlike
       40    previous approaches that assigned equal weight to each. Our approach can minimize unnecessary noise and
       41   enhance the model’s focus, thereby improving  its generalizability. Overall, the cross-attention layer
       42   empowered the interactions between the enzyme’s amino acids and the substrate’s atoms, potentially
       43    capturing significant specificity-driving information. Finally, a multi-layer perceptron is used to predict the
       44    specificity based on the representation of the enzyme-substrate pair. Subsequently, EZSpecificity was
       45    trained using the ESIbank dataset, as described in the next section. The main input data included enzymeACCELERATED46    sequences, enzyme-substrate complexes from docking, and substrate SMILES. The model generates a
       47    prediction score that defines the enzyme specificity.

        2   Enzyme-substrate interaction database
        3  A high-quality training dataset plays a critical role in enhancing the performance of machine learning
        4    models. To train the EZSpecificity model, we sought to develop a comprehensive enzyme-substrate
        5    interaction database (ESIbank) by integrating the sequence-based databases (e.g., BRENDA, UniProt) with
        6    structural information generated by AlphaFold, AlphaFill and Autodock/Vina-GPU (Figure 2a, Figure S1-
        7    S2). After obtaining diverse enzyme sequences from BRENDA and UniProt, we also collected enzyme-
        8    substrate data for six representative enzyme families, including esterases24, glycotransferases25, nitrilases26,
        9    phosphatases27, thiolases6, and domain of unknown function proteins (DUFs)28. Knowledgebases like
       10    UniProt used expert curation progress and rule-based prediction systems to gather data from various
       11    sources29, while their update often lags year(s) behind the rate at which new experimental data is released.
       12   To capture crucial enzymatic reactions depicted in images but not yet indexed by public databases, we
       13    developed a semi-automatic data extraction process consisting of four steps: identification, extraction,
       14    translation, and connection (Figure 2a, upper panel). As proof of concept, a halogenase-substrate (HaloS)
       15    dataset containing ~3300 enzyme-substrate pairs was established using this process for later model       16    validation (Supplementary Text 1 in the Supplementary Materials). PREVIEW
       17    Next, to prepare the functional 3D structure for docking, we used cofactors sourced from BRENDA as
       18    indicators to identify cofactor-involved structures from the AlphaFill database. Additionally, for  all
       19    reactions with annotated catalytic residues in BRENDA, these catalytic residues were used to define the
       20    active site for the docking box, and the substrate structure was prepared from Simplified Molecular Input
       21    Line Entry System (SMILES) using RDKit, followed by the docking process using AutoDock-GPU
       22    (Figure 2a, lower panel). AutoDock-GPU, as a hardware-accelerated version of AutoDock, enabled to
       23    explore the molecular space as necessary, while safely avoiding redundant computations30,31. We applied
       24    distance scoring for those pairs with known active sites and affinity scoring to determine the most favorable
       25    binding pose over 100 runs, with each run allowing up to 2,500,000 score evaluations—totaling a potential
       26   maximum of 250 million pose evaluations per pair. In summary, ESIbank encompassed a wide range of
       27    natural/non-natural substrates (34,417), wild type/variant enzymes (8,124) from published online servers       28   and literature. The ESIbank database containsARTICLE25 times more substrates than the dataset available in ESP,
       29   which consists of 1,379 unique substrates8. In total, 323,783 enzyme-substrate pairs were compiled to
       30    generate ESIbank (Figure 2b, Text 2-3 in the Supplementary Materials), representing the first broad
       31    enzyme-substrate complex database at the structural level to date.
       32   In silico evaluation of EZSpecificity
       33   To validate EZSpecificity, we designed multiple benchmarking experiments to evaluate  its overall
       34    effectiveness in predicting enzyme specificity on full ESIbank (Figure 3). EZSpecificity was first compared
       35    with ESP, the state-of-the-art general machine learning model for predicting enzyme substrate specificity.
       36    Four-fold cross-validation was performed on the ESIbank dataset, calculating the mean performance across
       37     all folds. In addition to the conventional setting of randomly splits, we assessed our model under three
       38    additional conditions: unknown substrate, unknown enzyme, and unknown enzyme & substrate. For
       39    example, the unknown substrate scenario includes exclusive substrates not found in the other folds. These
       40    conditions were designed to mimic various real-world scenarios. Notably, EZSpecificity consistently
       41    outperformed ESP across all four scenarios (Figure 3a, Figure S3). As the difficulty increased with the
       42    use of unknown substrates and/or enzymes, the improvement of EZSpecificity or EZSpecificity-w/oGCS
       43    over ESP appeared to slightly diminish. However, it showed particularly impressive performance in the
       44   most challenging scenario, unknown enzyme & substrate (AUROC (EZSpecificity): 0.7198 and AUROC
       45    (ESP): 0.6523; AUROC: Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve), indicating the
       46   improved reliability for practical applications. Moreover, an 8.72% improvement in the AUPR score was
       47    observed (Figure S4).ACCELERATED

       13   As shown in Figure 3b, the ablation experiment showed loss of active structure decreased AUROC score
       14   from 0.7198 to 0.7036 demonstrating the benefits of explicitly modeling atomic interactions between the
       15   enzyme’s binding pocket and substrates. Similarly, loss of cross-attention layers decreased the AUROC
       16    score from 0.7198 to 0.7021 indicating that cross-attention layers play “significant” roles in improving the              PREVIEW       17   model performance. While these numerical changes may seem small, in the context of computational
       18    modeling, particularly in deep learning, even marginal improvements are challenging to achieve and can
       19    reflect meaningful enhancements in predictive accuracy. Beyond the ablation experiment, we observed that
       20    structural features, along with graph and cross-attention mechanisms, played crucial roles in enhancing the
       21    performance of EZSpecificity, especially on the halogenase dataset, a high-quality curated dataset that is
       22    described in the following section. In contrast to previous methods that employed average pooling across
       23     all amino acids1,18,32, the cross-attention layers enhanced the model’s ability to focus on the atoms and/or
       24   amino acids that determined enzyme substrate specificity, thereby reducing the bias introduced by indirect
       25   atoms and/or amino acids. The ablation analysis, which evaluated the Area Under the Precision versus
       26    Recall Curve (AUPR) score, showed similar results (Figure S4).

       30   Experimental validation on halogenases
       31    Next, we sought to validate the prediction accuracy of EZSpecificity using halogenases, an understudied
       32   enzyme family with limited information on their substrate specificities. Halogenases were chosen for proof-
       33    of-concept due to their increased use in biocatalytic C-H functionalization and their excellent catalyst-
       34    controlled specificity and selectivity39. Generally, small molecules with halogen atom(s) produced by
       35    halogenases have promising bioactivity and physicochemical properties, thereby offering broad application
       36    in pharmaceutical and agrochemical fields40. By applying the semi-automatic data extraction method (Text
       37   1 in the Supplementary Materials), 386 sequences were identified covering all four types of halogenases

       26    Representative applications
       27   Because of its capability of predicting a score for a target enzyme-substrate pair, EZSpecificity can be used
       28     to: 1) rank all the potential substrates for a target enzyme based on substrate specificity; and 2) rank all the          ARTICLE
       29    enzyme(s) for a target substrate based on substrate specificity. As a case study, 34 metabolites and 860
       30   enzymes present in Escherichia coli were selected for investigation. After generating 29,240 = 34 × 860
       31    docking structures, we challenged EZSpecificity to identify the enzyme(s) that act on each target
       32    metabolite. As shown in Extended Data Figure 1a and Text 4, among the 34 metabolites, EZSpecificity
       33    successfully matched 10 metabolites (29.4%) with their corresponding enzymes within the top 5% of ranked
       34   enzymes as compared to using docking scores, which achieved (20.4%). When expanding the ranking
       35    threshold to the top 20%, the cumulative success rate increased to 50% (17 metabolites). As illustrated in
       36   Extended Data Figure 1b, a two-dimensional kernel density estimation (KDE) plot was generated to
       37   examine the relationship between the percentile rank of top predicted enzymes and their sequence similarity
       38    to enzymes in the training set. Analysis of percentile rank of top enzyme prediction revealed a peak density
       39     at approximately 10%, indicating that EZSpecificity demonstrates the highest confidence in selecting the
       40    correct reactive enzyme when it is ranked within the top 10% of candidates. Also, a notable density peak is
       41    observed in the lower-left region, suggesting that EZSpecificity performs well on similar sequences while
       42    the model maintains reasonable predictive accuracy even for enzymes with low sequence similarity to those
       43    encountered during training, demonstrating its capacity for generalization to novel sequences.
       44    In addition, EZSpecificity holds the potential for the study of biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) by linking
       45  BGC genes to their corresponding biosynthetic intermediates. This capability was validated using two
       46    representative BGCs: the clavulanic acid biosynthetic pathway41-43 and the albonoursin biosynthetic
       47    pathway44,45. EZSpecificity achieved up to 66.7% accuracy in identifying the correct target enzyme among
       48    the top three ranked candidates for each step in the biosynthetic pathway (Figure S53, Table S10-S11,ACCELERATED       49   Table S14-S15 and Text 5). Identifying gene–intermediate pairs within BGCs remains a major challenge

       11   Conclusions
       12    In conclusion, we developed a general deep learning model EZSpecificity for predicting enzyme substrate
       13    specificity, which can be used across diverse protein families and native/non-native substrates. This model
       14    introduces two innovations—an encoder for 3D binding complexes based on the ESIbank dataset and cross-
       15    attention layers, both of which are often overlooked in previous CPI studies—addressing limitations of              PREVIEW
       16    existing approaches that often overlook the 3D binding process and atomic enzyme-substrate interactions.
       17   The ESIbank complexes also provide a valuable community resource for studies of enzyme function and
       18    reactivity, although performance may be affected for pairs lacking active-site annotations. Despite this,
       19    EZSpecificity demonstrated strong performance, up to 48.1% higher AUROC than ESP in silico 91.7%
       20    accuracy versus 58.3% for ESP in vitro across eight halogenases and 78 substrates. EZSpecificity thus
       21    represents a broadly applicable tool for biocatalysis47,48, synthetic biology49, enzyme engineering50,51, and
       22    drug discovery52,53, with future integration of dynamic binding information poised to further enhance
       23    predictive power.

        1   Methods

        2   ESIbank database preparation
        3   To build the ESIbank, we first collected existing native and/or non-native substrates from BRENDA54 in
        4   SMILES. However, in BRENDA, the specific enzyme sequences corresponding to substrates are mostly
        5    not directly listed. Instead, only the EC number and the organism associated with the enzymes are provided.
        6   So we randomly selected one enzyme from the listed category, comprising EC number and organism name,
        7    to match the substrate that was obtained from the UniProt29,55, resulting in approximately 180,000 positive
        8    enzyme-substrate pairs. To generate negative samples, for each positive pair, we created five negative
        9   enzymes and five negative substrates, totaling ten negative enzyme-substrate pairs per positive pair. These
       10    negative pairs vary in difference to the actual enzyme and substrate to help model cover all resolutions of
       11   enzyme substrate specificity prediction. The digit number within EC numbers was employed to define the
       12    levels of difference between positive and negative data. These levels ranged from no common digits in the
       13   EC number to all four digits being identical, encompassing five levels. As a result, using EC numbers as a
       14    guide, we randomly generated approximately 1,100,000 negative substrate-enzyme pairs across these five
       15    different levels, corresponding to the different digits of the EC number. Some abnormal or low-quality data              PREVIEW       16    points were removed, including substrates containing more than 280 atoms, enzymes containing more than
       17   1000 amino acids, and enzymes lacking information about active sites in the UniPprot. Moreover, additional
       18    data from six representative enzyme families, including  esterases24, glycotransferases25,  nitrilases26,
       19    phosphatases27, thiolases6, and domain of unknown function proteins (DUFs)28, were included to enrich
       20    ESIbank. In addition, ~3300 enzyme-substrate pairs from halogenases were extracted using a semi-
       21    automatic data extraction process consisting of four steps: Identification, Extraction, Translation, and
       22    Connection. The detailed process was described in Supplementary Text 1. In total, we collected 323,783
       23    high-quality enzyme-substrate pairs (Table S1 and S7).

       24   Construction of 3D enzyme-substrate complexes
       25   To make the ESIbank more informative, we generated the prediction of 3D enzyme-substrate complexes of
       26    each enzyme-substrate pair. Previous methods often employed a brute force approach to dock substrates          ARTICLE       27    directly to enzymes, resulting in inaccuracies due to the current docking software’s inability to precisely
       28    identify enzyme binding sites56. Instead, we sourced active site information from the Uniprot database and
       29    constrained the binding box to a 20 Å cube centered around the active site amino acids due to the size of
       30    the dataset. Additionally, inaccuracies stemming from the absence of cofactors in enzyme structures were
       31    addressed in our pipeline by integrating the cofactors into the enzyme structures using AlphaFill57. This
       32    ensures the catalytic enzyme-substrate pose can be generated in a local environment similar to that of the
       33    native binding pocket.
       34    Specifically, substrate structures were generated from SMILES strings utilizing Open Babel58 and RDKit
       35      59. The 3D structures of reactive enzymes, along with any required cofactors, were obtained from the
       36    AlphaFill database57. All apo enzyme models were sourced from the AlphaFold Protein Structure
       37    Database’s FTP archive60, following the procedures outlined in the AlphaFill documentation. After the
       38    preparation of all input enzyme and substrate files, docking was performed on all enzyme-substrate pairs
       39    in the ESIbank using AutoDock-GPU31,61. First, all enzyme structure files were converted to map files for
       40    input into AutoDock-GPU and substrate files converted to pdbqt inputs. Next, a list of all enzymes in the
       41    dataset with known active sites was generated. For all enzymes with known active sites, the center of mass
       42    of the active site was used as the center of the box. For all enzymes without a known active site, the center
       43    of the enzyme was used as box center. To leverage available active site information, a box cutoff radius of
       44   20 Å from the center of the active site was applied for enzymes with known active site coordinates. During
       45    the docking, the number of runs was set to 100 per enzyme-substrate pair, where each run consisted of a
       46   maximum of 2,500,000 score evaluations for a unique binding position for a total maximum of 250 millionACCELERATED       47    pose evaluations. After docking, the highest scoring pose was output as a structure file and final pose

        4    Utilization of a pre-trained protein language model to represent enzymes
        5  We employed ESM-28,13,64, a pre-trained transformer protein language model, to embed the protein amino
        6    acid sequences in ESIbank. ESM-2 is one of the most powerful self-supervised protein language models
        7   which contain 15 billion parameters, which was trained to reconstruct the portions of proteins that are
        8   randomly masked during training over protein sequences in the Uniref database. We used the output from
        9    the penultimate layer of the neural network as the amino acid embedding for enzymes. The embedding
       10    generated by ESM-2 is a 1280-dimensional vector representation for each amino acid. A linear layer with
       11    128-dimensional embedding was used for each amino acid sequence, which was reprojected from the
       12    original 1280-dimensional vector in ESM-2. We referred to it as the amino acid representation of the
       13   enzyme and the average of these as the representation of the enzyme.

       14   Capturing catalytic pocket environment by SE(3)-equivariant graph neural networks              PREVIEW

       16    environment using a SE(3)-equivariant graph neural network23. We represented the binding pocket as
       17    graphs G = (V, E) where each node V is an atom from either substrate or enzyme. These nodes are connected
       18   by edges in E to their k-nearest atom neighbor in the structure. In practice, we set k as 32. The features of
       19   enzyme atoms include chemical elements, amino acid types, and an indication of whether the atoms belong
       20    to the backbone. In the case of substrate atoms, their types are represented as multi-hot vectors, which
       21    include information about chemical element types and aromatic properties. The edge features 𝑒𝑒𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖 include

       25    vector to specify whether they connect atoms between substrates and enzymes.       26   To make the encoding process equivariantARTICLEwith respect to E(3) transformations, inspired by E(n)-

       22   Training process of EZSpecificity
       23  We formulated the task of predicting enzyme specificity as a binary classification problem, with the training          ARTICLE       24    objective being the classification of pairs of substrates and enzymes into two categories. Our model was
       25    trained using a cross-entropy loss function, and we employed the AdamW optimizer with default
       26    parameters67. The learning rate was set at 0.0003. To enhance training stability, we implemented a learning
       27    rate schedule. In the initial few epochs, we linearly increased the learning rate from 0.000006 to 0.0003 for
       28   warm-up purposes. If the model's performance did not improve for 10 consecutive epochs, we reduced the
       29    learning rate to half of its previous value. Training was concluded once the learning rate dropped below
       30    0.000006. We set the batch size as 32 for training. The SE(3) neural network comprised three graph neural
       31   network layers and the base predictor, MLP, consisted of three feedforward layers. Unless specified
       32    otherwise, the dimensions of the hidden embeddings were all set to 128.

        1   Expression and purification of experimentally validated halogenases
        2    All experimentally validated halogenases (PrnA, PyrH, AetA, BorH, ThaL, BrvH and PltM) were expressed
        3    as described elsewhere13. After enzyme expression induced by adding 0.5 mM isopropyl β-D-1-
        4    thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) and continuing the incubation at 30 °C for 18 hours, the cells were collected
        5   by centrifugation at 4 °C and 3600 × g for 15 minutes. The resulting cell pellets were resuspended in 20
        6  mL of buffer A, which contained 50 mM sodium phosphate (pH 8.0) and 10 mM imidazole. Sonication was
        7    performed for 10 minutes, alternating between 10 seconds on and 5 seconds off at 50% amplitude, to disrupt
        8    the cells. Cellular debris was then removed by centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 1 hour. Protein purification
        9   was carried out using a Protino Ni-IDA 2000 packed column as per the manufacturer’s instructions using
       10    buffer A and buffer B (50 mM sodium phosphate, 330 mM imidazole pH 8.0). To remove imidazole, a PD-
       11   10 desalting column (GE Healthcare, Germany). The purified enzymes were stored in small aliquots in
       12   sodium phosphate buffer (50 mM sodium phosphate, pH 8.0, 5% (v/v) glycerol) at -80 °C, ensuring that
       13    each aliquot was used only once post-thawing. The purity of the proteins was confirmed by SDS-PAGE,
       14   showing greater than 90% purity, and the protein concentration was quantified using the PierceTM BCA
       15    protein assay. To rule out the possibility of non-regiospecific or non-enzymatic halogenation caused by
       16   H₂O₂ or HOBr leakage, we supplemented the reaction with superoxide dismutase (SOD, 3 U) and catalase              PREVIEW       17    (3 U) as controls for comparison. These enzymes specifically eliminate reactive oxygen species, ensuring
       18    that any halogenation observed is due to the enzymatic activity, rather than uncontrolled chemical reactions
       19    driven by leaked oxidizing agents. As shown in Figure S29, S33, S35, S37 and S38 identical halogenation
       20    results were observed in both the presence and absence of SOD and catalase, confirming that the
       21    halogenation was indeed catalyzed enzymatically, rather than being the result of non-enzymatic reactions.
       22   High  throughput  enzymatic  assays  and  product  identification  with  high-resolution  mass
       23   spectrometry
       24   The enzymatic reactions were conducted in a 96-well plate with shaking at 900 rpm. 100 μL reactions
       25    consisting of 1 mM target substrate, 10 μM FAD, 1 mM NADP+, 10 mM sodium phosphite, 10 μM purified
       26   enzymes (PrnA, PyrH, AetA, BorH, ThaL, BrvH, Th-Hal and PltM), 5 μM SsuE, and PtdH were incubated
       27    for 24 h and 30 °C with and without NaBr in a buffer consisting of 50 mM sodium phosphate (pH 8.0). 100       28   μL aliquots were taken out and mixed withARTICLE50 μL ice cold 2% formic acid to terminate the reaction by
       29    precipitating the proteins. Precipitates were removed by centrifugation (15,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C) before
       30   5 μL aliquots were subjected to liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS). All LC-MS analyses
       31   were carried out on a Thermo Scientific LC-MS, using a Hypersil GOLD™ VANQUISH™ PFP UHPLC
       32   column (1.9 μm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm) at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min with the following elution system: solvent
       33  A (H2O supplemented with 0.1% formic acid) and B (acetonitrile supplemented with 0.1% formic acid) and
       34    specific linear-gradient for various substrates. ThermoFisher Scientific Q Exactive was equipped with an
       35   ESI source and Orbitrap mass analyzer. Calibration was performed with Pierce LTQ Velos ESI Positive
       36    Ion Calibration Solution (ThermoFisher). Xcalibur from ThermoFisher Scientific was used to control,
       37    acquire, and interrogate data from the LC-MS system and related instruments. The Full MS-SIM was
       38    operated with the following parameters: 70,000 (resolution), 50 to 750 m/z (scan range), T = 3e6 (AGC
       39     target), 200 ms (Maximum IT), and positive (polarity). Data analysis was then conducted using the
       40    Freestyle application within Xcalibur software. Product identification was based on retention time
       41    comparison and the detection of an increased molecular weight marked by bromine (Br).
