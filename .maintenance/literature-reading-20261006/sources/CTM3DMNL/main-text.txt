ABSTRACT: An atypical nonheme iron-dependent dioxygenase
BTG13 with a rare iron coordination of four histidine residues and
a carboxylated-lysine (Kcx) was recently reported to catalyze the
C4a−C10 bond cleavage of anthraquinone. However, the reaction
mechanism of BTG13 remains elusive. Herein, the detailed
mechanism of BTG13 is studied using molecular dynamics
simulations and density functional theory calculations. The
comprehensive mechanistic study shows that the most favorable
pathway for the C−C bond cleavage of anthraquinone involves
two unusual steps: (1) a hydrogen atom abstraction (HAA) from
an sp3-hybridized carbon of the substrate by FeIII−O2
•−and (2) an
oxygen rebound to the substrate radical via homolytic O−O bond
cleavage, which activates FeIII−OOH to form FeIVO species.
Furthermore, our results reveal that Kcx could increase the electron-donating ability of the ferrous iron, thereby boosting the
activation of dioxygen to form FeIII−O2
•−species and facilitating the following HAA and O−O bond cleavage processes. This study
advances the current knowledge of reactions catalyzed by iron-dependent oxygenases.
KEYWORDS: Anthraquinone, C−C bond cleavage, nonheme iron dioxygenase, carboxylated lysine, catalytic mechanism
1. INTRODUCTION
Anthraquinone is one of the key intermediates for the
biosynthesis of numerous dibenzo-γ-pyrone-containing xan-
thone natural products,1 such as revenelin, agnestin, shamix-
anthone, beticolin 1, neosartorin, and xantholipin (Figure 1a).
Their biosynthetic gene clusters (BGC) or biosynthetic genes
are ubiquitously found in bacteria,2 actinobacteria,3 fungi,4 and
plants,5 and the elucidation of their biosynthetic pathways has
been extensively investigated.2,6−9 However, the detailed
information on the enzymatic C4a−C10 bond cleavage of
anthraquinone involved in the biosynthesis of xanthone natural
products still remains elusive.4,6,9−12
In fungi, it was first proposed that cephalanone F is formed
from chrysophanol via an oxidative intermediate alternaphenol
B (Figure 1b).8,12−15 The reaction was suggested to be
catalyzed by a Baeyer−Villiger monooxygenase (BVMO) with
an oxidative oxygen atom insertion.14,16−18 However, recent
studies revealed that the formation of cephalanone F involves a
reductive intermediate chrysophanol hydroquinone (1a,
anthrone form), which is generated by an NADPH-dependent
reductase,10 and the following oxidative C−C single bond
cleavage is catalyzed by an atypical nonheme iron-dependent
dioxygenase BTG13 (Figure 1c and d).6
Interestingly, the crystallographic studies and biochemical
assays on BTG13 demonstrated that the iron ion in the
catalytic site of BTG13 is uncommonly coordinated by four
histidine residues and an atypical carboxylated lysine (Kcx;
Figures 2 and S1). Based on the crystal structure, a general
mechanism was proposed (Figure 1d).6 The reaction is
triggered by a proton transfer (PT) from C10 of 1a to residue
H53, and an electron transfer (ET) from the substrate to the
FeIII−O2
•−center, thus forming radical intermediate 4.
Subsequently, the coupling of the superoxo and intermediate
4 generates a dioxanthracene intermediate 6 via intermediate
5. Finally, the cleavage of four-membered intermediate 6
generates the desired product cephalanone F by a PT from
H53 to dioxanthracene. However, this mechanism was
proposed based on the previous studies of other iron-
dependent dioxygenases. Therefore, the aforementioned
reaction steps should be further evaluated. Furthermore,
previous experimental studies on emodin hydroquinone,
obtained by reducing one carbonyl group of anthraquinone,

So far, various mechanisms have been proposed for the
oxidative C−C and CC bond cleavage reaction by
dioxygenases on the basis of computational studies.34−52 For
instance, the intradiol or extradiol dioxygenase (BphC),35
homoprotocatechuate 2,3-dioxygenase (HPCD),37,38 2-amino-
phenol 1,6-dioxygenase (APD),34 and 2,5-dihydroxypyridine
dioxygenase (NicX),36 which are iron-dependent, were
proposed to break the CC double or aromatic bond of
the substrate via a direct attack of the iron-superoxo on the
substrate. There are also cofactor independent dioxygenases
that catalyze the ring cleavage or oxidation reactions, such as 1-
H-3-hydroxy-4-oxoquinaldine 2,4-dioxygenases (HOD),53 van-
comycin biosynthetic dioxygenase (DpgC),45 and urate
oxidase (UOX),54 for which the triplet oxygen molecule can
be activated by the substrate at the active site of the
enzymes.44,55,56 Compared with these iron-dependent dioxy-
genases, which usually employ a 2-His-1-Asp/Glu facial triad
or heme coordination structure, the iron center of BTG13 has
a unique coordination of the 4-His-1-Kcx structure. Moreover,

In this work, by using molecular dynamics (MD)
simulations and the density functional theory (DFT) method,
the detailed reaction mechanism of BTG13-catalyzed C4a−C10
bond cleavage of chrysophanol hydroquinone was investigated.
The most favorable substrate isomer for BTG13 and a novel
mechanism for nonheme dioxygenase catalyzed oxidative C−C
bond cleavage were revealed. Furthermore, the effect of the
unusual Kcx on the catalytic power of the iron center was also
studied. The results indicate that Kcx could increase the
electron-donating ability of the ferrous iron, thereby boosting
the catalytic power of the active center. Thus, our computa-
tional study provides a novel insight into the catalytic diversity
of iron-dependent oxygenases, which can help to design new
nonheme iron-dependent catalysts to catalyze the C−C bond
cleavage reaction.
2. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

S2), the most representative snapshot (Figure 2a) was used to
build a quantum chemical (QC) cluster model (Figure 2b and

Information (SI). The optimized BTG13/1a/O2 complex (E·

optimized geometry, H53 forms hydrogen-bonding interac-
tions with the substrate and H185 (Figure 2c). The hydrogen
bond between residue H53 and the substrate is important in
substrate binding by orienting the substrate at a correct
position that favors the subsequent oxidation of its C10 (Figure

2.1. Tautomerization of Substrate. Previous studies on
the emodin analogues demonstrated that these compounds can
be tautomerized between the anthrone form and the naphthol
form.10,19,20 We first investigated the substrate preference of

and 1b in Figure 3). The tautomerization reaction of the
substrate was explored either in the enzyme active site or in the

converted to a rate constant of 8 s−1. Therefore, our results
indicate that the tautomerization process is feasible. Together
with the fact that the BTG13/1b/O2 complex is 4.2 kcal/mol
higher in energy than that of the BTG13/1a/O2 complex

2.2. Reactive State of the Reactant. Mechanistic studies
on other nonheme monoiron dependent oxygenases have
revealed that the binding of the triplet dioxygen to the quintet
FeII can activate the dioxygen, leading to the formation of a
FeIII−O2
•−species, which can be in the singlet (open shell),
triplet, quintet, or septet state.35−37,62 To ascertain the reactive
form of the FeIII−O2
•−species in the BTG13-catalyzed
reaction, geometries and electronic structures of the BTG13/
1a/O2 complex ((E·S·O2)I) in different spin states were
studied (Figure 4).
As shown in Figure 4a and Figure S7, the septet state is the
ground state, and the energies of the quintet, triplet, and open-
shell singlet states are higher than that of the septet state by
1.0, 4.0, and 9.9 kcal/mol, respectively (Figure 4a and Table
S6). Moreover, the calculation results show that the spin
populations on Fe in 7(E·S·O2)I and 5(E·S·O2)I are 4.39 and
4.36, and the spin populations on O2 are 1.29 and −0.76,
respectively, indicating that the lowest-lying septet and quintet
state are generated from the ferromagnetic or antiferromag-
netic coupling between the FeIII center and O2 (Table S7).
These electronic structures are further confirmed by the
analysis of spin natural orbitals (SNO) (Figure 4b). The
electronic structures of the activated species in 7(E·S·O2)I and
5(E·S·O2)I are thus demonstrated to be the FeIII−O2
•−species,
which is consistent with the previous studies on other
nonheme iron-dependent dioxygenases.35−37,62 As previous
studies on the iron-peroxo indicate that the side-on iron-
dioxygen complex conformation at the septet ground state is a
potential favorable starting point of the reactant com-
plex,40,70−73 the side-on binding pattern was also investigated.
However, by using QC calculations, the side-on structure
could be obtained only at the septet state. The side-on mode
(7(E·S·O2)I-side-on, Table S6) is 0.7 kcal/mol higher than the
end-on mode (7(E·S·O2)I). Note that the distance between the
two oxygen atoms and the spin distributions in the 7(E·S·O2)I-
side-on mode show high similarity to that of the end-on mode
(Figure S7 and Table S7), which indicated that the side-on
complex is a FeIII−O2
•−species. The optimization for a side-on
oxygen binding mode in the quintet state in our cluster model
always leads to the formation of an end-on oxygen binding
mode. We further tested the dioxygen side-on binding mode
using quantum mechanics/molecular mechanics (QM/MM)
calculations. The side-on structure could now be located in
both the quintet and septet states. However, the end-on
structure in the quintet state, which is the reactive state for the
BTG13-catalyzed reaction, is 3.5 kcal/mol lower than that of
the side-on structure. The former mode is thus proven to be
more energetically favorable, which is consistent with the
findings in other dioxygenases.42,74−76 Therefore, in this work,
the end-on mode was considered as the starting point of the
catalytic reaction.

In path I-a (Scheme 1), the reaction is initiated by an HAA
from C10 of 1a to the FeIII−O2•−species, thus forming an
intermediate with Sub•−FeIII−OOH species (IM1I, Figure 4a
and c) via TS1I. The calculated energies of 5TS1I and 5IM1I
are 11.4 and −19.1 kcal/mol relative to the reactant (5(E·S·
O2)I), respectively. The SNO analysis on IM1I shows that the
HAA from C10 leads to an electron transfer from the C10−H σ
bond to the σz
2* orbital of the iron center (Figure S10a).
Interestingly, different from the low energy barrier of this HAA
process in BTG13 (12.4 kcal/mol relative to
7(E·S·O2)I),
previous studies on hydroxyethylphosphonate dioxygenase
(HEPD)43 and hydroxypropylphosphonic acid epoxidase
(HppE)79 showed that the corresponding HAA processes
have high energy barriers (more than 25 kcal/mol), which
indicates that the HAA process of BTG13 in this mechanism is
an unusual step to initiate the following reaction process. As
bond dissociation energy (BDE) shows a good linear
relationship with the energy barrier of HAA,23,40,80,81 we
compared the BDEs of various groups on the IM1I here. The
calculated BDE of OO−H in FeIII−OO−H species is 76.2
kcal/mol (Table S10), which is consistent with the reported
ferric-superoxo species.40,82 However, the BDE of the C10−H
in the substrate was calculated to be 60.4 kcal/mol which is
smaller than the reported C−H BDE value (∼73 kcal/mol).82
Analysis of the spin population and SNO indicates that the low
C10−H BDE value of 1a could be attributed to the strong
conjugation effect from the aromatic rings nearby C10, which
stabilized the formed radical intermediate. As shown in Figure
S10a, the generated β electron on the C10 of the substrate is
located in a π* orbital that spreads over the substrate aromatic
ring skeleton.
Then, the reaction is continued with the homolytic Oa−Ob
bond cleavage and the rebound of the distal oxygen (Ob) to
the C10 of the substrate radical, leading to the formation of
IM2I via IM1I (Figures 4a and S10a−c). From IM1I to IM2I,
the distance of Fe−Oa changes from 1.94 to 1.64 Å, indicating
that the bond order between Fe and Oa is enhanced (Figure
4c). Further analysis of spin populations and SNOs confirms
the formation of a high valent FeIVO species in IM2I (Figure
S10c, Table S7).
Next, as shown in Figure 5a, the generated FeIVO
abstracts a hydrogen atom from the hydroxyl group of the
substrate; this process has an energy barrier of 11.5 kcal/mol
(IM2I to TS3I), and generates an oxygen radical on the
substrate and a FeIII−OH species (IM3I; Scheme 1). The
electronic structure analysis for this process reveals that an
electron transfers from the Ob−H bond of the substrate to the
σ*z2 orbital of the FeIVO species (Figure S10c and d),
leaving a spin-down electron on the oxygen of the substrate
moiety (TS3I; Figure 5a and Table S9). These results indicate
that this step is a σ-channel mechanism.70 The spin
distributions of the generated IM3I on iron, Oa, and Ob are
4.35, 0.27, and −0.64, respectively (Table S9). During the
reaction, the spin density on the leaving hydrogen at TS3I is
only calculated to be 0.02, and the SNO orbital of the substrate
shows the composition of an FeO moiety σ-lobe and π-type
substrate orbitals at TS3I (Figure S10d). All of these features
indicate that this hydrogen abstraction is a proton-coupled
electron transfer (PCET) process, thus leading to a much
lower energy barrier than a HAA process for the hydrogen
abstraction from the O−H moiety, which is consistent with the
previous studies on other iron-dependent enzymes.83−86 In
IM3I, the formed single electron on Ob separates to the
aromatic ring skeleton, thus stabilizing the intermediate
(Figure S11). Then, the formed oxygen radical in IM3I
could attack either C4a or C10a (path I-a and path I-R,
respectively, Figure 5a), which finally leads to the generation of
product cephalanone F ((E·P)I‑a) or monodictyphenone ((E·
P)I‑R), respectively. As the hydrogen bond with the hydroxyl of
the FeIII−OH orients the oxygen radical at a position favorable
for attacking C4a in IM3I (Figure 5b), the calculated energy
barrier of the oxygen radical attacking C10a (TS4I‑R in Figure
5a) is 2.9 kcal/mol higher than that of attacking C4a (TS4I‑a in
Figure 5a). This is consistent with our previous experimental
observation that only cephalanone F was observed.6 Therefore,
we will here focus on path I-a, and path I-R will not be further
discussed.
The following C4a−C10 bond cleavage of IM4I‑a leads to the
formation of a hemiacetal radical intermediate (IM5I‑a), which
could be rapidly transformed to a lactone intermediate
alternaphenol B (IM6I‑a) via a PCET process with the
assistance of residue H53. Then, the nucleophilic attack by
the hydroxyl group of the FeII−OH species on the lactone
intermediate takes place, leading to the formation of IM7I‑a,
whose energy is 10.4 kcal/mol lower than that of IM6I‑a. This
step has a barrier of 4.2 kcal/mol (TS7I‑a relative to IM6I‑a).
Finally, the cleavage of the C10−Ob bond of IM7I‑a generates
the final product (E·P)I‑a. From IM5I‑a to IM6I‑a, H53 acts as a
general base that accepts a proton. Together with the
important role of H53 in substrate binding suggested by our
MD simulations, we conclude that the conserved H53 is vital
for the function of the BTG13-type nonheme iron-dependent
dioxygenases. This is well supported by our previous
experimental results that BTG13 lost its activity when the
H53 was mutated to other residues (such as Phe, Asp, Asn,
Glu, and Gln).6
The direct cleavage of the C10−Ob bond and proton transfer
from the hydroxyl group to the Ob will involve a transition
state with a four-membered ring geometry, associating with a
higher energy barrier of 23.8 kcal/mol (Figure S12). To avoid
this high barrier transition state, a possible energetically
feasible pathway was proposed as the proton transfer process,
which might be mediated by a water molecule. To verify this
proposal, we first performed MD simulations to see whether
water molecules could get into the active site. Indeed, a water
channel between the active site and the solvent environment
was observed (Figure S13), and water molecules could easily
get into the active site. Therefore, we calculated the water-
assisted C10−Ob bond cleavage pathway and found that, with
the help of water, the C10−Ob bond cleavage very easily takes
place with a barrier of only 2.2 kcal/mol (IM7I‑a′ to TS8I‑a′). In
all, the produced enzyme−product complex ((E·P)I‑a′, Figure
5a) has an energy of −85.3 kcal/mol relative to the BTG13/
1a/O2 complex (5(E·S·O2)I, Figure 4a).
As mentioned in the Computational Details section in the
SI, our QC calculations utilized a continuum solvation model
to represent the effect of the protein environment on the
reaction, which has been proven to be a robust methodology in
elucidating the reaction mechanism of iron-dependent
enzymes.86 To further support the QC results discussed
above, the most favorable reaction pathway was validated by
using QM/MM calculations with four snapshots (Figure S14).

Based on the results discussed above, it can be concluded
that BTG13 adopts the mechanism path I-a, which involves
two unusual steps (Scheme 1): the hydrogen atom abstraction
from the sp3-hybridized C10 by FeIII−O2
•−and the homolytic
O−O cleavage of FeIII−OOH via the rebound of the

forming the high valent FeIVO. As far as we know, these two
atypical processes have not been reported in other nonheme
iron-dependent dioxygenases. The calculated energy profiles
for the entire reaction (Figures 4a and 5a) reveal that the O−O
bond cleavage process is the rate-determining step with an
energy barrier of 19.1 kcal/mol (IM1I to TS2I; Figure 4a).
2.4. Alternative C−C Bond Cleavage Mechanisms of
Anthrone Form 1a. Next, a number of alternative
mechanisms were also investigated, and our calculations
showed that all of them have a higher barrier than path I-a.
However, the results are worth briefly discussing here for
comparison. At first, we investigated previously proposed
mechanism where the reaction is initiated by PT from the C10
of 1a to residue H53 (Figure 1d).6 The calculation results
show that this proton transfer has an energy barrier of as high
as 31.3 kcal/mol (Figure S17, Tables S11 and S12). Therefore,
it can be ruled out. Then, another reaction pathway that we
examined was the nucleophilic attack of FeIII−O2
•−on the C4a
of 1a. The calculated barrier is 22.6 kcal/mol (Figure S18),
which is also much higher than that of the first step in path I-a
(11.4 kcal/mol relative to 5(E·S·O2)I, Figure 4a). This makes it
an unfavorable mechanism. Analysis of the structure shows that
the high barrier is caused by the high steric hindrance between
the substrate and enzyme active site. Thus, this alternative
process is not preferred by BTG13 either.
Moreover, diverging from the intermediates involved in path
I-a, we also tested C−C bond cleavage mechanisms either via
the electrophilic attack of FeIVO on the C4C4a bond in
IM2I or direct C4a−C10 bond cleavage from IM3I (path I-b and
I-c in Scheme 1). However, all of these pathways give much
higher energy barriers (19.7 and 21.4 kcal/mol relative to
IM2I, for path I-b and I-c, respectively) than the calculated
hemiacetal radical intermediate pathway (TS5I‑a, 14.5 kcal/mol
relative to IM2I). Therefore, these pathways are not considered
energetically favorable pathways. The calculated energy
profiles, reaction processes, and optimized geometries are
summarized in Figures S19 and S20.

spin up). Thus, the following O−O bond cleavage and
hydroxyl rebound steps (IM1I to IM2I in Scheme 1) would be
spin-forbidden. Consequently, the pathway in the septet state
has a higher energy than that at the quintet state; thus, the
septet state is not considered the active state but is only taken
as the ground state in the following discussion. Since the
second step has been shown to be rate-limiting as discussed
above, only the first two steps of the reaction were studied here
(Figures 4a and 5a). As shown in the calculated energy profiles
(Figure 7c), the energy barrier of the hydrogen atom
abstraction process for the KcxO−model is 13.2 kcal/mol
relative to the septet ground state. However, for the LysN
model, the calculated barrier is 23.6 kcal/mol, which is much
larger than that for the KcxO−model. These results suggest
that the carboxylation modification on the lysine is essential for
the catalytic activity of BTG13.
Previous studies revealed that the electron-donating ability
of the axial ligand has strong correlation between the energy
barrier of the O−OH bond cleavage process and the activity of
the FeIVO complex in P450s or biomimics.22,23,78 We thus
here evaluated the electron-donating ability of the Kcx−and
Lys ligands by calculating the ionization potentials (IP) of their
one electron missing pair (Kcx•, Lys•+), which has a negative
relationship with the electron-donating ability. Our results
reveal that Kcx−has a much lower IP value than Lys (Figure 7d
and Table S17), indicating a stronger electron-donating ability.
Furthermore, the electron-donating ability can also be
quantified by the difference in the charge transfer from the
ligand to the iron center (ΔqCT).78 As shown in Figure 7a,b
and Table S18, the ΔqCT in the KcxO−model is much larger
than that in the LysN−complex. Therefore, it can be
concluded that the carboxylation of the axial Lys ligand alters
the coordinated ligand from a poor electron donor to a
stronger one, decreasing the quintet−septet energy gap and
consequently making the quintet state more available along the
reaction.
To evaluate the influence of other ligands with different
electron donating abilities on the activity of the four-histidine/
iron center, BTG13/1a/O2 complex models with aspartic acid,
serine, cysteine, histidine, and glutamic acid as the axial ligand
were built, which are denoted as AspO−, SerO−, CysS−, HisN,
and GluO−, respectively (Figure S23). Indeed, the calculated
IPs of the axial ligands have a good linear correlation with the
quintet−septet state energy gaps (ΔEQ‑S, Figure 7d). When
using ligands with lower IP (SerO−, CysS−, KcxO−, AspO−,
and GluO−), the reactants have smaller quintet−septet state
energy gaps (Figure 7e and Table S15). In contrast, in LysN
and HisN models with larger IPs, larger quintet−septet state
energy gaps were observed (11.0 and 9.2 kcal/mol for LysN
and HisN, respectively), which consequently caused much
higher barriers for the HAA process in two systems (23.6 and
21.3 kcal/mol for LysN and HisN, respectively; Figure 7c).
Moreover, as mentioned above, the electron-donating ability of
the iron-coordinated ligand is negatively correlated to the IP.
According to the calculations, SerO−, CysS−, KcxO−, AspO−,
and GluO−display stronger electron-donating abilities than
LysN and HisN (Figures 7 and S23) and can activate the
dioxygen to the FeIII−O2
•−species (Table S16). The poor
electron-donating histidine ligand (HisN) displays features
very similar to those of LysN discussed in detail above, in
terms of the triplet state feature for the dioxygen and the high
barrier for the HAA step (Figure 7c and Table S16).
Note that it is interesting that previous studies proposed that
the charge maintenance is an important factor that affects the
activity of the nonheme iron oxygenases.73,89,93−95 In our
calculations, with the coordination of Kcx, the overall charge of
the iron center of BTG13 maintains a +1 charge throughout
the catalysis. As shown in Table S16, from the reactant
complex (7(E·S·O2)I and
5(E·S·O2)I) to IM1I, the spin
distribution in the Kcx ligand coordination complex is similar
to that of the carboxylate (mimics of Asp or Glu residue)
ligand. While during the O−O bond homolytic cleavage
process (5TS2I), the system with the Kcx ligand displays a
lower barrier than that with Asp or Glu because of the much
higher electron-donating ability, which can be characterized by
the difference of the spin density on iron in 5TS2I (4.09 with
Kcx vs 3.98 and 3.96 for Asp and Glu, respectively). These
results indicate that the Kcx ligand provides more charge
compensation to the iron.
According to the calculation results discussed above, it can
be concluded that the carboxylation of lysine promotes
electron transfer from the iron center to the dioxygen to
form the FeIII−O2
•−species, which is crucial for the following
HAA process. Furthermore, the carboxylated lysine also
facilitates the following O−O bond cleavage of the FeIII−
OOH species. Thus, our study reveals that the carboxylation of
lysine could significantly increase the electron-donating ability
of the axial ligand and consequently enhance the catalytic
activity of the iron center in BTG13.
3. CONCLUSIONS
In this study, the reaction mechanism of C−C bond cleavage
of anthraquinone catalyzed by the atypical nonheme iron-
dependent dioxygenase BTG13 was systematically investigated
by quantum chemical calculations. On the basis of the
calculations, the most plausible catalytic mechanism of
BTG13 for the oxidative C−C bond cleavage of anthraquinone
involves two unusual steps: (1) the hydrogen atom abstraction
from the sp3-hybridized carbon by the FeIII−O2
•−radical

anion, which has a low energy barrier due to the strong
conjugation effect of the substrate, and (2) a rebound of the
distal oxygen of FeIII−OOH to the substrate radical via
homolytic O−O bond cleavage, which activates FeIII−OOH to
form FeIVO species. These steps have not been reported in
other nonheme iron-dependent dioxygenases. Moreover, the
carboxylated lysine is shown to promote electron transfer from
FeII to dioxygen and provide charge compensation to the iron
during the catalytic cycle, making it easier for dioxygen to be
activated to form FeIII−O2
•−species, thereby facilitating the
following HAA and the O−O bond cleavage process. Thus, it
can be concluded that the unique 4-His-1-Kcx coordinated
structure prevents the coordination of the substrate with an
iron center, and Kcx provides fine-tuning charge maintenance
of the iron center. We believe these findings provide not only
new insights into the understanding of the catalytic mechanism
of nonheme iron-dependent dioxygenases but also new
concepts to design nonheme biomimic catalysts to catalyze
the C−C bond cleavage reaction.
