ABSTRACT: Subplenones A−J (1−10), 10 new xanthone dimers, have been
isolated and characterized from the endophytic fungus Subplenodomus sp.
CPCC 401465, which resides within the Chinese medicinal plant Gentiana
straminea. The isolation process was guided by antibacterial assays and
molecular-networking-based analyses. The chemical structures of these
compounds were elucidated through the interpretation of nuclear magnetic
resonance (NMR) and high-resolution electrospray ionization mass spectrom-
etry (HRESIMS) data. Furthermore, the relative configuration of the
compounds was determined using NMR and single-crystal X-ray diffraction
analyses, and the absolute configuration was established using electronic
circular dichroism calculations. All of the isolated compounds exhibited
significant inhibitory activity against Gram-positive bacteria. Notably,
compounds 1, 5, and 7 displayed remarkable inhibitory activity against
methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) ATCC 700698, with a minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of 0.25 μg/mL,
and against vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus faecium (VRE) ATCC 700221, with MIC values ranging from 0.5 to 1.0 μg/mL.
T
hroughout history, natural products have consistently
served as a valuable reservoir of compounds in drug
discovery. In fact, between 1981 and 2019, approximately 55%
of antibacterial agents originated from natural products and
their derivatives.1 Despite this, the emergence and global
spread of drug resistance among pathogenic bacteria have
significantly hindered the pace of drug discovery over the past
three decades.2 As a result, there has been a growing urgency
to tap into rare resources found in unique habitats3 and to
develop efficient techniques that enable rapid exploration of
novel natural products.4
Our laboratory is dedicated to investigating unique environ-
mental microorganisms found in three polar regions of the
Earth (Antarctic, Arctic, and the Tibetan plateau)5 and plant
endophytes.6 We have collected thousands of these micro-
organisms, which have now been deposited in the China
Pharmaceutical Culture Collection (CPCC). In our laboratory,
we conducted activity screening of the fermented extract
library that we have constructed. The results showed that the
crude extract derived from rice cultures of Subplenodomus sp.
CPCC 401465, an endophytic fungus obtained from a Chinese
medicinal herb (Gentiana straminea Maxim.) in the Tibet
Autonomous Region of China, exhibited potent antibacterial
activity against Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 29213. To date,
Subplenodomus sp. has not been thoroughly studied chemically,
and we anticipate that studies of this genus will have a high
probability of yielding unprecedented bioactive compounds.
To profile the active metabolites comprehensively, we
adopted a strategy incorporating both antibacterial assays
and MS/MS molecular networking. This strategy has been
proven effective as an alternative tool for structure-based
discovery of unknown natural compounds by mining MS/MS
fragmentation similarity.7 Our analyses revealed a prominent
cluster, composed of 27 compounds (cluster II in Figure 1b),
that exhibited the highest potential for bioactivity.
Further comparison with MS and ultraviolet (UV)
absorption data from the Dictionary of Natural Products
indicated that the compounds within cluster II were likely
xanthone derivatives. Xanthones are a class of polyketides
commonly found in fungi, known for their diverse structures8
and broad spectrum of activities.9−11 Notably, the targeted
precursor ions illustrated in Figure 1 were of particular interest
for molecular-network-based dereplication due to the absence
of library matches in the existing literature on xanthones or in
available libraries on the Global Natural Products Social
Molecular Networking (GNPS) platform (Figure 1).

■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
The biological assays were conducted to evaluate the
antibacterial activity of the methanol extract (the crude
extract) of rice cultures as well as its ethyl acetate (EtOAc)
and n-butanol (n-BuOH) fractions against both Gram-positive
and Gram-negative bacteria. It was observed that the EtOAc
fraction showed stronger antibacterial activity against Gram-
positive strain Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 29213 compared to
the crude extract, whereas the n-BuOH fraction did not show
any activity. These results indicated that the constituents
responsible for the antibacterial activity were enriched in the
EtOAc fraction and may have been present in a lower
abundance or masked by other components in the crude
extract. To further prioritize the identification of new active
components, MS/MS data from the crude extract and EtOAc
fraction were visualized as two groups by molecular networking
generated via GNPS (https://gnps.ucsd.edu) (Figure 1).
The molecular network analysis revealed the presence of 20
clusters (node≥2), among which cluster II was particularly
significant (Figure 1b). This significance stemmed from the
enrichment of the components in the EtOAc extract. By
contrast, cluster I, which contained mostly nodes that were
absent from the EtOAc extract, was excluded from further
analysis. By comparing the spectral data with known
compounds in the Dictionary of Natural Products and the
GNPS libraries, we identified four labeled nodes, denoted in
Figure 1 with circular borders. These four nodes gave MS/MS
library matches to xanthone derivatives 1-hydroxy-10-
methoxydibenz[b,e]oxepin-6,11-dione,13 calyxanthone,14
monodictysin A,15 and phomalevone A12c (see Figure 1b).
After subsequent isolation and purification we were able to
confirm the identity of calyxanthone (12, m/z 287.1) and
identified the other compounds as alternaphenol B (11, m/z
271.9),16 penicixanthone D (13 m/z 295.5),17 and an as yet
unknown xanthone (m/z 547.1) (Figures S92−S97).
Based on these results, we predicted that the remaining
signals at m/z > 500 Da in cluster II corresponded to potential
new dimeric xanthones. Among these signals, the ions

Compound 1 was obtained as a yellow crystal, and its
molecular formula was established as C30H24O10 based on
HRESIMS data [m/z 543.12964 [M −H]−(calcd for
C30H23O10, 543.12857)], which indicated the presence of 20
double-bond equivalents. The 1H NMR spectrum (Table 1)
revealed signals corresponding to four hydroxy protons at δH
13.76 (1H, s), 13.17 (1H, s), 12.05 (1H, s), and 11.54 (1H, s),
four aromatic protons at δH 6.52 (1H, d, J = 8.5 Hz), 6.65 (1H,
d, J = 8.5 Hz), 7.40 (1H, d, J = 8.5 Hz), and 7.52 (1H, d, J =
8.5 Hz), two olefinic protons at δH 6.00 (1H, d, J = 1.6 Hz)
and 6.79 (1H, d, J = 1.6 Hz), one deshielded methine proton
at δH 4.00 (1H, s), and four methyl groups at δH 1.50 (3H, s),
1.61 (3H, s), 2.05 (3H, d, J = 1.6 Hz), and 2.18 (3H, d, J = 1.6
Hz). The 13C NMR spectrum exhibited 30 carbon signals.
Based on the analysis of the 13C NMR and DEPT spectra, the
carbon signals were assigned as three carbonyl carbons at δC
196.0, 186.4, and 186.3, 12 aromatic carbons at δC 161.8,
159.3, 157.1, 154.6, 141.0, 139.9, 117.8, 117.4, 110.2, 108.1,
107.2, and 106.8, eight olefinic carbons at δC 168.7, 163.2,
151.9, 143.1, 134.2, 121.2, 109.7, and 101.6, three oxygenated
carbons at δC 80.6, 80.5, and 73.7, and four methyl carbons at
δC 26.6, 23.7, 23.1, and 16.2. The 1D NMR spectrum also
indicated that this compound is an unsymmetrical dimer.
The planar structure of 1 was further elucidated through
HMBC and
1H−1H COSY correlations (Figure 2). The
HMBC correlations of H-3 (δH 7.40) with C-1(δC 159.3) and
C-4a (δC 157.1) and of H-4 (δH 6.52) with C-2 (δC 117.8) and
C-9a (δC 106.8), together with the 1H−1H COSY correlation
of H-3 (δH 7.40)/H-4 (δH 6.52), suggested the presence of an
AB spin system as shown in ring A. Additionally, according to
analysis of HMBC and 1H−1H COSY correlations, ring A′ was
identified as a tetrasubstituted phenol, similar to ring A.
Furthermore, the HMBC correlations of H3-11 (δH 1.50) with
C-5 (δC 73.7), C-8a (δC 101.6), and C-10a (δC 80.5), H3-12
(δH 2.18) with C-5 (δC 73.7), C-6 (δC 151.9), and C-7 (δC
121.2), and the proton of 8-OH (δH 13.76) with C-7 (δC
121.2, C-8 (δC 168.7), and C-8a (δC 101.6) provided evidence
for the presence of the dihydrobenzene ring, identified as ring
C. The only difference between ring C and ring C′ was the
substitution pattern at C-5 and C-5′: one with a hydroxy
group, the other with a carbonyl group. This distinction was
supported by the correlations of H3-11′ (δH 1.61) and H3-12′
(δH 2.05) with C-5′ (δC 196.0). The remaining portions, ring B
and ring B′ of monomeric units I and II, were identified
through the analysis of the HRESIMS data and the HMBC
correlations of H-4 (δH 6.52) and H-5 (δH 4.00) with C-9 (δC
186.4) and of H-2′ (δH 6.65) and H-7′ (δH 6.79) with C-9′ (δC
186.3). Moreover, units I and II were linked together via a
bond between C-2 and C-4′, as confirmed by the HMBC
correlations of 1-OH (δH 12.05) with C-1 (δC 159.3), C-2 (δC
117.8), and C-9a (δC 106.8), 1′-OH (δH 11.54) with C-1′ (δC
161.8), C-2′ (δC 110.2), and C-9a′ (δC 107.2), H-3 (δH 7.40)
with C-4′ (δC 117.4), and H-3′ (δH 7.52) with C-2 (δC 117.8).

absolute configuration as aR, 5S, 10aR, 10a′R. Compound 1

Compound 2 was obtained as a yellow solid, and its
molecular formula was established as C30H24O10 based on the
analysis of HRESIMS data [m/z 543.12946 [M −H]−(calcd
for C30H23O10, 543.12857)]. The

(Table 1) were found to be similar to those of compound 1.
Further analysis of 2D NMR data, including HSQC, HMBC,
and 1H−1H COSY confirmed that 1 and 2 shared identical
planar structures.
Both 1 and 2 exhibited a NOESY correlation (Figure 4)
between H-5/H3-11, indicating the presence of a cis
configuration for these protons in compound 2. However,
notable differences were observed in their ECD spectra, as
shown in Figure 5, suggesting that they were not enantiomers.

epimers at C-10a′. Furthermore, the NOESY correlation
between H-3 and H3-11′ indicated that 1 and 2 were a pair
of atropisomers. Based on the above analyses, the most
probable relative configuration of 2 was speculated to be aS*,
5S*, 10aR*, 10a′S*. Ultimately, by comparison of experimental
and calculated ECD spectra, the absolute configuration of 2
was determined as aS, 5S, 10aR, 10a′S (Figure 5). Compound
2 was named subplenone B.

Compound 3 was obtained as a yellow crystal, and its
molecular formula was established as C30H26O10 based on the
analysis of HRESIMS data [m/z 545.14465 [M −H]−(calcd
for C30H25O10, 545.14422)]. The
1H and
13C NMR data
(Table 1) suggested that 3 was a symmetrical homodimer. The
1H and 13C NMR and HMQC data revealed the presence of 15
carbon signals in this compound, including one carbonyl at δC
185.2, seven non-hydrogenated sp2 carbons at δC 169.4, 158.3,
157.4, 156.2, 116.9, 105.7, and 101.7, three sp2 methines (δC/
δH 139.7/7.41, 119.0/6.02, and 107.6/6.49), one oxygen-

23.5/1.39 and 22.2/2.11). Compound 3 exhibited 1D NMR
data that closely resembled phomalevone A,12c making it
impossible to differentiate between the two compounds based
solely on the analysis of their 1D NMR data.
Further analysis of 2D NMR data (Figure 2) revealed a
1H−1H COSY correlation of H-3 (δH 7.41)/H-4 (δH 6.49),
indicating the presence of an AB spin system. This spin system
was verified by the HMBC correlations of H-3 (δH 7.41) with
C-1 (δC 158.3) and C-4a (δC 157.4) and H-4 (δH 6.49) with
C-2 (δC 116.9) and C-9a (δC 105.7), as shown in ring A. In
addition, the HMBC correlations of H3-11 (δH 1.39) with C-5
(δC 72.1), C-8a (δC 101.7), and C-10a (δC 80.0), H3-12 (δH
2.11) with C-5 (δC 72.1), C-6 (δC 156.2), and C-7 (δC 119.0),
and H-7 (δH 6.02) with C-8a (δC 101.7) revealed the presence
of a different structure of ring C compared to phomalevone A.
Specifically, ring C was identified as 5,8-dihydroxy-6-methyl-
dihydrobenzene. In the remaining parts of the monomeric unit,
ring B was determined based on the weak HMBC correlations
of H-4 (δH 6.49) and H-5 (δH 3.88) with C-9 (δC 185.2).
Moreover, the HMBC correlations from 1-OH (δH 11.99) to
C-1 (δC 158.3), C-2 (δC 116.9), and C-9a (δC 105.7) revealed
that the connection between the two subunits occurred at C-2
and C-2′.
The relative configuration of compound 3 was elucidated as
5S*, 10aR*, 5′S*, and 10a′R*, through X-ray diffraction
analysis using Mo Kα radiation (Figure 6). (Single crystals
were obtained in dichloromethane.) This configuration was
consistent with the NOESY correlation (Figure 4) observed
between H-5 and H3-11, indicating their cis-relationship. This
configuration was attributed to the dominant conformation of
ring C, which positioned the 11-CH3 group in a pseudoaxial
orientation. The absolute configuration of compound 3 was
determined as 5S, 10aR, 5′S, and 10a′R by comparing the
calculated and experimental ECD spectra (Figure 7).
Compound 3 was named subplenone C.

Subplenones C−J (3−10) possessed 2−2′ linkages of two
slightly different homochiral monomeric units in analogy with
secalonic acid A and dicerandrol B,19a,b which were known to
allow interconversions between the aR and aS helicity forms at
room temperature. The rotational barrier around the C-2−C-
2′ bond, which had 1- and 1′ -hydroxy groups at the ortho-
positions, was approximately 5 kcal/mol,19a,b lower than the
energy barriers (20 kcal/mol)19c that would result in steric
hindrance and interrupt free rotation. Therefore, the aR and aS
conformers could freely equilibrate at room temperature. The
relative populations of atropisomer properties shown in
Figures S98−S107 in the SI were responsible for the observed
experimental ECD spectra.
The antibacterial activity of 1−10 was evaluated against both
sensitive and multidrug-resistant Gram-positive pathogenic
bacteria (Table 4). Compounds 1−2, 4−8, and 10 exhibited
significant inhibitory activity against S. aureus ATCC 700698
(MRSA) and E. faecium ATCC 700221 (VRE) with MIC
values ranging from 0.25 to 0.5 μg/mL and from 0.5 to 2.0 μg/
mL, respectively. Notably, compounds 1, 4, 5, 7, 8, and 10
showed excellent activity against S. epidermidis ATCC 12228
with MIC values lower than 0.125 μg/mL, surpassing the
positive control levofloxacin.
Recent studies have highlighted the potential of endophytic
fungi residing within higher plants as promising sources of new
antibacterial agents.20 MS/MS molecular networking has
proven to be a useful approach in rapidly identifying potential
active products from endophytes, especially in cases where
such investigation has not been previously conducted.21
This study employed a combination of molecular network-
ing-assisted, bioassay-directed purification strategy and the
utilization of public spectra libraries to discover bioactive
compounds. Consequently, these multiple approaches led to
the isolation of 10 new dimeric xanthones, subplenones A−J
(1−10), which exhibited remarkable activities against Gram-
positive bacteria.

using Gaussian 09. At first, all conformers were optimized at PM6.

General Antimicrobial Assays. Antimicrobial assays were
performed following the guidelines outlined by the Clinical and
Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) for Antimicrobial Suscepti-
bility Testing Standards. The strains used included S. aureus ATCC
29213, methicillin-resistant S. aureus ATCC 43300 and 700698, E.
faecalis ATCC 29212, vancomycin-resistant E. faecalis, ATCC 51299,
vancomycin-resistant E. faecium ATCC 700221, and S. epidermidis
ATCC 12228. For each organism, a loopful of glycerol stock was
streaked onto an LB-agar plate, which was then incubated overnight at
37 °C. A single bacterial colony was selected and suspended in cation-
adjusted Mueller-Hinton broth (CAMHB) to achieve an approximate
concentration of 5 × 106 CFU/mL. Each compound (2560 μg/mL in
DMSO) was diluted to 256 μg/mL using CAMHB broth. An aliquot
of each compound dilution (100 μL) was added to a 96-well flat-
bottomed microtiter plate (Greiner). Serial 2-fold dilution of each
compound was prepared using CAMHB broth, resulting in final
compound concentrations ranging from 128 to 0.125 μg/mL.
Levofloxacin served as the positive control, while DMSO was used
as the negative control. Subsequently, an aliquot (10 μL) of bacterial
suspension was added to each well, and the plate was incubated
aerobically at 37 °C for 16−20 h. The MIC was defined as the
minimum concentration of each compound that visibly inhibited the
bacterial growth. All experiments were performed in triplicate.
