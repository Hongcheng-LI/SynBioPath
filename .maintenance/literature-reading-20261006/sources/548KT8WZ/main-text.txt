Genome Mining of Cinnamoyl-Containing Nonribosomal Peptide
Gene Clusters Directs the Production of Malacinnamycin

ABSTRACT: Cinnamoyl-containing nonribosomal peptides
(CCNPs) constitute a unique family of actinobacterial secondary
metabolites that display a broad spectrum of biological activities.
Here, we present a genome mining approach targeting cyclase and
is isomerase to discover new CCNPs, which led to the
identification of 207 putative CCNP gene clusters from public
bacterial genome databases. After strain prioritization, a novel class
of CCNP-type glycopeptides named malacinnamycin was identi-
fied. A plausible biosynthetic pathway for malacinnamycin was
deduced by bioinformatics analysis.
T
he cinnamoyl-containing nonribosomal peptides
(CCNPs) have attracted much attention in recent years
for their unique structures and potent biological activities. The
common structural feature of these NRPs is the presence of a
cinnamoyl acyl unit modification at their N-terminus. This
end-cap is believed to play a significant role in protecting the
N-terminus from degradation, regulating the polarity, and
confering physiological functions. WS9326A, isolated from
Streptomyces violaceusniger no. 9326, was the first member
reported in this family, and it has been reported to exhibit
tachykinin antagonist activity.1−3 Pepticinnamin was then
isolated from a Streptomyces strain and found to exhibit potent
inhibitory activity against farnesyl-protein transferase.4 Sky-
llamycin isolated from Streptomyces sp. KY11784 showed
activity in inhibiting the platelet-derived growth factor
(PDGF) signaling pathway. It was also found to function as
biofilm inhibitors against Pseudomonas aeruginosa.5−8 Since
then, a series of structurally diverse CCNPs including
eudistamide,9 NC-1,10 atratumycin,11 atrovimycin,12 cinnapep-
tin,13 nyuzenamide,14 mohangamide,15 and coprisamide have
been isolated from actinobacterial strains.16−19
CCNPs are biosynthesized through the combined actions of
type II polyketide synthases (PKSs) and nonribosomal peptide
synthetases (NRPSs). Specifically, the NRPS is responsible for
assembling the peptide portion, while a type II PKS system is
speculated to be responsible for the biosynthesis of the
cinnamoyl polyketide portion. Our previous study revealed
that the type II PKSs in cinnamoyl biosynthesis are
evolutionarily distinct from canonical type II PKS and fatty
acid synthases (FASs). Based on this, we carried out our
genome mining efforts targeting the unusual ketosynthase
(KS) and ketoreductase (KR) domains, which resulted in the
discovery of a series of CCNP-type biosynthetic gene clusters
(BGCs). We then successfully activated one novel glycopep-
tide BGC and characterized kitacinnamycin, which showed
activity in activating the STING signaling pathway.20 In our
further biosynthetic study on ktiacinnamycin, we disclosed that
the type II polyketide synthase initially synthesizes a trans-
polyene carbon chain.21 The isomerases convert one of the
trans double bonds into the cis-configuration. This is followed
by a 6π-electrocyclization and dehydrogenation catalyzed by a
complex of three small proteins, Kcn17, Kcn18, and Kcn19, to
form the key benzene ring in the cinnamoyl moiety. It is worth
noting that the homologues of Kcn17, Kcn18, and Kcn19 are
also present in other CCNPs, including ws9326A, petincinna-
mycin, cinnapeptin, and others, suggesting that these three
unique small proteins and isomerases for benzene ring
formation can serve as specific probes for discovering
additional CCNPs. In this work, we report the discovery of
207 gene clusters through a genome mining strategy targeting
both cyclases and isomerases from a public genomic database.
We successfully isolated and characterized a novel CCNP-type
glyocopeptide from Streptomyces malaysienesis NA03722.
The isomerase enzymes, Kcn15 and Kcn33, in the kcn BGC
belong to the 2-hydroxychromene-2-carboxylate (HCCA)

complexes, Kcn17 belongs to the DsrE/DsrF-like family
(PF02635), while Kcn18 and Kcn19 are proteins of unknown
families. Therefore, in order to obtain more CCNP-type
compounds, we first selected isomerases as probes and
searched for PF01323 in the UniProt database. This resulted
in a total of 15 138 hits, 12 730 of which were of bacterial
origin. After obtaining these entries, we then used the
neighborhood connectivity feature of EFI-EST to analyze
whether the 12 730 sequences had DsrE/DsrF-like family
proteins (PF02635) within ten genes upstream and down-
stream.22 The results showed that there were 291 entries
containing these two genes. Finally, we submitted these 291
gene clusters for analysis by AntiSMASH23 and selected those
containing NRPS as potential BGC candidates for encoding
CCNPs. After removing duplicates, we obtained a total of 207
potential CCNP gene clusters.

To further systematically analyze and prioritize these gene
clusters, we established a genome neighboring network
(GNN) using the 8200 protein sequences from the 207 gene
clusters,24 along with the proteins from the gene clusters of
known CCNPs such as kitacinnamycin, WS9326A, pepticinna-
min, and cinnapeptin (Scheme 1). When setting the E value to
10−10, we could effectively cluster proteins with different
functions such as all NRPSs and ketosynthase proteins (KSα
and KSβ). Notably, these 207 BGCs all contain homologous
proteins to Kcn17, Kcn18, and Kcn19 and other proteins
required for the biosynthesis of the cinnamoyl moiety,
indicating that these 207 clusters potentially have the capacity
to produce CCNPs. Importantly, besides kitacinnamycin, we
found six gene clusters that encode glycosyltransferases (GTs).
As six gene clusters exhibit a high degree of sequence
homology, we picked one strain, S. malayxienesis NA03722,
among them for further fermentation and product analysis.
S. malayxienesis NA03722 was cultivated in 10 different
media. After 7 days of fermentation, metabolites were analyzed
through HPLC. In the extract obtained from fermentation
using the F culture medium, two HPLC peaks appeared with
maximum UV absorption wavelengths at 270 and 300 nm,
which are characteristic UV absorption wavelengths for the
cinnamoyl group (Figure S3).16 After large-scale fermentation,
malacinnamycins A (1, 11.5 mg) and B (2, 9.6 mg) were
purified and characterized.
Malacinnamycin A (1) was purified as a pale yellow
amorphous powder. The molecular formula of 1 was deduced
from the molecular ion at m/z 1145.4845 [M + Na]+ in the
high-resolution electrospray ionization mass spectrometry
(HRESIMS) data as C50H74N8O21, indicating 32 degrees of
unsaturation. Initial analysis of 1D and 2D NMR data revealed
the presence of at least 7 exchangeable protons (δH 9.40−
7.73), 8 aromatic/olefinic protons (δH 5.51−7.98), 23 protons
bound to N/O-bearing carbons (δH 3.07−5.50), and 10 ester/
amide carbonyl carbons (δC 165.8−174.5), suggesting the
glycopeptidic nature of 1 (Table S2).
Further inspection of the 2D NMR spectra, including
COSY, HSQC, and HMBC analyses, allowed the assignment
of seven proteinogenic amino acids consisting of one Leu, one
Asp, one Gly, two Thr, and two Ser, which is consistent with
the predictions of the amino acids recognized by the
adenylation domains (A domains) in the NRPS within the
gene cluster. The HMBC correlations of these NHs with
carbonyl carbons link these amino acid residues in order. In
addition, H-19 in Thr-1 has an HMBC correlation with C-46
(δC 169.6) in Ser-7, which indicates that the hydroxyl group of
Thr-1 forms an ester bond with the carboxyl group of Ser-7 to
form a cyclic peptide.
We observed four mutually coupled hydrogen signals at δH
7.43 (H-9, d, J = 7.8 Hz), 7.35 (H-10, t, J = 7.8 Hz), 7.33 (H-
11, t, J = 7.8 Hz), and 7.51 (H-12, d, J = 7.8 Hz), which is a
typical coupling pattern signature for a 1,2-disubstituted
benzene ring. A long spin system from H-1 to H-7 and a
pair of trans-double bond signals at δH 7.98 (H-14, d, J = 15.5
Hz) and 7.03 (H-15, d, J = 15.5 Hz) were also identified from
the COSY spectrum. H-7 and H-14 were found to have
HMBC correlations with C-9 and C-12 on the benzene ring,
indicating that these two spin systems are attached to adjacent
positions on the benzene ring. In addition, H-14 and H-15
showed correlations with the amide carbonyl at δC 165.9 (C-
16), confirming the presence of the cinnamoyl moiety in 1.
The HMBC correlation of H-18 with C-16 indicated that the
cinnamoyl moiety was attached at the Thr-1, which is
consistent with the structures of previously reported CCNPs.21
In addition, a spin system from H-1′ (δH 4.50) to oxygen-
bearing methylene protons H6′ (δH 3.67) through H-2′ (δH
3.42), H-3′ (δH 3.38), H-4′ (δH 3.07), and H-5′ (δH 3.46) was
observed in the COSY spectrum. An N-acetyl group was
attached to C-2′, as evidenced from H-2′ to C-8′ as well as the
COSY correlation of H-2′ with NH-7′. This analysis indicated
the presence of a N-acetylglucosamine, which was attached to
C-7′ according to the HMBC correlation of H-1′ with C-7′.
Thus, the planar structure of malacinnamycin A (1) was
completed (Figure 1).
The sequence of the amino acids and the planar structure of
1 were elucidated by ammonolysis and subsequent MS
analysis. The compound was subjected to NH4OH to yield
the linearized derivative. A HRESIMS/MS experiment
provided a variety of fragmentation ions for the derivative
(Figure S2). The linkage sequence for all residues was
confirmed by scission of amido bonds in the MS/MS
spectrum. The absolute configuration for all amino acids in 1
were determined using modified Marfey’s method (Figure S3).
Two Thr and two Ser were determined to be L-amino acids,
while Leu and Asp possessed the D-configuration.
The configuration of N-acetylglucosamine was determined
by chemical derivatization and LC-MS analysis. 1 was
hydrolyzed and reacted with
L-cysteine methyl ester to
introduce a chiral center and then derivatized with o-tolyl
isocyanate to facilitate detection. Standard D-N-acetylglucos-
amine was treated in the same manner. The resulting
derivatives were injected into LC/MS for analysis and showed
identical retention times (Figure S4).
Malacinnamycin B (2) was also obtained as a pale-yellow
powder. Its molecular formula was determined to be
C42H61N7O16 on the basis of HRESIMS data that displayed
a [M + Na]+ ion at m/z 942.4067, which was 203 Da less than
1, corresponding to a reduction in the molecular weight of N-
acetylglucosamine. Comprehensive analysis of the 2D NMR
spectra of 2 revealed that the seven amino acid residues were
identical to those in 1, but there were some differences in
cinnamyl residues. Olefinic protons H-7 and H-6 (δH 7.04 and
7.25) have a COSY correlation with each other and HMBC
correlations with aromatic carbon C-8, suggesting that the
double bond is directly connected to the benzene ring. To
determine the stereochemistry of the two chiral carbons in the
cinnamoyl moiety, we subjected 2 to hydrolysis with KOH and
separated the cinnamoyl fragment. On the basis of the J-based
configuration analysis,25 the most appropriate rotamers of C4
and C5 were selected. The relationships between C-3/H-5 and
C-6/H-4 were deduced as anti because of the large
corresponding 3JH5−C3 value (7.4 Hz) and 3JH4−C6 (8.7 Hz).

Malacinnamycin is the second class of cinnamoyl-containing
glycopeptide reported after kitacinnamycin. Based on the
bioinformatic analysis of the mcn BGC (Table S1), we deduced
the biosynthetic pathway for malacinnamycin (Figure S8). The
type II PKS system composed of KS/CLF (Mcn6/Mcn7,
Mcn5/Mcn10), ACP (Mcn4), KR (Mcn14), and DH (Mcn12
and Mcn13) in the mcn cluster shared moderate to high
sequence homologies (>50%) with their counterparts in the
kcn gene cluster. Thus, we proposed that the type II PKS in the
mcn cluster utilizes butyryl-ACP and malonyl-CoA as
substrates to synthesize a C12-pentaene intermediate that is
tethered to the ACP after four rounds of extension.
Subsequently, with the action of isomerase enzymes
(Mcn15), the fourth double bond undergoes isomerization.
The intermediate then further undergoes two more rounds of
extension to form a C16-heptaene, which is finally cyclized into
the cinnamoyl moiety by the action of the three-protein
complex Mcn23, Mcn24, and Mcn25 (Figure S2).
In the mcn gene cluster, there are a total of four NRPS
proteins, including Mcn20, Mcn16, Mcn17, and Mcn18, that
encode seven modules. According to antiSMASH analysis, they
can sequentially recognize and load L-Thr, L-Thr, L-Leu, L-Ser,
L-Asp, Gly, and L-Ser onto the NRPS assembly line. Modules 3
and 5 also contain epimerase domains (E domains), which are
presumed to isomerize L-Leu and L-Asp into D-Leu and D-Asp,
respectively, consistent with the structures of 1 and 2.
Interestingly, at the C-terminus of module 1 there is a TE
domain, while module 2 lacks a key condensation domain (C
domain). Instead, we observed an additional peptide
synthetase (Mcn31) in the gene cluster. Therefore, it is likely
that after the synthesis in module 1 it is released from PCP by
the TE domain, then reactivated by Mcn31 and incorporated
in module 2. It is noteworthy that haoxinamide (Figure S1),21
characterized by a cinnamoyl moiety with an N-terminal
threonine residue, bears structural resemblance to 6. This
suggests that the proposed temporary offloading and reloading
of the initial condensation product share a similar biosynthetic
system in these CCNPs. Subsequently, the remaining NRPS
modules in the mcn cluster act in sequence, extending the
peptide chain to an heptapeptide, which cyclizes to release the
intermediate 5 from NRPS assembly line. The P450 enzyme
Mcn21 encoded in the mcn cluster shares 74% sequence
identity with Avm43, which is responsible for the epoxidation
of the alkene in the cinnamoyl moiety in the atrovimycin
BGC.12 Thus, Mcn21 is proposed to epoxidize the double
bonds at C4 and C5 to give 4. Subsequently, a water molecule
is proposed to attack the epoxide ring either from C5 or C7,26
resulting in the formation of 2 and 3. Finally, under the action
of the glycosyltransferase, Mcn22, a molecule of N-
acetylglucosamine is transferred to the hydroxyl group at 7-
OH in 3 to yield 1 (Figure S8).
In conclusion, the explosive growth of microbial genomic
data in recent years clearly demonstrates that the biosynthetic
potential of microbes has been greatly underestimated.
However, the discovery of secondary metabolites has always
been an arduous and laborious process. In this study, using
conserved cyclases and isomerases in cinnamoyl biosynthesis
as probes, we identified 207 biosynthetic gene clusters from
genomic databases, and further GNN analysis revealed the
chemical diversity of the encoded CCNPs. The successful
identification of malacinnamycins as a new class of CCNP-type
glycopeptides verifies the power of our genomics-guided
discovery strategy for natural products discovery. Future
studies of other gene clusters will uncover more novel
CCNPs, which will expand the chemical space of CCNP-
type natural products.
