ABSTRACT: Glycosides make up a biomedically important class
of secondary metabolites. Most naturally occurring glycosides were
isolated from plants and bacteria; however, the chemical diversity
of glycosylated natural products in fungi remains largely unex-
plored. Herein, we present a paradigm to specifically discover
diverse and bioactive glycosylated natural products from fungi by
combining tailoring enzyme-guided genome mining with mass
spectrometry (MS)-based metabolome analysis. Through in vivo
genes deletion and heterologous expression, the first fungal C-
glycosyltransferase AuCGT involved in the biosynthesis of
stromemycin was identified from Aspergillus ustus. Subsequent
homology-based genome mining for fungal glycosyltransferases by using AuCGT as a probe revealed a variety of biosynthetic gene
clusters (BGCs) containing its homologues in diverse fungi, of which the glycoside-producing capability was corroborated by high-
performance liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS) analysis. Consequently, 28 fungal aromatic polyketide C/O-
glycosides, including 20 new compounds, were efficiently discovered and isolated from the three selected fungi. Moreover, several
novel fungal C/O-glycosyltransferases, especially three novel α-pyrone C-glycosyltransferases, were functionally characterized and
verified in the biosynthesis of these glycosides. In addition, a proof of principle for combinatorial biosynthesis was applied to design
the production of unnatural glycosides in Aspergillus nidulans. Notably, the newly discovered glycosides exhibited significant antiviral,
antibacterial, and antidiabetic activities. Our work demonstrates the promise of tailoring enzyme-guided genome-mining approach
for the targeted discovery of fungal glycosides and promotes the exploration of a broader chemical space for natural products with a
target structural motif in microbial genomes.
■INTRODUCTION
Glycosides are a structurally diverse group of secondary
metabolites that exhibit broad biological activities and include
many important clinical drugs.1 For example, aminoglycosides
and macrolide glycosides are renowned as antibacterial or
antifungal antibiotics;2 digitoxin and puerarin have been
clinically approved to treat cardiovascular diseases;3 phlorizin
was used as drug leads to design dapagliflozin as a sodium−
glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitor for the treatment of
type 2 diabetes.4 The remarkable bioactivity of glycosides has
inspired many attempts to search for more novel and active
congeners in the research and development of medicinal
agents.5 Most naturally occurring glycosides were isolated from
plants and bacteria,6 whereas examples of glycosides from fungi
appeared to be comparatively rare. To date, only about 410
fungal glycosides have been reported by the traditional grind-
and-find approaches.7 However, considering the estimated
over 2 million of fungal species in the earth,8 the chemical
space of glycosylated natural products in fungi remains largely
unexplored. The discovery of novel entities by traditional
strategies is labor-intensive and time-consuming. Thus, it is of
importance and necessity to develop a targeted search method
to the discovery of those unidentified glycosides from the huge
numbers of fungal species with various biological activities.
Glycosylation is typically a late-stage modification during the
biosynthesis of secondary metabolites catalyzed by glycosyl-
transferases (GTs).9 Although numerous secondary metabolite
GTs have been identified from plants or bacteria,10 only a
limited number of fungal GTs have been characterized. Up to
now, 15 fungal O-glycosyltransferases (OGTs) have been
verified in the biosynthesis of terpene O-glycosides11 or used as
biocatalysts for the O-glycosylation of phenolic compounds.12
With more and more fungal genomes sequenced, there are
many unannotated or misannotated secondary metabolite GTs
that remain to be identified. Therefore, a GT-based mining

Stromemycin (1), a depside C-glucoside with two
unsaturated C9 side chains belonging to aromatic polyketide
glycosides, was initially isolated from unidentified fungus
(DSM 12038) with matrix metalloproteinase inhibitory activity
and was rediscovered in Emericella and Penicillium sp.13 We
recently also isolated 1 in the filamentous fungi Aspergillus ustus
CGMCC3.3904. Although the biosynthesis of fungal depsides
has been discussed very recently,14 little is known about the
GTs naturally responsible for their glycosylation. Herein, we
identified the first fungal C-glycosyltransferase AuCGT, which
was related to the biosynthesis of stromemycin from A. ustus.
Based on this, a targeted strategy to mine fungal aromatic
polyketide glycosides was demonstrated by combining GT-
guided genome mining and mass spectrometry (MS)-based
metabolome analysis (Figure S1). Consequently, 28 fungal
aromatic polyketide glycosides, as well as a few novel fungal C/
OGTs for depsides and α-pyrones, were efficiently obtained.
The unnatural glycosides were also produced via heterologous
combinatorial biosynthesis in Aspergillus nidulans. A systematic
bioassay revealed that many glycosides exhibited significant
antiviral, antibacterial, and antidiabetic activities.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
AuCGT Is the C-Glycosyltransferase in the Biosyn-
thesis of Stromemycin. Analyzing the published genome
and our transcriptome of A. ustus,15 we found a candidate
biosynthetic gene cluster (BGC) stm containing both
polyketide synthase (PKS) and GT (Table S1). Bioinformatics
analysis suggested that stmA is a highly reducing PKS for
polyketide side chain assembly and stmB is a nonreducing PKS
for aromatic unit biosynthesis (Figure 1A).16 To verify this, we
initially deleted the stmA gene in the genome of A. ustus
(Figure S2). Comparison of the secondary metabolites
between the ΔstmA mutant and the wild-type by high-
performance liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry
(HPLC-MS) analysis revealed that the production of 1 was
completely abolished (Figure 1B). Similarly, knockout of stmB
also resulted in a complete loss of 1 production. Taken
together, these results clearly confirmed that cluster stm is
responsible for the biosynthesis of stromemycin.
As expected, when the gene stmC was deleted, stromemycin
production was completely abolished, but a new product 2 was
observed (Figure 1B). The appearance of the parent ion peak
at m/z 533 [M −H]−, which was 162 amu less than that of 1,
suggested that 2 was an aglycone of 1. It was confirmed by the
isolation from a 10 L-scale fermentation of the ΔstmC mutant
and unambiguous structural elucidation of 2 as the aglycone of
stromemycin, named bininalkenylresorcylic acid, by MS, 1H,
and 13C NMR analyses (Table S4). Meanwhile, genes stmABC
were expressed in the heterologous host A. nidulans A1145,
and 1 and 2 were detected by HPLC-MS compared with the
control strain (Figure 1C,E,1F). These results further
supported the idea that the identified gene cluster is
responsible for stromemycin biosynthesis. The StmA and
StmB were assigned as scaffold-generating enzymes responsible
for polyketide skeleton, in which the starter-unit acyltransferase
(SAT) domain of nrPKS StmB was proposed to catalyze the
depside bond formation,14a and StmC was the tailoring
enzyme responsible for 3-C-glycosylation (Figure 1G).

frame (ORF) of 1608 bp; and the encoded polypeptide of

Homology-Based Genome Mining for Fungal GTs
and Aromatic Polyketide Glycosides Using AuCGT as a
Probe. A tBLASTp homology search with AuCGT as a query
against the National Center for Biotechnology Information
(NCBI) protein database as well as in-house fungal genome
sequences was performed and identified 158 hits for
homologues with 40−88% identities to AuCGT (Table S5).
All of these homologues are uncharacterized proteins and
occurred in 80 different fungal strains (Figure S10 and Table
S6), including Eurotiomycetes (17 strains), Leotiomycetes (2
strains), Sordariomycetes (53 strains), Dothideomycetes (4
strains), Lecanoromycetes (3 strains), and Pezizomycetes (1
strain). Interestingly, these AuCGT homologues are clustered
with a nonreducing PKS gene in the fungal genome except for
a few unclustered GTs (Table S5), suggesting their similar
catalytic property. However, the previously reported fungal
OGTs were not identified as AuCGT homologues due to their
lower similarity and sequence coverage (Table S7). To better
understand the evolution of fungal GTs, a phylogenetic tree
was constructed to analyze the evolutionary relationships of
AuCGT and homologues with characterized fungal OGTs and
bacterial CGT (Figures 2A and S11). It was particularly
noteworthy that AuCGT and homologues are grouped
together to form a branch, which is clearly divergent from
the reported fungal OGTs and bacterial CGTs. These
significant distinctions indicated that AuCGT and homologues
might have evolved independently from those reported fungal
diterpene, triterpene, and phenolics OGTs and show different
biochemical functions.
From the above bioinformatics analysis, it was supposed that
the fungi containing AuCGT homologues might have
biosynthetic potential to produce aromatic polyketide glyco-
sides (Table S5), although the glycosides have never been
isolated from most of these strains. To test this supposition,

Targeted Discovery of Diverse Fungal Aromatic
Polyketide Glycosides Guided by MS. To further confirm
the above hypotheses, the aforementioned strains were initially
cultivated in PDB liquid medium, and their extracts were
subjected to HPLC-MS analysis and investigated for character-
istic fragment ions of glycosides (Figure 2C).20 As expected,
glycoside production in three Talaromyces spp. was unequiv-
ocally confirmed by HPLC-MS analysis in comparison with the
authentic compound 1 (Figure S12). The MS/MS spectra
showed characteristic fragment ions of a C-linked hexosyl
moiety [M −H −120]−and [M −H −90]−(Figures 2D and
S12). Interestingly, two divergent fungal species P. chlamydo-
spora and C. longipes appear to produce some identical
glycosylated metabolites (Figure S13). The [M −H]−ion
peaks at m/z 837, 865, 647, 675, and 703 and the characteristic
fragment ion [M −H −162]−featuring the existence of an O-
glycosidic hexosyl moiety were observed in both fungal strains
(Figures 2E and S13). Moreover, glycosylated compound
candidates were also detected in the extracts of V. dahliae and
Fusarium sp. MMA4 (Figure S14).21 The [M −H]−ions
appeared at m/z 591, 575, 605, and 589, and the MS/MS
spectra showed characteristic fragment ions of C-hexosyl and
C-deoxyhexosyl moieties, [M −H −120]−and [M −H −
104]−, respectively (Figures 2F and S14). Therefore, their
capability to produce glycosides was corroborated by HPLC-
MS analysis. To specifically find more glycosides and simplify
their isolation, three representative fungi (T. cellulolyticus, P.
chlamydospora, and V. dahliae) were cultivated on different
media (Figures S15−S17, respectively). Through further
investigation and comparison of UV and MS spectra of the
possible glycosides, large-scale cultivation of three strains was
conducted on their preferred medium.
A large-scale cultivation of T. cellulolyticus was performed in
PDB liquid medium for targeted discovery of diverse fungal
polyketide glycosides as well as a bioassay of the glycosides.
After a series of purification steps guided by HPLC-MS
analysis, 14 glycosylated products (1, 3−15) were obtained,
including 10 new glycosides (3−12) and four known ones (1;
13, carnemycin B; 14, carnemycin A; and 15, carnemycin
E).13b22 The structures of 10 new glycosylated compounds
were elucidated by extensive comparison of their UV, MS, and
NMR spectroscopic data with those of stromemycin and
carnemycin B (Figure 3A). As shown in Figure 3A, compounds
3−5 were congeners of stromemycin with variety on the side
chains and designated as talarocellmycin A−C (Table S8).
Compound 6 was the congener of carnemycin B with the
COOH at C-1 and was named carnemycin I (Table S9).
Compounds 7−10 were the dimers of 6 or carnemycin B with
the ester bonds between the COOH group of one molecule
and 2′-OH or 6′-OH of the sugar moiety in the second
molecule and designated as talarocellmycin D−G (Table S10).
Compounds 11 and 12 were formed via an ester linkage
between the COOH group of 6 and 2′-OH or 6′-OH of the
sugar moiety of stromemycin and were named talarocellmycin
H and I (Table S11), respectively. To the best of our
knowledge, this is the first report on depside C-glycosides with
the ester bonds between COOH and OH of the sugar moiety,
therefore increasing the knowledge about the structural
complexity and diversity of fungal C-glycosides. As predicted
by antiSMASH, stromemycin (1) was the major product in our
isolation, while compounds 3−15 were a series of structurally
related glycosides with different tailoring modification.
Likewise, 10 glycosides (16−25) were isolated from a large-
scale fermentation of P. chlamydospora on barley solid medium
(Figure 3B). Among them, two known depside 2-O-β-D-
glucosides were identified as exophillic acid (24) and TPI-1
(25) compared with the reported data.23 Eight new glycosides
(16−23) were elucidated by comparison of their UV, MS, and
NMR spectra with those of exophillic acid (24). To determine
the polyketide side chain length of glycosides 17, 18, 20, and
21, these compounds were hydrolyzed by base buffer,
respectively, and the resulting products (17a, 18a, 18b, 20a,
and 21a) were analyzed by MS and NMR (Tables S14 and
S15). The absolute configuration of the sugar components was
further confirmed by acid hydrolysis and derivation followed
by HPLC analysis (Figure S18).24 As shown in Figure 3B,
compound 16 was an isomer of exophillic acid with a D-
galactose at 4-OH, named phaeomoniecin A (Table S12).
Compounds 17 and 18 were congeners of exophillic acid with
two saturated C9 or C7 side chains and were designated as
phaeomoniecin B and C (Table S13), respectively. Com-
pounds 19−22 were di-O-glycosides, namely, 4-O-β-D-galacto-
sides of 24, 17, 18 and 25, respectively, and named
phaeomoniecin D−G (Tables S12, S13, and S16). Compound
23 was a 6′-O-acetylated product of 19 at the sugar moiety and
was designated as phaeomoniecin H (Table S12). To the best
of our knowledge, this is the first report on depside di-O-
glycosides from fungi, in which the O-glucosylation and the O-
galactosylation were catalyzed by PcOGT1 and PcOGT2
(Figure S19), respectively.
Moreover, the large-scale fermentation of V. dahliae and
fractionation led to the isolation of four C-glycosides (26−29,
Figure 3C). Among them, compounds 26 and 27 were
identified as two new α-pyrone 3-C-β-D-galactosides, named
verapyrone A and B, on the basis of the high-resolution
electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (HR-ESI-MS) and
extensive one-dimensional (1D) and two-dimensional (2D)
NMR spectroscopic data analysis (Table S17), while
deoxyneofusapyrone (28) and 7-desmethyldeoxyneofusapyr-
one (29) were two known α-pyrone 3-C-β-D-4-deoxy-gluco-
sides elucidated by comparing with the reported MS and NMR
data.21

isolated, which significantly enhance the structural complexity
and increase the diversity of fungal glycosides. Although some
α-pyrone C-4-deoxy-glucosides have been reported,21 α-

Functional Characterization of AuCGT Homologues.
To further determine whether these AuCGT homologues were
responsible for the biosynthesis of above depside or α-pyrone
glycosides, five representative GTs (TaCGT, TcCGT,
TpCGT, PcOGT1, and VdCGT from T. amestolkiae, T.
cellulolyticus, T. pinophilus, P. chlamydospora, and V. dahliae,
respectively) were heterologously expressed in yeast or in A.
nidulans. Their catalytic activities were assessed with UDP-
glucose or UDP-galactose and their corresponding aglycons in
vitro, respectively. As shown in Figure 2A, TaCGT, TcCGT,
and TpCGT were able to catalyze the formation of
stromemycin via their 3-C-glucosyltransferase activity and
thus are considered isozymes of AuCGT (Figure S20).
PcOGT1 was a depside 2-O-glucosyltransferase for A1 (Figure
S19). VdCGT was a novel 3-C-glycosyltransferase for the C-
galacosylation of α-pyrone A9 (Figure S21). To the best of our
knowledge, this is the first report on α-pyrone C-glycosyl-
transferase found in nature. Therefore, the catalytic functions
of these five GTs were in agreement with the types of
glycosides discovered in selected strains, which provide
evidence for the involvement of these enzymes in the
formation of these glycosides. Moreover, another two fungal
GTs (AcCGT and TmCGT from Aspergillus carlsbadensis and
Talaromyces marneffei, respectively) were also characterized as
depside 3-C-glucosyltransferases for the formation of 1 (Figure
S20), and RlCGT from Rhizodiscina lignyota was identified as
α-pyrone 3-C-glycosyltransferase for the formation of 27
(Figure S21), supporting the potential glycoside-producing
capability of these strains. In turn, these identified fungal GTs
with low similarity to AuCGT, such as PcOGT1, PcOGT2,
and VdCGT, could also be used as probes to further guide
genome mining for the targeted discovery of more novel fungal
glycosides in future work. Therefore, these findings further
expand the nature’s toolbox of GTs and provide more genetic
information for better understanding their evolution and the
future mining of additional novel fungal GTs.
To explore whether there are specific residues of fungal GTs
that determine the C-glycosylation versus the O-glycosylation,
10 mutants and eight chimeras were constructed between C-
glycosyltransferase AuCGT and O-glycosyltransferase
PcOGT1 (Figure S22). However, no AuCGT mutants
acquired the capability for O-glycosylation, and the corre-
sponding PcOGT1 mutants also displayed no C-glycosylation
activity (Table S18). Future structural biology studies on
fungal CGTs and OGTs including enzyme−substrate com-
plexes combined with site mutagenesis will be performed to
provide molecular insights of C-glycosylation and O-
glycosylation.
Diversifying Glycosides by Combinatorial Biosyn-
thesis in A. nidulans. To generate more novel glycosides,
genes from different fungi were combined into an artificial
biosynthetic pathway of unnatural glycosides in A. nidulans.
Two pairs of PKSs for the different skeleton formation were
initially selected from A. ustus and P. chlamydospora,
respectively, and heterologous combinatorial expression was
performed in A. nidulans, resulting in four transformant strains.
HPLC analysis revealed that one strain expressing stmAB could
produce the desired compound 2 (Figure S23). Coexpression
of stmAB with O-glucosyltransferase PcOGT1 or O-galactosyl-
transferase PcOGT2 was then conducted in A. nidulans,
respectively, and led to the production of three corresponding
compounds 30−32 (Figure 4). Based on the HR-ESI-MS and
extensive 1D and 2D NMR spectroscopic data analysis,
compounds 30 and 31 were identified as 2-O-β-D-glucoside
and 4-O-β-D-galactoside of 2 and were designated as
talarocellmycin J and K, respectively (Table S19). Likewise,
compound 32 was determined as a phenolic 4-O-β-D-
galactoside and named carnemycin J (Table S19). Such a
proof of principle suggested the feasibility of the design of new
glycosides by heterologous combinatorial biosynthetic meth-
ods. In addition, genome-mined BGCs (PKS and GT genes)
from V. dahliae and Fusarium sp. were also heterologously
expressed in A. nidulans. However, only BGC from the
Fusarium sp. was productive in A. nidulans (Figure S24). The

Bioactivity of Fungal Glycosides In Vitro. All glycosides
were then biologically evaluated for their antiviral, antibacte-
rial, antidiabetic, and cytotoxic activities in vitro (Table 1). Six
O-glycosides (17−19, 23, 24, and 30) displayed anti-HIV
activity with IC50 of 2.3−8.6 μM, and two O-glycosides (31
and 32) showed anti-IAV activity with IC50 of 4.5 and 3.0 μM,
respectively (Table S20). Six C/O-glycosides (17, 18, 24, 25,
28, and 30) were found to show antibacterial activity against
methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) with
minimum inhibition concentration (MIC) of 8.9−10.9 μM
(Table S21). Moreover, nine O-glycosides (17−21, 23−25,
and 30) showed significant α-glucosidase inhibitory activity
with IC50 of 2.2−5.2 μM compared with the control acarcose
(Table S22); 16 C/O-glycosylated compounds (1, 3, 4, 11, 12,
16−21, 23−25, 30, and 31) exhibited significant protein
tyrosine phosphatase 1B (PTP1B) inhibitory activity with IC50
of 0.32−6.4 μM (Table S22). In addition, some glycosides
displayed moderate cytotoxic activity against cancer cells
(Table S23). These results show that the discovered
compounds hold promise for drug discovery projects (Table
1).■CONCLUSIONS
In this study, we identified and characterized the first fungal C-
glycosyltransferase AuCGT, which was used as a probe to
explore the chemical diversity of glycosylated natural products
in fungi. Based on aromatic polyketide GT-guided genome
mining and MS analysis for the characteristic fragment ions of
glycosides, 28 fungal aromatic polyketide C/O-glycosides
including 20 new compounds were specifically discovered
and isolated from the three selected fungi. Moreover, another
five depside CGTs and a depside OGT were functionally
characterized, and three novel α-pyrone CGTs were identified
for the first time in nature. Combinatorial biosynthesis of PKSs
and GTs from different strains was further applied to design
the production of new unnatural glycosides in A. nidulans.
Notably, many glycosides exhibited significant antiviral,
antibacterial, and antidiabetic activities. Through aromatic
polyketide GT-guided genome mining and HPLC-MS-based
metabolome analysis, our studies increase the chemical
diversity of fungal bioactive glycosides and expand the nature’s
toolbox of fungal GTs. With growing fungal genomic
resources, tailoring enzyme-based genome mining and MS
analysis strategies would promote the exploration of a broader
chemical space and bioactivities for natural products with a
target structural motif from microbes. Further studies on
mining more different BGCs including nonpolyketide BGCs
with GT genes, activating silent BGCs and combinatorial
biosynthesis of more core enzymes, and tailoring enzymes in
different hosts will be developed to further expand the
chemical diversity of bioactive natural and unnatural products
for the discovery of drug leads.
