Two Iron(II), α‑Ketoglutarate-Dependent Enzymes Encoded by the
PPZ Gene Cluster of Metarhizium majus Enable Production of
8‑Hydroxyperamine
Kristina W. Rothchild, Mostafa Hagar, Daniel Berry, and Katherine S. Ryan*

ABSTRACT: Entomopathogenic fungus Metarhizium majus contains the nine-gene PPZ cluster, with ppzA, encoding a peramine-
producing nonribosomal peptide synthetase, as the central component. In this work, the roles of two α-ketoglutarate, iron-dependent
oxygenases encoded by the PPZ genes ppzC and ppzD were elucidated. PpzD was found to produce both trans-4-hydroxy-L-proline
and trans-3-hydroxy-L-proline in a 13.1:1 ratio, yielding a key precursor for peramine biosynthesis. PpzC was found to act directly on
peramine, yielding the novel analogue 8-hydroxyperamine.
N
atural products are a rich source of novel scaffolds,
driving insecticide discovery.1 In particular, fungi
represent an untapped source of highly diverse, bioactive
natural products2 with great potential in crop protection.3
Peramine (1) is an insect feeding deterrent produced by
endophytic fungi from the genus Epichloë.4−7 It is biosynthe-
sized by a two-module, nonribosomal peptide synthetase
(NRPS) PpzA, incorporating arginine and hydroxyproline as
its substrates.8,9 In 2019, Berry et al.10 discovered putative
ppzA orthologues in the published genomes of entomopatho-
genic fungi Metarhizium rileyi and Metahrizium majus, and the
lichen-forming fungus Cladonia grayi. However, unlike in
Epichloë spp., these ppzA orthologues are the central
components of larger “PPZ” clusters, each containing seven
conserved genes (ppzA-F) plus additional genus-specific genes.
Metarhizium ppzA orthologues were shown to encode
functional peramine-synthetases via heterologous expression
in Penicillium paxilli, leading to the hypothesis that 1, the final
product in Epichloë spp., is further modified into novel
secondary metabolite(s) in M. rileyi, M. majus, and C.
grayi.10 The M. majus PPZ gene cluster, and peramine
biosynthesis by PpzA, are summarized in Figure 1, with the
domain structure of PpzA, and the PPZ gene clusters from M.
majus, M. rileyi, and C. grayi shown in Figure S1.
Reported herein are the uncovered roles of the M. majus
PpzC and PpzD enzymes, two α-ketoglutarate iron-dependent
(αKG/Fe) oxygenases.10 αKG/Fe oxygenases are known to
catalyze a wide range of chemical transformations in natural
products,11,12 including hydroxylation,13 epoxidation,14−16
stereoinversion,17 isonitrile formation,18 desaturation,19 perox-
idation,20 isomerization,20 and esterification.21 In this work, we
experimentally determined the intron/exon boundaries in
ppzC and ppzD, and expressed codon-optimized coding
sequences of these genes in Escherichia coli. Our in vitro
enzyme assays show that PpzD is involved in the biosynthesis
of a known precursor to 1, and that PpzC acts directly on 1,
yielding a novel compound that was isolated and structurally
elucidated via NMR spectroscopy.
To facilitate heterologous expression of codon-optimized
PPZ coding sequences in E. coli, the cDNA sequences of ppzC
and ppzD from M. majus ARSEF 297 were determined
experimentally. Total RNA was extracted from fungal cultures
and, following DNase treatment, reverse-transcribed into
cDNA, which was used as a template for PCR amplification,
cloning, and sequencing of the target sequences. Gene ppzC
was found to contain no introns, while gene ppzD was found to
contain two introns, as described in Figure S2.

PpzD was hypothesized to be a prolyl-4-hydroxylase due to
its sequence homology with known fungal proyl-4-hydroxylases
BuaE22 and HtyE,23 which both share ∼50% sequence identity
with PpzD. While it is unknown which stereoisomer of 4-
hydroxyproline is produced by BuaE, HtyE was found to
produce both trans-4-hydroxy-L-proline (2) and trans-3-
hydroxy-L-proline (3) in a 2:1 molar ratio.13 Given this
homology, and the fact that feeding both 2 and cis-4-hydroxy-
D-proline (4) was previously shown to increase peramine
production by PpzA in vivo,9 we investigated PpzD for
production of all three of these stereoisomers.
We assayed the recombinant PpzD activity in vitro with α-
ketoglutarate, iron sulfate, sodium ascorbate, and L-proline.
Reaction mixtures were derivatized with 9-fluorenylmethoxy-
carbonyl chloride (Fmoc-Cl) and analyzed via liquid
chromatography−mass spectrometry (LCMS), selectively
monitoring the N-Fmoc-hydroxyproline [M + H]+ ion
(354.1 m/z). Through comparison with analytical standards,
we determined that the major product of PpzD’s reaction with
L-proline is 2, and the minor product is 3 (Figure 2A).
To confirm the stereochemistry of the products, we repeated
the LCMS analysis using chiral affinity chromatography with
analytical standards for cis-4-hydroxy-L-proline and 2 (Figure
2A). We also determined that PpzD accepts D-proline as a
substrate, producing 4 (Figure S5).
To further investigate the regioselectivity of PpzD’s reaction
with L-proline, the enzyme was assayed in Tris buffer while
varying pH, and the ratio of regioisomers 2 to 3 was compared.
The ratio was consistent from pH 6.5 to 8.0, ranging from
12.0:1 to 14.0:1. The average ratio across the range was 13.1:1,
with a standard deviation of 1.4 (Figure S6). Production of 3
was lost at pH 8.5, and the production of both 2 and 3 was lost
at pH 9.0. Production of 2 and 3 was the highest at pH 6.5 and
pH 7.0 (Figure S7). Reactions performed by PpzD are
summarized in Figure 2.
Based on these results, we classify PpzD as the fourth
member of a small group of enzymes capable of producing
both 2 and 3, alongside fungal prolyl-hydroxylase GloF from
Glarea lozoyensis,24 bacterial prolyl-hydroxylase UbP3H from
the uncultured bacterium esnapd13,25 and HtyE from
Aspergillus pachycristatus ATCC 58397.13 Unlike UbP3H,
which produces 3 as its major product,25 PpzD is more closely
aligned with the fungal prolyl-hydoxylases in this group,
producing a greater amount of 2. However, while GloF and
HtyE produce 2 and 3 in an 8:1 and 2:1 ratio, respectively,
PpzD highly favors the production of 2 with a 13.1:1 ratio.
Thus, we hypothesize that the role of PpzD in the PPZ gene
cluster is to facilitate peramine production by ensuring the
supply of a rate-limiting substrate. To further investigate this
possibility, we analyzed published Epichloë genomes for genes
encoding PpzD homologues that could serve to increase
peramine production in those organisms. Interestingly, almost
all Epichloë genomesincluding many from known peramine
producerslacked strong homologues to PpzD, suggesting
that most Epichloë spp. use an alternative source of
hydroxyproline for peramine biosynthesis. One notable
exception was the Epichloë typhina strain E8, known to be a
particularly strong producer of peramine.26 This strain contains
the gene Ety8_004252 which encodes a protein that shares
61% sequence homology with PpzD. Like ppzA from E. typhina
E8, the pattern of gene regulation of Ety8_004252 suggests
that it is involved in symbiosis between E. typhina E8 and its
host.26 However, Ety8_004252 also forms part of a larger gene
cluster that does not include ppzA,26 so more research is
required to determine if it plays a role in peramine
biosynthesis. Regardless, introducing ppzD into other per-
amine-producing Epichloë spp. might also be a useful way to
boost production of this agriculturally beneficial natural
product.
Unlike PpzD, PpzC lacks close sequence homologues with
known functions. An attempt was made to identify structural
homologues by performing a DALI27 search using an
AlphaFold28 prediction of PpzC (Table S6), but we were
not able to glean any insights regarding PpzC’s potential
function. Given that fungal biosynthetic gene clusters often
contain “core genes” which specify the skeletal structure of the
secondary metabolite, and peripheral “accessory genes” which
further modify the skeleton29 we tested synthetic 1 as a
putative substrate.
Recombinant PpzC was assayed with 1, α-ketoglutarate, iron
sulfate, and sodium ascorbate and analyzed via liquid
chromatography-high-resolution mass spectrometry (LC-
HRMS). We detected a new, earlier eluting chromatographic
peak (5), which was absent in the heat-inactivated control
(Figure 3A). The observed m/z is appropriate for an [M + H]+
ion with a molecular formula of C12H18N5O2
+ (exp. m/z
264.1455, calc. m/z 264.1455, Δ = 0 mDa), exactly one oxygen
atom heavier than peramine, suggesting that PpzC had formed

an oxygenated peramine species. The reaction was repeated in
vivo in E. coli BL21(DE3), which we found to more effectively
convert 1 into 5 compared to the in vitro reaction (Figure 3B).
Also, we observed accumulation of 5 in the growth media,
indicating export from producing cells.
To elucidate the structure of 5, the in vivo reaction was
scaled up, and the product was isolated using reversed-phase
high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), with
ultraviolet (UV) detection (λmax= 235 nm) to afford 5 as an
off-white amorphous solid (2.0 mg). Analysis by NMR
revealed resonances with similar chemical shifts as those
reported for peramine,30 as expected. Examining 1D 1H and
multiplicity-edited 1H−13C HSQC data, we identified three
methylene groups (δH 1.71, 2.48, 3.17), three alkene proton
resonances (δH 6.93, 6.98, 5.94), an N-methyl group (δH 3.25),
and a broad exchangeable guanidine proton resonance (δH
7.9). COSY correlations show the three methylene groups (H-
1′,2′,3′) and guanidino proton forming a contiguous spin-
system, indicating that oxygenation had not occurred on the C-
1′C-3′ chain. Further inspection of 13C and 1H−13C HMBC
data identified carbonyl and guanidino carbons (δC 156.9,
157.0), and three quaternary carbons (δC 106.7, 126.6, 145.6).
The presence of an additional quaternary carbon and the
absence of an alkene resonance compared to 1 confirm that
oxygenation had indeed occurred in the pyrrolopyrazine
system.
The site of oxygenation can be identified by accounting for
the pyrrolopyrazine protons. The alkene proton resonance (δH
6.93) shows an NOE correlation with H-1′, as well as allylic 4J
coupling observed in the COSY data, and can therefore be
assigned as H-4, narrowing possible oxygenation sites to the
pyrrole ring (C-6, 7, 8). The remaining alkene proton
resonances δH 6.98 and δH 5.94 are doublets with coupling
constants 3J = 2.7 Hz, similar to H-6,7 in peramine (3J = 2.6
Hz).31 This indicated that the remaining pyrrole protons are
vicinal and eliminated 7-hydroxy-peramine as a possible
structure. Although an NOE correlation between H-6 and H-
4 would unambiguously identify the oxygenation site, the two
proton resonances are nearly isochronous (δH = 6.93 and 6.98)
and would obfuscate the cross-peak. However, the HMBC
correlation between H-6 and C-4 eliminates 6- hydroxy-
peramine as a possible structure, leading to the structural
assignment of 5 as 8-hydroxy-peramine.
Key NMR correlations are depicted in Figure 4B and Figure
S15. Spectra are available in Figures S9−S14. Our structural
proposal is corroborated with density functional theory (DFT)
calculations and application of the DP4+ formalism,32 in which
8-hydroxyperamine is the most likely candidate (>99%) given
our experimental data (compared to 6-hydroxy- and 7-
hydroxperamine, Supporting Information). Collision-induced
fragmentation MS spectra were also acquired for both 1 and 5
to support this assignment (Figure S16).
The production of 5 by PpzC is consistent with the proposal
by Berry et al.10 that PPZ genes encode “accessory enzymes”
which further derivatize peramine. Thus, our findings indicate
that 5 is an intermediate in the PPZ biosynthetic pathway,
further modified by the remaining members of the gene cluster.
Aside from its implications in the biosynthesis of peramine
derivatives, PpzC could present useful applications in
bioengineering, where there has been a growing interest in
employing αKG/Fe oxygenases for chemoenzymatic syntheses
of biologically relevant compounds.33−36 Among fungal αKG/
Fe oxygenases, PpzC is unique in its ability to hydroxylate an
sp2-hybridized carbon center while preserving hybridization.
To the best of our knowledge, the only other enzymes known
to hydroxylate an aromatic carbon are plant enzymes, such as
melatonin 2-hydroxylase from rice (Oryza sativa),37 salicylic
acid 3-hydroxylase from Arabidopsis thaliana,38 F6H from
Chrysosplenium americanum,39,40 and BX6 from Zea mays.41,42
In summary, we have uncovered the role of PpzD encoded
by the M. majus PPZ gene cluster. We demonstrated that PpzD
generates multiple stereo- and constitutional isomers of
hydroxyproline, a key substrate for peramine biosynthesis by
PpzA. Thus, we hypothesize that the role of PpzD in the PPZ
gene cluster is to facilitate peramine production by ensuring
the supply of a rate-limiting substrate for further derivatization
by the PPZ gene cluster. We also demonstrated that PpzC acts
directly on peramine, yielding a novel compound which was
isolated for structure elucidation by NMR spectroscopy, and
determined to be 8-hydroxyperamine. This result supports the
hypothesis by Berry et al.10 that the PPZ genes encode proteins
that are involved in the further derivatization of peramine.

Thus, further work must be done to fully elucidate the PPZ
pathway, potentially yielding a novel insecticide or biocontrol
agent. Remaining, unexplored genes in the pathway include
ppzE and ppzG, encoding cytochrome P450 oxygenases, ppzF,
encoding a sulfotransferase, and ppz1, encoding a sulfatase.10
These enzymes may act directly on peramine, 8-hydroxyper-
