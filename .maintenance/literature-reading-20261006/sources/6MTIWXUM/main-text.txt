ABSTRACT: Skyrin and rugulosin A are bioactive bisanthraquinones found in
many fungi, with the former suggested as a precursor of hypericin (a diversely
bioactive phytochemical) and the latter characterized by its distinct cage-like
structure. However, their biosynthetic pathways remain mysterious, although
they have been characterized for over six decades. Here, we present the rug gene
cluster that governs simultaneously the biosynthesis of skyrin and rugulosin A in
Talaromyces sp. YE3016, a fungal endophyte residing in Aconitum carmichaeli. A
combination of genome sequencing, gene inactivation, heterologous expression,
and biotransformation tests allowed the identiﬁcation of the gene function,
biosynthetic precursor, and enzymatic sets involved in their molecular
architecture constructions. In particular, skyrin was demonstrated to form from
the 5,5′-dimerization of emodin radicals catalyzed by RugG, a cytochrome P450
monooxygenase evidenced to be potentially applicable for the (chemo)enzymatic
synthesis of dimeric polyphenols. The fungal aldo-keto reductase RugH was shown to be capable of hijacking the closest skyrin
precursor (CSP) immediately after the emodin radical coupling, catalyzing the ketone reduction of CSP to inactivate its
tautomerization into skyrin and thus allowing for the spontaneous intramolecular Michael addition to cyclize the ketone-reduced
form of CSP into rugulosin A, a representative of diverse cage-structured bisanthraquinones. Collectively, the work updates our
understanding of bisanthraquinone biosynthesis and paves the way for synthetic biology accesses to skyrin, rugulosin A, and their
siblings.
■INTRODUCTION
Anthraquinones are structurally featured by a 9,10-dioxoan-
thracene core and constitute a structurally diverse class of
natural products with a wide range of applications as colorants
and medicines.1,2 Anthraquinone-rich/containing plants such
as rhubarb and aloe have been used as folk medicine for more
than 4000 years in many parts of the world.3 The structural
diversity of natural anthraquinones is ensured both by the
substituents at benzene rings thereof and by their homo- and
heterodimerization into bisanthraquinones. Some fungi are
eﬃcient bisanthraquinone producers with two identical or
diﬀerent anthraquinone monomers linked together through
one to four C−C bonds (Figure 1). Such dimerizations seem
to have conferred distinct physiological activities on the
bisanthraquinones in comparison with the monomeric
precursors.4−8 Accordingly, the exploration of the mechanism
underlying anthraquinone dimerization in nature has attracted
the attention of many researchers.9
Skyrin (1) and rugulosin A (2) are representative fungal
bisanthraquinones with important physiological activities. As
an antibacterial and antitumor agent,10,11 1 was hypothesized
to result from two emodin (5) molecules that are linked
together through the C5−C5′ bond (Figure 1). As a cage-
structured anti-HIV7 and antimicrobial bisanthraquinone,8 2
was biosynthetically proposed to form from the same emodin

molecule through three bonds (C7−C10a′, C5−C5′, and

structures of 1 and 2 have spurred their chemical syn-

thesis remains limited to the isotope feeding tests (performed
half a century ago16,17) and the bioinformatic prediction of the
skyrin (1) biosynthetic cluster that awaits experimental
veriﬁcation.18 Skyrin (1) and rugulosin A (2) have been
individually isolated from a broad range of taxonomically
distant fungi (e.g., 1 from Cyanodermella asteris,18 Talaromyces
islandicus,19 Dermocybe icterinoides,20 and T. rugulosus;21 2 from
T. rugulosus,22 Ophiocordyceps formosana,23 Chrysoporthe sp.
SNB-CN74,24 and Phialocephala scopiformis25). More notably,
some Talaromyces species including T. rugulosus,21 T.
brunneus,26 and T. wortmannii27 have been demonstrated to
be eﬃcient coproducers of 1 and 2.28,29 However, nothing is
known about how a single fungal cell produces simultaneously
these two skeletally distinct bisanthraquinones.
In this study, we aimed to identify the biosynthetic genes
responsible for the bioassembly of 1 and 2 by the endophytic
fungus Talaromyces sp. YE3016.30 Using a combination of
genome sequencing, gene inactivation, heterologous expres-
sion, and biotransformation approaches, we were able to
identify the rug gene cluster that encodes a complete set of
enzymes required for the molecular construction of 1 and 2. In
particular, the metabolic ﬂux toward 1 or 2 was shown to be
controlled by RugG (a cytochrome P450 monooxygenase) and
RugH (an aldo-keto reductase), which funnels the closest
skyrin (1) precursor to the generation of the cage-like
substructure found in many natural bisanthraquinones.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
The rug Gene Cluster That Governs the Biosynthesis
of Skyrin (1) and Rugulosin A (2). Talaromyces sp. YE3016
was isolated as a fungal endophyte from Aconitum carmichaeli
Dexb. (Ranunculaceae), a traditional Chinese herbal medi-
cine.30 From its solid-state fermentation, 1 was isolated
without detecting 2.30 Encouraged by the simultaneous
characterization of 1 and 2 from some Talaromyces
species,21,26,27 the YE3016 strain was regrown to ascertain
whether it produces 2, too. Luckily, 2 was obtained (Figure 2B,
i) by this endeavor with its robust identiﬁcation accomplished
by MS (Table S12) and NMR (Table S6 and Figure S22).
Based on their structural feature and isotope feeding studies,16
both 1 and 2 are proposed to derive from emodin (5), which
was identiﬁed as a byproduct or intermediate on the
biosynthetic pathways of a few more nonreducing polyketides
such as atrochrysone,31 monodictyphenone,32 agnestin A,33
and cladofulvin.4 In order to get insight into the 1 and 2
biosynthesis, the whole genome of Talaromyces sp. YE3016 was
sequenced and bioinformatically analyzed using antiSMASH.34
The fungal genome was found to possess a total of 13
biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) (designated as pks1−pks13)
that contain polyketide synthase genes (Table S1 and Figure
S1). Protein sequence alignment analysis showed that the
PKS12 shares a high amino acid sequence identity to the
known emodin-producing PKSs such as ACAS (59%),31
MdpG (61%),32 AgnPKS (62%),33 and ClaG (64%) (Figure
S2).4 Our inactivation of pks12 abolished the production of 1
and 2 (Figure 2B, ii), indicating that the pks12 gene (hereafter
referred to as rugA) is required for the biosynthesis of the two
bisanthraquinones.
The above rugA inactivation results directed our attention to
the rugA-containing BGC, which we termed the rug gene
cluster. Located in scaﬀold 9 (Table S1), the rug gene cluster
includes 11 additional genes that encode respectively a β-
lactamase-type thioesterase (RugB), an α-glucoside permease
(RugC), an α-glucosidase (RugD), an anthrone oxygenase
(RugE), a decarboxylase (RugF), a cytochrome P450
monooxygenase (RugG), an aldo-keto reductase (RugH), an
MFS-type transporter (RugJ), a transcriptional coactivator
(RugK), and two Zn(II)Cys6 transcription factors (RugI and
RugL) (Figure 2A and Table S2). To ascertain their roles in
the biosynthesis of 1 and 2, we individually knocked out the
possibly functional genes (rugB, rugC, rugD, rugE, rugF, rugG,
and rugH) to obtain a series of mutants with catalytic activity
distinct from that of the wild-type (WT) strain.
The HPLC analysis of the mutant cultures showed that the
ΔrugB strain was unable to produce 1 and 2 (Figure 2B, iii).
This agreed with the bioinformatic prediction of RugB as a β-
lactamase-type thioesterase capable of releasing the polycyclic
enzyme-bound intermediate, thus compensating for the
absence of canonical thiolesterase (TE) or Claisen cyclase
domains in RugA (Table S1). However, the ΔrugC mutant
generated 1 and 2 as did the WT strain (Figure 2B, iv). The
observation indicated that RugC, an α-glucoside permease, is
not involved in the biosynthesis of 1 and 2. The ΔrugD strain
was deprived of biosynthesizing 1 and 2 (Figure 2B, v),
indicating that the α-glucosidase (RugD) activity is important
in their production. As predicted by the TMHMM program,
RugE was likely a membrane-associated protein (Figure S4)
sharing a 48% amino acid identity with AgnL2, an anthrone
oxidase that was supposed to accelerate the spontaneous
conversion of emodin anthrone (4) into emodin (5) (Figure
S6).33 Unexpectedly, the ΔrugE strain was unable to produce 1
and 2 (Figure 2B, vi), suggesting the RugE-dependent

Stepwise Reconstitution of Skyrin (1) and Rugulosin
A (2) Biosyntheses in Aspergillus oryzae. With the mutants
in hand, we sought out to identify the intermediates that could
help understand the biosynthetic steps toward 1 and 2.
However, such attempts turned out to be a failure since the
extracts derived from the mutant fermentations were extremely
complicated presumably owing to the coexpression of diverse
BGCs in Talaromyces sp. YE3016 (Figure S1). To overcome
the frustration, we alternatively reproduced the 1 and 2
biosynthetic steps in Aspergillus oryzae NSAR1 (shortened as
“AO”). According to the predicted function, the rug genes were
grouped and expressed in AO to ascertain the cascade
reactions in the bioassembly line of 1 and 2.
When the nrPKS gene (rugA) was expressed alone, no
polyketide appeared (Figure 3, i). This conﬁrmed the
prediction that RugA contains no TE and Claisen cyclase
domains (Table S1) like its orthologs ACAS,31 MdpG,32
AgnPKS,33 and ClaG.4 In contrast, the AO-rugAB cotrans-
formant capable of coexpressing RugA and RugB produced
metabolites 3−9 (Figure 3, ii; Figure S7), whose molecular
architectures (vide infra) signiﬁed the involvement of RugB (a
metallo-β-lactamase-type thioesterase) in the cyclization and
release of the nascent polyketide intermediates as discerned
with its orthologs ACTE,31 MdpF,32 AgnL7,33 and ClaF.4
Readily identiﬁed were atrochrysone (3), emodin anthrone
(4), emodin (5), and endocrocin (6) through comparing the
MS (Table S12) and 1H NMR data with those reported35−37
and/or of authentic samples (Tables S7−S9 and Figures S7
and S23−S25). Compounds 7 and 8 were determined to be a
pair of racemates of the 10,10′-homodimer of endocrocin
anthrone. According to our chiral HPLC analysis and speciﬁc
rotation measurement, 7 and 8 were found to be the
interchangeable and thus inseparable enantiomeric mixtures
of (10R,10′R)-to-(10S,10′S)- and (10R,10′S)-to-(10S,10′R)-
endocrocin anthrone homodimers (Table S10 and Figures
S26−S29),31 respectively. Compound 9 was evidenced to have
a molecular formula of C15H12O7 from the HR-ESI-MS data at
m/z 303.0511 (negative ion mode; C15H11O7 requires
303.0510). Subsequent interpretation of its 1D (1H and 13C)
and 2D NMR spectra (1H−1H COSY, HSQC, and HMBC)
established 9 as a new metabolite, which we have named
emodinic acid after its presumable precursor emodin (5). Since
undetected in the culture of Talaromyces sp. YE3016, emodinic
acid was reasoned to be a byproduct arising from A. oryzae that
expresses a Baeyer−Villigerase to catalyze the oxidation of 5
(Figure 4A, i; Table S11 and Figures S30−S34).
Our integration of the rugE gene into the AO-rugAB strain
gave the AO-rugABE cotransformant, which produced a
reduced level of 5 but an escalated abundance of endocrocin
anthrone homodimers 7 and 8 (Figure 3, iii). This agreed with
the prediction of RugE as an anthrone oxygenase that
accelerates the spontaneous dimerization of endocrocin
anthrone. However, the AO-rugABE strain lacking a decarbox-
ylase (RugF) displayed a reduced abundance of emodin
anthrone but an escalated production of endocrocin anthrone,
thereby strengthening the generation of 7 and 8 on exposure to
O2 (Scheme 1). Accordingly, RugE was reasoned to catalyze
the ﬁxation of molecular oxygen into endocrocin anthrone to
form the corresponding anthraquinone 6. As predicted
bioinformatically, the AO-rugABEF cotransformant mainly
produced 5 (Figure 3, iv), conﬁrming that RugF possesses a
decarboxylase function as does its ortholog ClaH, capable of
catalyzing the decarboxylation of atrochrysone carboxylic acid
in the cladofulvin biosynthesis.4 However, skyrin (1) was not
detected in the AO-rugABEF culture, but appeared in the
cultivation of the AO-rugABEFG cotransformant constructed
by integrating the P450-encoding rugG gene into the AO-
rugABEF strain (Figure 3, v). The observation indicated that
RugG was involved in the 5,5′-dimerization of 5 into 1.
Accordingly, the RugABEFG pentad represents the minimal
set of enzymes for the biochemical synthesis of 1. Inspired by
the coinvolvement of RugH and RugG in the 2 biosynthesis,
the rugH gene was integrated into the AO-rugABEFG strain to
give the AO-rugABEFGH cotransformant which generated
rugulosin A (2, major) and skyrin (1, minor) (Figure 3, vii).
The ﬁnding underpinned that only six genes, rugABEFGH,
were suﬃcient for the 2 biosynthesis. In addition, we directly
integrated the rugH gene to the AO-rugABEF strain, but none
of the AO-rugABEFH and AO-rugABEF transformants
generated 1 or 2 (Figure 3, iv and vi). This set of experiments
clariﬁed that the RugH function is RugG-dependent.

gene was heterologously expressed in A. oryzae and
Saccharomyces cerevisiae RC01 (shortened as “SC-RC01”;

with the bioinformatic analysis, conﬁrmed that RugG is a
cytochrome P450 monooxygenase shown herein to catalyze
the 5,5′-dimerization of 5 to form 1.
In addition to 1, Talaromyces sp. YE3016 was demonstrated
to produce other 5,5′-linked bisanthraquinones such as 3-
demethyl-3-(2-hydroxypropyl)skyrin and oxyskyrin.30 We
therefore questioned whether RugG is substrate-promiscuous.
However, RugG is a eukaryotic membrane-bound P450
enzyme (Figure S5), and after heterologously expressed, the
puriﬁcation of such enzymes remains a trial-and-error process.
In view of the A. oryzae Baeyer−Villigerase that cleaves 5 into
9 (Figure 4A, i and v), the whole-cell catalysis system based on
the SC-RC01-rugG transformant (Figure 4A, iii), other than
the in vitro catalysis assay, was adopted to evaluate the
substrate promiscuity of RugG using a group of emodin
analogues with varied substituents on benzene rings. Thus, the
SC-RC01-rugG strain was cultured with exposure to each of
the emodin derivatives (Figure 4B), followed by the LC-UV-
MS analysis of the extracts derived from the transformant
cultivations. The retrieved UV and MS data showed that RugG
catalyzed the 5,5′-dimerization of citreoresein (10), catenarin
(11), and alaternin (12) into the corresponding bisanthraqui-
nones (Figure 4B, Figure S14). Thus, RugG is tolerant to some
variations of the ring C substituents of emodin (5) as its most
preferred substrate with the highest dimer yield among these
entries. However, endocrocin (6, a 2-carboxylated derivative of
5) was shown to be inert to RugG (Figure 4B, Figure S14).
This was likely due to the intermolecular hydrogen bonding
between 2-carboxylic and 6-hydroxyl groups. Also inspired by
the P450-catalyzed radicalization of free (unbound) phenolic
hydroxyls,39 we envisioned that RugG might have aﬃnity to
the 6-OH group of 5 with its accessibility allowed by 5,7-
dimethine motifs (no substituent at C-5 and C-7). This
hypothesis was conﬁrmed by the failure of RugG in catalyzing
the dimerization of chrysophanol, aloe emodin, and physion (a
6-O-methyl ether of emodin (5)), all being free of the 6-OH
group (Figure 4B, Figure S14). Ascertaining the 6-OH
involvement in the RugG-catalyzed 5,5′-dimerization of
anthraquinone units, we were curious about whether RugG
catalyzes the heterodimerization of anthraquinone pairs
grouped from emodin (5) and its variants, which were
citreorosein (10), catenarin (11), and alaternin (12). The
substrate candidates (5 and 10−12) were pairwisely
supplemented into the SC-RC01-rugG cultures. Gratifyingly,
such eﬀorts ascertained the RugG-catalyzed generation of both
homo- (skyrin, skyrinol, and dicatenarin) and heterodimers
derived from a broad range of 6-hydroxyanthraquinone pairs:
emodin plus one of catenarin, alaternin, or citreorosein;
alaternin plus catenarin or citreorosein; and citreorosein plus
catenarin (Figure 4B, Figures S15−S20). The anthraquinone
dimers thus formed were readily analyzed by the LC-UV-MS
approach in view of the molecular weights of anticipated
bisanthraquinones and the red-shifts in UV spectra resulting
from the aryl-extended conjugation system (Figures S14−S20).

Functional Characterization of RugH. With RugG
functionally addressed, we were motivated to decipher the
catalytic mechanism of RugH, a fungal aldo-keto reductase
involved in the rugulosin A (2) biosynthesis (Scheme 1). To
probe the RugH substrate, we heterologously expressed the
rugH gene in A. oryzae and Saccharomyces cerevisiae BJ5464-
NpgA. Since 2 was produced by the AO-rugABEFGH
transformant rather than the AO-rugABEFH strain, we
speculated that skyrin (1) could be a RugH substrate (Figure
3, vi and vii). However, 2 refused to appear in the 1-
supplemented cultures of the AO-rugH (Figure 5A, i) and SC-
BJ5464-rugH transformants (Figure 5B, i). In view of the co-
involvement of RugG and RugH in the 2 biosynthesis, we
constructed the corresponding cotransformants (AO-rugGH
and SC-RC01-rugGH) to explore the function of RugH.
Interestingly, 2 was produced by the AO-rugGH (Figure 5A, iv
and v) and SC-RC01-rugGH (Figure 5B, iv and v) strains after
cultured on exposure to emodin (5). However, 2 failed to be
detected in the 5-exposed cultures of AO-rugH (alone and
together with the AO-rugG strain; Figure 5A, ii and iii) and
SC-BJ5464-rugH transformants (alone and in combination
with the SC-RC01-rugG strain; Figure 5B, ii and iii).

RugH was bioinformatically predicted to be an aldo-keto
reductase, belonging to a large superfamily of NAD(P)H-
dependent oxidoreductases involved in carbonyl metabolism.
RugH shares a 42% sequence homology with human aﬂatoxin
B1 aldehyde reductase member 3 (AKR7A3) (Table S2),
which catalyzes NADPH-dependently the reduction of the
dialdehydic phenolate form of aﬂatoxin B1-8,9-dihydrodiol to a
dialcohol.41 In addition, our phylogenic analysis indicated that
RugH is located in the aldo-keto reductase family 7, to which
the aﬂatoxin B1 aldehyde reductase belongs (Figure S9). For
the aldo-keto reductase, the catalysis is promoted by a
conserved tetrad of active site residues (Tyr, Lys, Asp, and
His).42 Our multiple sequence alignment indicated that Asp52,
Tyr57, Lys81, and His117 in RugH are conserved (Figure S8).
Furthermore, a homology model of RugG complexed with
NADPH was generated using I-TASSER, an online platform
for protein structure and function prediction.43 The crystal
structure of rat aﬂatoxin aldehyde reductase (PDB: 1GVE)
sharing a relatively high (42%) sequence identity with RugH
was ranked as the top threading template by the I-TASSER for
modeling, and the COFACTOR server in I-TASSER was used
to predict the NADPH-binding site of RugH.44 In the modeled
RugH-NADPH complex, RugH possesses a typical alpha/beta-
barrel motif, and the conserved tetrad residues were located
near the nicotinamide ring of NADPH, consistent with those
reported for the AKR superfamily (Figure 5C).45 However,
since the RugH substrate, the ketone tautomer of 1, is too
labile to be assayed enzymatically, we conﬁrmed alternatively
the attribution of the conserved tetrad to the catalytic activity
of RugH by coexpressing the mutant rugH and wild-type rugG
in A. oryzae. Mutation of Asp52, Tyr57, and Lys81 residues to
alanine completely abolished the enzymatic activity of RugH
(Figure 5D). The observation was consistent with the catalytic
mechanism proposed for aldo-keto reductases. Namely, the
reductive catalysis by these enzymes involves three conserved
residues (Asp, Tyr, and Lys) near the nicotinamide ring, and of
them, Tyr acts as the general acid with its pKa value being
lowered by its hydrogen bonding with Lys, which is salt-linked

■CONCLUSIONS
In summary, this work has elucidated the skyrin (1) and
rugulosin A (2) biosynthesis and pinpointed the unique
mechanism through which the cage-structured motif of many
bisanthraquinones forms in fungi such as Talaromyces sp.
YE3016. Both 1 and 2 are biosynthetically preconditioned by
the generation of emodin (5) as a common on-pathway
precursor under the catalysis of a nonreducing polyketide
synthase (RugA), a β-lactamase-type thioesterase (RugB), an
anthrone oxygenase (RugE), and a decarboxylase (RugF). The
shared biosynthetic steps allow a single rug gene cluster to
accomplish the molecular construction of the two skeletally
distinct bisanthraquinones. Also notable is the tailoring
enzymes RugG (a cytochrome P450 monooxygenase) and
RugH (an aldo-keto reductase), which coordinate the
conversion of 5 to 1 and 2. The formation of 1 depends on
RugG, which catalyzes the emodin dimerization into the
ketone tautomer (5c) that tautomerizes spontaneously into 1.
But the availability of 5c is also a prerequisite for the rugulosin
A (2) production. It is RugH that siphons, reduces, and
eventually converts 5c into the cage-structured bisanthraqui-
none. RugG appears to be substrate-promiscuous and thus
valuable for expanding the chemodiversity of bisanthraqui-
nones. In aggregation, the work presents the biosynthetic
routes to skyrin (1) and rugulosin A (2), two structurally
complex but strikingly diﬀerent bisanthraquinones, and may
advance the ﬁeld by inspiring the synthetic biology accesses to
bioactive (anthra)quinone dimers of natural and synthetic
origins.
