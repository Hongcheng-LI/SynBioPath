Challenging Aromaticity: Revealing a Thioesterase Domain in a
Fungal Nonreducing Polyketide Synthase Governing the Production
of 3‑Methylene Isochromanone

ABSTRACT: Thioesterase (TE) domain exerts a great influence
over the structure of the final product and TE-released nonreduced
polyketides (nrPKs) retain aromaticity. 3-Methylene isochroma-
nones are lactones with a unique olefin at C3 that disrupts the
aromaticity, whose biosynthetic details are speculative. Our study
unveils the complete biosynthesis of ascochin, in which the
construction of the 3-methylene isochromanone backbone is
achieved by a nonreducing polyketide synthase (nrPKS) alone
and two subsequent oxidations are involved. Intriguingly, the
TEAscD serves as a gatekeeper to direct the product release toward formation of nonaromatic 3-methylene isochromanone, rather
than the typical aromatic product.
T
hioesterases (TEs) are widely involved in the biosyn-
thesis of polyketides or nonribosomal peptides, releasing
the products from carrier protein (ACP or PCP) or CoA
through a two-step mechanism.1,2 The first step is trans-
esterification, in which the substrate acyl chain is transferred
onto the catalytic serine/cysteine residue as a result of attacks
from the hydroxyl on the serine (or thiol for cysteine) on the
electrophilic carbonyl of the substrate thioester. The second
step involves selective intermolecular or intramolecular
nucleophilic attack from functional groups such as hydroxyl,
thiol, enol, and amino groups, resulting in the releasing of the
substrate from TE and formation of the final products.
Therefore, TE exerts great influence over the structure of the
final product.3
One of the commonly observed rules that applies to most
TE released nonreduced polyketide products is to maintain the
aromaticity or conjugation in the final products. An illustrative
example is PksA-catalyzed biosynthesis of norsolorinic acid
anthrone (noranthrone), the polyketide precursor of the
notorious fungal toxin aflatoxin B1.4 The TE domain in
PksA catalyzes a Claisen cyclization, which results in the
formation of an additional aromatic ring. Silencing or deletion
of the TE domain in PksA leads to production of
benzopyrones,4 which follows the Hückel’s rule and is thought
also to be aromatic. TE domains also exhibit the capability to
catalyze the formation of C−O bonds during aromatic
polyketide release by simple hydrolysis (e.g., orsellinic acid
and CJ-20,557),5,6 macrolactone formation (e.g., desmethyl-
lasiodiplodin),7 cross-coupling (e.g., melledonol),8 and pyrone
formation (e.g., the pyrone ring of acremonone I) is formed
through lactonization of doubly C-methylated pentaketide
intermediates catalyzed by the TE domain of the fungal
nonreducing polyketide synthase (nrPKS) AcreC (Scheme 1).9
Other examples for pyrones include the formation of 3-methyl-
6,8-dihydroxyisocoumarin, nor-toralactone and so on (Scheme
1).10,11 Formation of benzopyrones or other aromatic
structures is commonly found as TE-released products from
nrPKSs.
However, 3-methylene isochromanones are a special
subgroup of isocoumarins that disobey this rule. Although
considered to be typical polyketides, this group of lactones
contains the characteristic olefin at C3, which breaks the
aromatic benzopyrone backbone. Examples include antibiotic
clearanol C from Paraphoma radicina, algicidal ascochin (1)
from Ascochyta sp., and antifungal phomopsilactone from fungi
of the genus Phomopsis (Figure 1A).12−15 These bicyclic
compounds obviously lack aromaticity since the conjugation
breaks down at the benzylic carbon, next to which an unusual
exocyclic double bond is located. Chemical synthesis of similar
3-alkylidene-isochromanones is difficult and the few examples
involve transition-metal catalysis.16,17 Their biosynthesis was
hypothesized as either dehydrogenation on the lactone from a

The endophytic fungi from genus Paraphaeosphaeria
(Loculoascomycetes) have been reported as natural producers
for a series of 3-methylene isochromanones such as
lycopodiellactone and sphaerialactonam (Figure 1A),18,20 and
structurally related 3-methyl isocoumarins sporulactone E,
sporulactone F, and acremonone E (Figure 1A).21 Due to their
structural similarity of the isocoumarin and isochromanone
backbones, it is reasonable to assume that they share the same
biosynthetic origin. Acremonone I (Scheme 1) matches the
proposed isocoumarin precursors for the Paraphaeosphaeria
compounds; therefore, we speculated that an AcreC-like
nrPKS would be involved in the biosynthesis of 3-methylene
isochromanones (Figure 1C).
Therefore, we carefully analyzed the genome sequence of the
Paraphaeosphaeria sp. strain SJC-F9 isolated from Herba
lycopodii, among which the highest hit to AcreC is showing
∼57% identity in predicted amino acid sequence of the nrPKS
(Table S3) and the same domain organization as SAT-KS-AT-
PT-ACP-MT-TE. Detailed bioinformatic analysis showed that
there are other biosynthetic genes around the nrPKS gene,
including genes encoding a FAD-dependent oxidoreductase, an
aldo/keto reductase, and a cytochrome P450 monooxygenase
(see Figure 1B and Table S3). Therefore, we predicted that
this cluster is possibly related to the biosynthesis of 3-
methylene isochromanones.
To validate this hypothesis, we took a bottom-up
constructive strategy by expressing all four genes in the
heterologous biosynthetic host Aspergillus nidulans A1145 (A.
nidulans). Comparative analysis with the metabolites produced
by the host transformed with empty vectors (Figure 2A(vi))
revealed that two compounds (1, 2) were produced due to the
introduced genes (Figure 2A(i)). 1 and 2 were then isolated
and characterized by NMR study. Compound 1, the major new
metabolite, was quickly identified as ascochin, according to
identical 1H and 13C NMR data, optical value ([ ]D

(c 0.28, CH2Cl2)) and UV spectra to those in the literature
(Table S4 and Figures S5 and S6).13 Ascochin is a well-
characterized isochromanone with a 3-methylene structure,

X-ray diffraction.13 Compound 2 (C12H12O5) was suggested as
a derivative of 1 (C12H10O5) by reduction on the aldehyde at
the C-5 position, based on comparative analysis of HR-MS

To elucidate the biosynthetic pathway of compounds 1 and
2, we took a “gene knockout-like” strategy in a heterologous
host. Elimination of the oxidoreductase gene ascA (A. nidulans-
ascBCD) led to the exclusive detection of 2 (Figure 2A(ii)),
indicating that 1 is oxidized from 2 by AscA. The strain
without the reductase gene ascB (A. nidulans-ascACD) showed
a nearly identical profile to the parental strain (Figure 2A(iii)),
suggesting that ascB may not be involved in the biosynthetic
pathway of ascochin, which was also verified by the normal
transcription of ascB shown in Figure S3 and feeding
experimental results described lately. Expressing nrPKS gene
ascD alone produced a sole product 3 (Figure 2A(v)), which
was identified as ophioisocoumarin by
1H/13C NMR data
(Table S6 and Figures S11 and S12) and optical rotation
values ([ ]D
20 = +178.0 (c 0.1, MeOH)) according to the
literature.23 Significant quantity of 2 was generated after the
introduction of the P450 gene ascC (A. nidulans-ascCD, Figure
2A(iv)), indicating that AscC is responsible for the
hydroxylation of 3 to generate 2. In addition, we observed
that 4 is the C-11 methylated derivative of 2 in the metabolite
extract of the A. nidulans expressing ascCD (Figure 2A(iv)).
The structure of 4 was determined through HR-MS (Figure
S2) and 1D/2D NMR (see Table S7 and Figures S13−S16;
structural assignments were made with additional information
from gHSQC and gHMBC experiments, and the structure
elucidation was provided in the Supporting Information).
Bioinformatic analysis revealed no methyltransferase within the
asc gene cluster. Subsequently, it was proved that 4 resulted
from a coincidental modification of 2 by an endogenous
methyltransferase in A. nidulans by the feeding experiments
(Figure 3(v and vi)). Methanol was intentionally omitted
during the extraction process to prevent any nonenzymatic
methylation reactions.
Additional feeding experiments were carried out to clarify
the functions of the oxidoreductase AscA and P450 enzyme
AscC. We started from feeding 3 to the culture of A. nidulans
strain expressing ascC, which resulted in consumption of 3 and
production of compounds 2 and 4 (Figure 3(i and ii).
Moreover, coexpression of ascA and ascC in A. nidulans
accompanied by feeding of 3, led to the production of 1
(Figure 3 (iii and iv), confirming the stepwise oxidative
function of AscC and AscA. Until now, our heterologous
expression results support the conclusion that the cytochrome
P450 enzyme AscC catalyzes the conversion of 3 into the 11-
hydroxylated product 2, and further oxidation of 2 by the
FAD-dependent oxidoreductase AscA leads to the production
of 11-aldehyde product 1 (Scheme 2).
To our surprise, compound 3 with a 3-methylene group
could be produced by expressing the nrPKS gene ascD alone
(A. nidulans-ascD, Figure 2A(v)), exhibiting a novel function.
To determine the domain involved in the formation of the 3-
methylene isochromanone, we carried out truncation and
combination experiments on TE domains of AscD and AcreC.
Upon deletion of the TE domain of AscD (AscD-ΔTE), the
enzyme lost the ability to produce compound 3 in A. nidulans,
and no other products were generated (Figure 4A(i)). Note
that the production of acremonone I by the nrPKS AcreC also
relied on the TE-domain-catalyzed lactonization process.9 We
then coexpressed complementary units (PKS-ΔTE and TE) in
A. nidulans in various combinations. Interestingly, the
combination of AscD-ΔTE and TEAscD recovered cognate
catalytic activity and effectively produced 3 (Figure 4A(ii)).
Similarly, in the case of AcreC-ΔTE and TEAscD, 3 was
efficiently produced (Figure 4A(iv)). When we coexpressed
the genes for AscD-ΔTE and TEAcreC in A. nidulans, 5 was
detected in HPLC analysis (Figure 4A(iii)). Detailed analysis
of the 1H and 13C NMR spectra confirmed 5 to be acremonone
I (see Table S8 and Figures S17 and S18).9,24 These findings
provide compelling evidence that TEAscD possesses the
distinctive ability to specifically catalyze the formation of
isochromanone with a 3-methylene group.
To assess the similarity of TEAscD to other thioesterases, we
initially used Synthaser25 to extract the 676 TE domain
sequences from fungal nrPKSs obtained from UniProt.

Similar to the results observed in the phylogenetic tree analysis
(Figure S4), the sequences were roughly classified into five
categories. Notably, TEAscD, along with TEAcreC, were found to
cluster in family 2 with the TE domains of nrPKSs catalyzing
the formation of orsellinic acid derivatives, including TEPreu3,
TEStbA, TEDrcA, TEMpaC, etc. Further SSN analysis specifically
focusing on family 2 revealed a closer correlation between
TEAscD and TEAcreC (Figure S19). We would believe that a key
mutation on TEAscD would explain its special function. To
investigate if the same catalytic triad residues are involved in
TEAscD-catalyzed reactions, a mutation of the S183 residue
within the GxSxG motif26 was introduced by replacing the
residue with alanine. This mutation resulted in the loss of
TEAscD activity, as evidenced by the failure to form compound
3 (Figure 4B). Energetic calculation of the two different
resulting molecules by TEAscD and TEAcreC revealed that the
free energy of 3 was indeed 5.5 kcal/mol higher than that of 5
(Scheme 2), which matches our predicted stability (aroma-
ticity) on 5. Further calculation on the analogues of the
proposed intermediates (6, 7, and 8) also disclosed that
intermediate toward 3 had a higher free energy (Figure 5). We
would deduce that the conversion from the polyketide chain
on the PKS to the resulting lactone 3 is a kinetically controlled
process that quickly leads to the formation of a thermodynami-
cally less-stable product. Therefore, we speculate that the
catalytic mechanism of TEAscD involves an initial step of
classical catalysis by the catalytic triad Ser183/Asp218/His376 for
transacylation that starts with nucleophilic attack from the
Ser183−OH group on the thioester carbonyl carbon of the
matured ACPAscD tethered polyketide intermediate (Figure
S20). The departing thiolate of the ACP-Pant deprotonates the
catalytic His376 to allow the catalytic cycle to continue.
Subsequently, His376 likely facilitates deprotonation of C9 to
form an enolate, enabling the further nucleophilic attack on the
carbonyl of the TE tethered ester and leading to lactonization
as a typical TE-catalyzed process. During the catalytic process,
the TE in AscD would stabilize the polyketide as an isolated
enolate on C-3 rather than a conjugated structure toward the
neighboring aromatic ring, and the 3-methylene group is
formed by a regular ester exchange, as shown in Scheme 2.
In conclusion, our research has revealed a distinctive TE
domain within the nrPKS AscD, which catalyzes a kinetically
controlled process that defies aromaticity and provides
significant insights into the biosynthetic pathway of 3-
methylene isochromanones. The nrPKS AscD alone is
adequate for the formation of the 3-methylene isochromanone
core structure, i.e., ophioisocoumarin (3). The cytochrome
P450 monooxygenase AscC and FAD-dependent oxidoreduc-
tase AscA then act as enzymes responsible for oxidizing 3 to 2
and 2 to 1, respectively. Results from domain swapping,
mutagenesis, and DFT calculation provide evidence that the
TE domain in AscD contributes crucially in directing the
