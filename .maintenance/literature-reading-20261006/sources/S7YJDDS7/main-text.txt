A Flavin-Dependent Oxygenase Catalyzes Hydroxylation and
Simultaneous Pyrrolidine Ring Formation in Protubonine

Supporting Information
ABSTRACT: The hydroxylated and diacetylated cyclo-L-Trp-L-
Leu derivative (−)-protubonine B was isolated from a culture of
Aspergillus ustus 3.3904. Genome mining led to the identification of
a putative biosynthetic gene cluster coding for a bimodular
nonribosomal peptide synthetase, a flavin-dependent monooxyge-
nase, and two acetyltransferases. Heterologous expression of the
pbo cluster in Aspergillus nidulans showed that it is responsible for
the formation of the isolated metabolite. Gene deletion experi-
ments and structural elucidation of the isolated intermediates
confirmed the biosynthetic steps. In vitro experiments with the
recombinant protein proved that the flavin-dependent oxygenase is
responsible for stereospecific hydroxylation at the indole ring
accompanied by pyrrolidine ring formation.
F
ungi are recognized as a wide resource for the discovery of
new natural products including peptide derivatives.1
Within the nonribosomal peptides (NRPs), cyclodipeptides,
most possessing a 2,5-diketopiperazine scaffold, represent the
smallest peptides derived from two amino acids. These simple
molecules display diverse biological and pharmacological
activities.2 In fungi, cyclodipeptides are mainly formed by
bimodular NRP synthetases (NRPSs). Numerous tailoring
enzymes in the NRP pathways are responsible for the
modification of the NRPS products, e.g., by redox reactions
or transfer of prenyl, methyl, or acetyl moieties.3−5 Many
cyclodipeptide derivatives isolated from the genera Aspergillus
and Penicillium belong to the group of indole alkaloids with a
diketopiperazine ring.6 These tryptophan-derived natural
products are of interest due to their structural characteristics
and biological activities such as antimicrobial, antiviral,
immunomodulatory, and anticancer effects.7
Several tryptophan-containing di- and tripeptides share a
common fused 6/5/5/6 ring system with a hydroxy group at
C-3 of the indoline ring, such as protubonine B from
Aspergillus sp. SF-5044,8 notoamide D from Aspergillus sp.,9
okaramine G from Penicillium simplicissimum,10 and asperlicin
E from Aspergillus alliaceus.11
Oxidoreductases play an essential role in the modification of
backbone skeletons of natural products to increase diversity of
chemical structures and biological activities.12 Like cytochrome
P45013 and α-ketoglutarate-dependent oxygenases,14 flavin-
dependent monooxygenases catalyze redox reactions in the
biosynthesis of diverse natural product classes.15,16 The
isoalloxazine ring system from the cofactor flavin adenine
dinucleotide (FAD) or flavin mononucleotide (FMN) allows
different oxidation states, leading to high reactivity and
formation of reactive oxygenating species.16 Flavin-dependent
monooxygenases (FMOs) are also involved in the biosynthesis
of notoamide D. The enzyme NotB uses a C-2-prenylated
derivative as substrate for the hydroxylation.17 Pyrrolidine ring
hydroxylation in the biosynthesis of the tripeptide derivative
asperlicin E is catalyzed by AspB.18
In this article, we report the elucidation of the biosynthetic
pathway of the tryptophan-derived cyclodipeptide protubonine
B in Aspergillus ustus by heterologous expression in Aspergillus
nidulans and biochemical characterization of a recombinant
protein. The flavin-dependent monooxygenase PboD catalyzes
the key step, i.e., the hydroxylation at C-3 and the formation of
a fused 6/5/5/6 ring system. To the best of our knowledge,
direct hydroxylation of an unmodified tryptophan-containing
cyclodipeptide accompanied by pyrroloindoline ring formation
has not been reported prior to this study.

■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Identification of a Biosynthetic Gene Cluster for
Protubonines. A hydroxylated and diacetylated cyclo-L-Trp-L-
Leu derivative (1) with [M + H]+ at m/z 400.1868 was
isolated from a 7-day-old rice culture of A. ustus 3.3904.
Comparison of its 1H and 13C NMR data (Table S5, Figures
S3 and S4) and specific rotation with literature values revealed
the isolated metabolite 1 to be (−)-protubonine B (note: the
originally reported structure of protubonine B8 was revised to
its C-11 epimer based on total synthesis of both the proposed
and revised structures19). The relative configuration of 1 was
also confirmed by observed correlations between H-10a and
H-2 and between H-10b and H-11 in the NOESY spectrum
(Figure S5). Genome mining using AntiSMASH20 led to the
identification of a putative biosynthetic gene cluster for
(−)-protubonine B, termed the pbo cluster hereafter, in the
genome sequence of A. ustus 3.3904.21 The putative cluster
consists of four genes, pboA−pboD, encoding an NRPS, two
acetyltransferases, and one flavin-dependent monooxygenase
(Figure 1A). The NRPS PboA (KIA75848) with two A-T-C
modules (A: adenylation, T: thiolation, C: condensation)
shows 44% amino acid sequence identity to CirC for the
formation of cyclo-L-Trp-L-Ala.22 Sequence analysis of the first
A domain revealed 50% sequence identity with that for
tryptophan activation in CirC.22 The two putative acetyl-
transferases PboB (KIA75847) and PboC (KIA75846) share
sequence identities of 37% with the N-acetyltransferase AnaAT
and 39% with the O-acetyltransferase AusP, respectively.23,24
The flavin-dependent oxygenase PboD (KIA75845) displays
low amino acid sequence identity to the known flavin-
dependent oxygenases NotB (31%) and AspB (39%) in the
biosynthesis of notoamide and asperlicin E, respectively.17,18
This is consistent with the requirement for the biosynthesis of
(−)-protubonine B. It can be speculated that PboA assembles
the cyclodipeptide cyclo-L-Trp-L-Leu, which is then hydroxy-
lated by PboD and diacetylated by PboB and PboC. However,
the low sequence identities of PboD to NotB and AspB raised
the question about its function as a hydroxylase in the
biosynthesis of (−)-protubonine B.
To prove the involvement of PboA−PboD in the biosyn-
thesis of (−)-protubonine B, we PCR amplified a segment of
14.8 kbp containing their gene sequences in four fragments
from the genomic DNA of A. ustus 3.3904 and cloned these
into the expression vector pYWB225 by homologous
recombination in Saccharomyces cerevisiae.26 The resulting
expression construct pDJ006 was linearized with the restriction
enzyme SwaI and introduced into A. nidulans LO803027 by
polyethylene glycol-mediated protoplast transformation for

N-Acetylation as the Last Step in the Biosynthesis of
(−)-Protubonine B. To figure out the biosynthetic steps and
the reaction order, pboB, pboC, and pboD were sequentially
knocked out in the expression strain A. nidulans DJ06.
Approximately 1.5 kbp fragments of the 5′-UTR and 3′-UTR
regions of the genes pboB and pboC were amplified via PCR
from genomic DNA of A. ustus 3.3904 and cloned into p5HY
and p3YG for the split marker strategy with hygromycin B as a
selection marker.29 In the case of pboD deletion, the sequences
of the selection markers URA3, AmpR, and Afpyro were
amplified via PCR from pYWB1, an analog of pYWB225 with a
pyridoxine selection marker, and assembled with the 1500 bp
fragments of the 5′-UTR and 3′-UTR of pboD in
Saccharomyces cerevisiae HOD114-2B via homologous recom-
bination. The resulting deletion constructs pDJ15 and pDJ16
for pboB, pDJ17 and pDJ18 for pboC, and pDJ24 for pboD
were used for transformation into A. nidulans DJ06, resulting in
transformants A. nidulans DJ08 (ΔpboB), DJ07 (ΔpboC), and
DJ09 (ΔpboD), respectively. All of the deletion mutants were
verified via PCR and cultivated on rice, followed by HPLC
analysis of the crude extracts (Figure 1B).
Deletion of pboB led to the detection of 2 and 3 as the
predominant peaks. Products 3 and 4 were accumulated in the
deletion mutants of pboC and pboD, respectively (Figure 1B).
Isolation and structure elucidation proved that both 2 and 3
are C-3-hydroxylated pyrrolidine derivatives of cyclo-L-Trp-L-
Leu and, therefore, termed protubonines C and D, respectively.
Protubonine C differs from protubonine D by the presence of
an additional acetyl group. The structure of protubonine D was
also confirmed by comparison of its NMR data (Table S5 and
Figures S6−S8) with those of a synthetic sample.30,31 Analysis
of their circular dichroism spectra (Figure S9) confirmed that
all three metabolites 1, 2, and 3 share the same absolute
configuration as given in Scheme 1.
This proved unequivocally that PboC catalyzes the first
acetylation at the hydroxy group and PboB the second one at
N-1 of the indoline ring. Compound 4 from the pboD deletion
mutant was identified as the NRPS product cyclo-L-Trp-L-Leu
by comparison with an authentic standard. These results
provide solid evidence for the proposed biosynthetic pathway
for (−)-protubonine B illustrated in Scheme 1. As speculated
in the introduction, the bimodular NRPS PboA assembles the
cyclodipeptide cyclo-L-Trp-L-Leu, which is then hydroxylated
by PboD accompanied by pyrrolidine ring formation. The O-
acetyltransferase PboC catalyzes the first acetylation, followed
by N-acetylation with PboB.
PboD Is Alone Responsible for the Hydroxylation
and Pyrrolidine Ring Formation. As described above, cyclo-
L-Trp-L-Leu accumulated in the pboD-deficient mutant. To
provide evidence that PboD is alone responsible for the
hydroxylation and the pyrrolidine ring formation, we carried
out biochemical investigations with a purified recombinant
protein. The genomic sequence of PboD consists of three
exons of 178, 599, and 645 bp, interrupted by two introns of
54 and 81 bp. The existence of these introns was confirmed by
sequencing of a PCR fragment amplified from cDNA. PboD
comprises 473 amino acids with a calculated molecular mass of
52.0 kDa.
In addition to NotB and AspB mentioned in the
introduction, PboD also shares sequence homology with
other flavin-dependent monooxygenases, such as FmqB from
Aspergillus fumigatus Af293 (41% identity on the amino acid
level) and NodY2 from Hypoxylon pulicicidum (38%).32 For
biochemical characterization, the coding sequence of pboD was
amplified from cDNA of A. ustus and cloned into the
expression vector pET28a (+) for overexpression in Escherichia
coli BL21. The recombinant N-terminal His6-tagged protein
with a size of 54.2 kDa and a slight yellow color was purified
on Ni-NTA agarose and cobalt resin to near homogeneity
(Figure 2A). Incubation of PboD with compound 4 in the
presence of NADPH resulted in the clear formation of 3,
which was not detected in the incubation mixtures with
NADH, with heat-inactivated PboD, or without NADPH
(Figure 2C). This demonstrated the strict requirement of the
active enzyme and the cofactor NADPH. Determination of the
kinetic parameters showed that the PboD reaction followed the
Michaelis−Menten equation with KM = 0.10 ± 0.01 mM for 4
and a turnover number kcat of 1.00 ± 0.03 s−1 (Figure 2B). The

It can be proposed that the PboD reaction includes the
formation of an indole epoxide or an indole-derived
hydroxyiminium intermediate (Figure S10). Both routes are
initiated by the formation of the C-4a-hydroperoxy-FAD as an
electrophilic oxygen donor, followed by either epoxidation at
the indole ring or formation of the iminium ion. Both
intermediates enable capture by the amide and subsequent C−
N bond formation to obtain the C-3-hydroxylated pyrrolidine
ring. Similar reaction mechanisms have also been proposed for
flavin-dependent monooxygenases involved in the biosynthesis
of other indole alkaloids.11,17
In summary, we identified a biosynthetic gene cluster for the
biosynthesis of (−)-protubonine B in A. ustus. Heterologous
expression and isolation of the accumulated products as well as
biochemical characterization revealed that the formation of
cyclo-L-Trp-L-Leu is catalyzed by the nonribosomal peptide
synthase PboA. The flavin-dependent monooxygenase PboD is
responsible for its hydroxylation at C-3 of the indole ring and
subsequent formation of the pyrrolidine ring. The resulting
product protubonine D is further diacetylated by two
acetyltransferases, PboB and PboC, to form the final product
protubonine B. These findings provide new insights into the
formation of 6/5/5/6-membered ring systems in indole
alkaloid biosynthesis. Although several FMOs have already
been described for catalysis of such reactions, most of the
reported oxidations of the indole moiety are catalyzed by other
oxygenases.15 To the best of our knowledge, no FMO has been
characterized for the oxidation of unmodified indole
diketopiperazines prior to this study.
■EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
General Experimental Procedures. Jasco DIP-370 with the D-
line of the sodium lamp at λ = 589.3 nm was employed to measure the
optical rotations of the isolated compounds. UV data were acquired
using a J-1500 CD spectrometer (Jasco Deutschland GmbH). CD
spectra were taken on a J-1500 CD spectrometer (Jasco Deutschland
GmbH) using a 1 mm path length quartz cuvette (Hellma Analytics).
NMR measurements were performed on a JEOL ECA-500
spectrometer. All spectra were processed with MestReNov.9.0.0
(Mestrelab Research). Chemical shifts are referenced to those of the
solvent signals, i.e., DMSO-d6 at 2.50 ppm for 1H and 39.52 ppm for
13C and CDCl3 at 7.26 ppm for 1H and 77.16 ppm for 13C. LC-MS
analysis was performed on an Agilent HPLC 1260 equipped with a
Bruker microTOF QIII mass spectrometer using a VDSpher PUR100
C18-M-SE column (3 μm, 150 × 2.0 mm, VDS Optilab
Chromatographietechnik GmbH). Separation was performed at a
flow rate of 0.3 mL/min with a 30 min linear gradient from 5% to
100% MeCN in H2O, both containing 0.1% (v/v) HCOOH. Data
collection and analysis were carried out with Compass DataAnalysis
4.2 software (Bruker Daltonik).
Analysis of the extracts was achieved on an Agilent HPLC 1200
with an Agilent Eclipse XDB-C18 column (5 μm, 4.6 × 150 mm) as
described previously.34 The substances were eluted at a flow rate of
0.5 mL/min with a linear gradient from 5% to 100% MeCN in H2O,
both containing 0.1% (v/v) HCOOH, in 45 min. The column was
then washed with 100% MeCN for 5 min and equilibrated with 5%
MeCN in H2O for an additional 10 min. Detection was carried out on
a photodiode array detector, and absorptions at 254 nm are illustrated
in this study. For analysis of enzyme assays, a gradient in 20 min was
used.
Semipreparative HPLC was performed on the same equipment
with a Multospher 120 RP-18 column (5 μm, 10 × 250 mm, VDS
Optilab Chromatographie Technik GmbH) and the same solvents at
a flow rate of 2 mL/min. Separation was done with stepped gradient
elution in the range of 45−70% MeCN in H2O, containing 0.1% (v/
v) HCOOH, for 10−20 min.

Sequence Analysis. Prediction of biosynthetic gene clusters was
carried out using AntiSMASH.20 For further analysis and prediction of
enzyme function, the web-based bioinformatic tools NCBI BLAST
(http://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov), FGENESH,33 and 2nd Find (https://

Strains, Media, and Growth Conditions. Escherichia coli DH5α
and BL21(DE3) cells as well as Saccharomyces cerevisiae HoD114-2B
cells were cultivated as mentioned in a previous protocol.34
Fungal strains used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table
S2. A. ustus 3.3904 was cultivated on rice medium (20 g of Alnatura
long-grain rice with 30 mL of H2O in a 250 mL flask) at 25 °C for
secondary metabolite production.
A. nidulans strains were grown at 37 °C on minimal medium for
sporulation with appropriate supplements as required (0.1% uracil,
0.12% uridine, 5 × 10−5% pyridoxine, and 2.5 × 10−4% riboflavin).
The medium was prepared as described before.34 For checking the
production of secondary metabolites, A. nidulans strains were
cultivated on rice medium (15 g of royal tiger rice with 20 mL of
H2O in a 250 mL flask) at 25 °C.
Extraction of Genomic DNA, RNA, and cDNA Synthesis.
Mycelia of A. ustus and A. nidulans were cultivated in liquid minimal
medium and harvested after 2 or 3 days, dried on paper towels, and
transferred to 2 mL screw cap vials. Isolation of genomic DNA and
RNA and synthesis of complementary DNA were performed as
described before.35
PCR Amplification, Gene Cloning, and Plasmid Construc-
tion. Plasmids used in this study are listed in Tables S3. Primers in
Table S4 were synthesized by Seqlab GmbH. Phusion High-Fidelity
DNA polymerase from New England Biolabs (NEB) was used for
PCR amplification, conducted on a Labcycler from SensoQuest
according to the manufacturer’s manual for temperature profiles.
For cloning of pDJ006, the whole pbo biosynthetic gene cluster
containing the putative genes for KIA75844−KIA75848 and addi-
tional 500 bp at 5′-UTR and 3′-UTR was amplified by PCR in four
fragments of approximately 4 kbp. The fragments were designed to
overlap by approximately 100 bp with each other and by 30 bp with
the BamHI linearized vector pYWB2. Assembly of the vector
backbone and the fragments was carried out by homologous
recombination in Saccharomyces cerevisiae HoD114-2B.26 The
resulting plasmid was analyzed by restriction and linearized with
SwaI prior to the transformation of A. nidulans.
Construction of the deletion constructs pDJ015, pDJ016, pDJ017,
and pDJ018 was performed according to a split marker strategy by
using hygromycin B as a selection marker.29 Hence, 1500 bp
fragments of 5′-UTR and 3′-UTR of the genes for KIA75847 and
KIA75846 were PCR amplified from the genomic DNA of A. ustus
3.3904 individually and cloned into p5HY and p3YG via homologous
recombination in E. coli DH5α,36 respectively. Correct cloning was
confirmed by restriction, and the resulting plasmids were linearized
with DraI for pDJ015 and pDJ018, XbaI for pDJ016, and SpeI and
NheI for pDJ017 prior to the transformation of A. nidulans.
For construction of the pboD deletion construct pDJ024, 1500 bp
fragments of 5′-UTR and 3′-UTR were amplified by PCR from A.
nidulans DJ06, the afpyro fragment was amplified by PCR from
pYWB1, and the vector backbone containing ampR and URA3
elements amplified from pYWB2 were assembled by homologous
recombination in yeast. The resulting deletion construct was analyzed
by restriction and linearized with DraI prior to the transformation of
A. nidulans.
The expression plasmid pDJ033 was constructed by cloning the
coding sequence of pboD into pET28a (+) at the EcoRI restriction
site via homologous recombination in E. coli DH5α. The intron-free
coding sequence of pboD was amplified from A. ustus cDNA. To
prove the correct insertion, the plasmid pDJ033 was analyzed by
restriction and sequencing.
Genetic Manipulation in A. nidulans and Cultivation of
Mutants. For fungal transformation, germination and protoplast
formation as well as preparation of the required solutions, buffers, and
media were carried out according to a protocol described before.37
Different from that, protoplast preparation was performed by using
200 μL of cellulase TXL, 40 μL of chitinase, 8 μL of protease (ASA
spezial enzyme GmbH), and 20 mg of yatalase from Corynebacterium
sp. OZ-21 (OZEKI Co., Ltd.) in 10 mL of osmotic medium. The
obtained protoplasts were diluted in 3−5 mL of STC buffer to get a
protoplast concentration of (1−5) × 108 cells/mL.
For transformation, 100 μL of the protoplast suspension was
incubated with DNA samples (10−20 μg, dissolved in STC-buffer) on
ice for 30−50 min. Then, 1.75 mL of polyethylene glycol solution was
added in three steps (250 μL, 500 μL, and 1 mL) with inverting in
between and incubated at room temperature for 20 min. An 8 mL
amount of STC buffer were added afterward, followed by
centrifugation at 3000g for 3 min. After removal of the supernatant,
the pellet was resuspended in 1 mL of STC buffer and spread on two
plates with SMM bottom medium. The plates were gently overlaid
with SMM top medium. Both bottom and top agar medium contained
the required supplements for selection. After cultivating at 37 °C for
2−4 days, the fungal colonies were transferred onto fresh minimal
medium agar plates for further selection. This step was repeated at
least twice to get genetically pure and stable mutants. The potential
transformants were verified via PCR amplification (Figure S2) and
cultivated on rice at 25 °C for 7 days. The EtOAc extracts were
dissolved in MeOH and analyzed on LC-MS.
Overproduction and Purification of PboD. The expression
plasmid pDJ033 was transferred to E. coli BL21(DE3). Terrific Broth
(TB) medium (2.4% yeast extract, 2.0% tryptone, 0.4% glycerol, 0.1
M phosphate buffer, pH 7.4) was used for cultivation. PboD
overproduction was induced with 1 mM IPTG at 16 °C for 20 h. The
recombinant His6-tagged protein was purified by Ni-NTA affinity
chromatography (Qiagen), followed by an additional purification step
using cobalt resin (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The concentration of
the purified protein was determined using a Nanodrop 2000c
spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific) and analyzed on sodium
dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) with
an acrylamide concentration of 12% (Figure 2A). The protein yield
was approximately 1 mg/L culture.
In Vitro Assays of PboD. To test the enzyme activity of PboD,
the reaction mixtures (10 μL) containing 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5),
5 mM NADPH or NADH, 1 mM cyclo-L-Trp-L-Leu, and 5.4 μM
purified recombinant PboD were incubated at 30 °C for 1 h.
Incubations without protein or with heat-inactivated protein were
used as controls. The reactions were terminated by addition of 40 μL
of MeOH and monitored using LC-MS.
Determination of the Kinetic Parameters. Enzyme assays for
determination of the kinetic parameters for PboD (15 μL) contained
50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 5 mM NADPH, 2.7 μM purified
recombinant PboD, and cyclo-L-Trp-L-Leu at final concentrations from
0.02 to 2 mM. The samples were incubated at 30 °C for 30 min.
MeOH was added to the reaction mixtures, which were then analyzed
on HPLC. The assays were carried out in three independent
experiments. SEMs are given as ± values. Kinetic parameters were
calculated by nonlinear regression and the Michaelis−Menten
equation using the software GraphPad Prism 6.0.
