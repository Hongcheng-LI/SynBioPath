Determination of Key Factors for Selective or Divergent Synthesis

ABSTRACT: Tetrahydroxanthone dimers are fungal products,
among which secalonic acid D (1) is one of the most studied
compounds because of its potent biological activity. Because the
biosynthetic gene cluster of 1 has been previously identiﬁed, we
sought to heterologously produce 1 in Aspergillus oryzae by
expressing the relevant biosynthetic genes. However, our initial
attempt of the total biosynthesis of 1 failed; instead, it produced
four isomers of 1 due to the activity of an endogenous enzyme of
A. oryzae. Subsequent overexpression of the Baeyer−Villiger
monooxygenase, AacuH, which competes with the endogenous
enzyme, altered the product proﬁle and successfully generated 1.
Characterization of the key biosynthetic enzymes revealed the
surprising substrate promiscuity of the dimerizing enzyme, AacuE,
and indicated that eﬃcient synthesis of 1 requires highly selective preparation of the tetrahydroxanthone monomer, which is
apparently controlled by AacuH. This study facilitates engineered biosynthesis of tetrahydroxanthone dimers both in a selective and
divergent manner.
■INTRODUCTION
Natural products with the xanthone scaﬀold are widely
distributed in nature and exhibit wide structural diversity,
and fungi are especially rich sources of xanthone derivatives.1,2
Xanthone natural products are often dimerized or trimerized to
further increase the structural complexity,2 and many dimeric
xanthones display potent biological activity and may contribute
to future drug development. Secalonic acid D (1), produced by
several ﬁlamentous fungi, is a homodimer of blennolide B (2)
and is one of the most intensively studied xanthone dimers in
terms of biological activity; 1 reportedly induces leukemia cell
apoptosis,3 inhibits DNA topoisomerase I,4 and represses
HIF1α/VEGF-mediated tumor angiogenesis.5 Thus, the
biosynthetic investigation on 1 and related metabolites should
contribute to the eﬃcient production and engineered biosyn-
thesis of this important class of natural products.
The biosynthesis of fungal xanthone derivatives has been
investigated at the genetic and enzymological levels by several
research groups, including ours.6−13 It is well understood that
fungal xanthones are biosynthesized via the common
intermediate chrysophanol, and we recently elucidated the
biosynthetic pathways of several tetrahydroxanthone (THX)
monomers starting from chrysophanol.13 Bioinformatic anal-
ysis has identiﬁed putative biosynthetic gene clusters of
secalonic acid D (1) in Aspergillus aculeatus and Penicillium
oxalicum.12 Nevertheless, the biosynthetic gene cluster of 1 has
not been experimentally examined, and production of dimeric
xanthones, including 1, in a heterologous host has not been
reported.
In this study, we sought to reconstitute the secalonic acid D
biosynthesis in a heterologous fungus Aspergillus oryzae based
on the predicted biosynthetic pathway. The introduction of the
relevant biosynthetic genes unexpectedly resulted in the
production of four isomers of 1, which is caused by an
endogenous enzyme of A. oryzae that can utilize chrysophanol.
Further overexpression of the Baeyer−Villiger monooxygenase
(BMVO) AacuH, which selectively oxidizes chrysophanol,
altered the metabolic proﬁle and yielded 1. These observations,
as well as the bioconversion experiments using the dimerizing
enzyme AacuE, demonstrated the broad substrate speciﬁcity of
the enzyme, which can accept more substrates than expected
based on the known metabolites of A. aculeatus. Our study
provides a general strategy for not only selective synthesis but
also divergent synthesis of THX dimers.

■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
To achieve the total biosynthesis of secalonic acid D (1), we
initially evaluated the biosynthesis of 1 in A. aculeatus ATCC
16872 (CBS 172.66),14 in which the biosynthetic gene cluster
for 1 was identiﬁed (Figure 1A).12 On the basis of the previous
bioinformatic analysis and our recent biosynthetic study on
fungal THX monomers,13 the biosynthetic route leading to
secalonic acid D (1) as well as its analogues can be predicted as
follows (Figure 1B). First, chrysophanol is generated in the
same manner as that known for other fungal xanthones and
then undergoes Baeyer−Villiger oxidation catalyzed by AacuH,
which is followed by hydrolysis to aﬀord monodictyphenone
(3). Methylesteriﬁcation of 3 by the methyltransferase AacuQ
gives 4, which is transformed into compounds with the THX
skeleton by the ﬂavin-dependent monooxygenase (FMO)
AacuC, the isomerase AacuP, and the short chain dehydrogen-
ase/reductase (SDR) AacuF or AacuD. AacuF and AacuD
should accept the same compound as a substrate but perform
the ketoreduction with a diﬀerent stereoselectivity, thus
yielding blennolides B (2) and A (5), respectively. In the
ﬁnal step of the biosynthesis, the cytochrome P450
monooxygenase AacuE accepts 2 and/or 5 to conduct the
dimerization reaction to furnish the THX dimers, secalonic
acids D (1), B (6), and F (7), which have been isolated from
A. aculeatus.15
We next focused on the Baeyer−Villiger monooxygenase
(BMVO) AacuH, because AacuH was expected to possess a
somewhat diﬀerent function from that of NsrF involved in the
neosartorin biosynthesis in Aspergillus novofumigatus;10 NsrF
inserts an oxygen atom at two diﬀerent positions of
chrysophanol for the pathway divergence (Figure 1B), whereas
AacuH should have a stricter product selectivity to selectively
aﬀord 3. To investigate the functional diﬀerences between
AacuH and NsrF, the genes encoding these proteins were
individually introduced into the previously constructed A.
oryzae transformant synthesizing chrysophanol.13 The resultant
metabolites were analyzed by LC-MS. The production levels of
3 and 8 were evaluated based on the fragment signals of these
compounds (Figure 2), because the separation of the two
products was found to be diﬃcult. Although this method does
not allow the quantiﬁcation of the two products, the
comparison of the peak areas for 3 and 8 in metabolites
from the two transformants suggested that AacuH harbors a
stricter product selectivity than that of NsrF (Figure 2A, traces
ii and iii). Thus, AacuH should be more suitable for the
selective synthesis of 1 and used in the following experiments.
Subsequently, we sought to reconstitute the biosynthesis of
blennolide B (2) in A. oryzae. To this end, nsrG, nsrK, and nsrQ
from the neosartorin pathway were used instead of AacuQ,
AacuC, and AacuP, respectively, because the nsr genes were
predicted to have the same function as that of their
corresponding genes in A. aculeatus (Figure 1B). Thus, the

To complete the total biosynthesis of secalonic acid D (1),
the P450 gene AacuE was further introduced, which resulted in
the formation of four new products 9−12 (Figure 3A, trace iii,
and Figure 3B), whose molecular formulas are all consistent
with that of secalonic acid D (1). However, surprisingly, none
of the compounds were identical to 1 or even observed in the
extract of A. aculeatus, which only produced 1 and secalonic
acid F (7) in our experimental condition (Figure 3A, trace vi).
To determine the structures of 9−12, these compounds were
isolated from large-scale cultivation. Based on the NMR
analysis as well as speciﬁc rotation measurements, 9 and 11
were determined to be the known bioactive compounds,
rugulotrosin A17 and versixanthone H,18 respectively (Figure
3D). These molecules both contain 5-epi-blennolide C (13) as
a monomeric unit, which is derived from the undesired
Baeyer−Villiger oxidation of chrysophanol. On the other hand,
10 and 12 were characterized to be heterodimers of 13/
(−)-blennolide C (14) and 14/blennolide B (2), respectively
(Figure 3D). The axial chiralities of 10 and 12 were established
using the CD exciton chirality method.18,19 The CD spectra of
10 and 12 both displayed a positive Cotton eﬀect at around
230 nm (Figure S1), indicating that the two benzoyl
chromophores are oriented in a clockwise manner. Thus, the
axial chiralities of 10 and 12 were both determined to be aS,
and all the obtained THX dimers have the same axial chirality.
Compounds 10 and 12 were hereby designated as 10a′-epi-
rugulotrosin A and 10a′-epi-versixanthone H, respectively. To
investigate the possibility that the utilization of the nsr genes
aﬀected the product proﬁle of THX dimers, we introduced
AacuH, AacuQ, AacuC, AacuP, AacuF, and AacuE to the A.
oryzae strain producing chrysophanol. Unfortunately, the
metabolite proﬁle of the new transformant was similar to
that of the strain with nsrG, nsrK, and nsrQ, and 1 was, again,
not observed (Figure 3A, trace iv).
The unsuccessful total biosynthesis of secalonic acid D (1)
and the eﬃcient production of several THX dimers derived
from 8 led us to hypothesize that an endogenous enzyme of A.
oryzae might accept chrysophanol and transform it into 8. To
investigate this hypothesis, the metabolites of the A. oryzae
transformant without the BVMO gene (A. oryzae/nsrBCEDR-
JI) were analyzed by LC-MS, which revealed the production of
both 3 and 8 apparently in a nonselective manner (Figure 2A,
trace iv). Thus, we expected that further overexpression of the
BVMO gene AacuH would decrease the production rate of 8
and increase the supply of blennolide B (2) required for the
biosynthesis of 1. To upregulate the expression level of AacuH,
an additional copy of AacuH was introduced to the A. oryzae
transformant producing 9−12 under the control of the
improved enolase promoter P-enoA14220 combined with the
5′ untranslated region of the Hsp12 gene.21 The metabolite
proﬁle of the resulting transformant was somewhat diﬀerent
from that of the host strain. The production level of 9/10
signiﬁcantly decreased, and importantly, the formation of 1 was
observed, albeit at low yield (Figure 3A, trace v). It should be
noted that the precursors of 9−12, 5-epi-blennolide C (13),
and (−)-blennolide C (14) were not observed at an obvious
level in the transformant synthesizing blennolide B (2) (Figure
3A, trace ii). The expression level of the gene encoding the
endogenous enzyme might perhaps be upregulated under a
certain condition, but this needs to be clariﬁed in future
studies.

production of THXs derived from 8 in A. oryzae, we then

blennolide B (2) was fed to the strain, 2 was eﬃciently
converted into secalonic acid D (1) (Figure 3B, traces i and ii),
suggesting that only AacuE is suﬃcient to synthesize 1 from 2.
We also utilized blennolide A (5) as a substrate, and the
bioconversion generated a new product (Figure 3B, traces iii
and iv). The compound was deduced to be secalonic acid B
(6), which was not observed as a major product in A. aculeatus
(Figure 3B, traces iii and iv). Furthermore, when both 1 and 5
were fed to the strain, only 1 and secalonic acid F (7) were
produced (Figure 3B, traces v and vi), which is consistent with
the metabolite proﬁle of A. aculeatus (Figure 3B, trace vii).
On the basis of the experimental results, it is illuminated that
the dimerizing enzyme AacuE can accept diverse THX
monomers to synthesize both homodimeric and heterodimeric
THXs, including THX dimers that have not been observed in
A. aculeatus. Additionally, our study suggested that AacuE
prefers the substrates in the following order: 5-epi-blennolide C
(13)/(−)-blennolide C (14) > blennolide B (2) > blennolide
A (5). Meanwhile, the bioconversion experiment revealed that
even fewer preferred substrates can be eﬃciently converted to
corresponding dimers by AacuE. Thus, selective biosynthesis
of a desired THX dimer requires precisely controlled
preparation of corresponding THX monomer(s), whereas the
broad substrate speciﬁcity of AacuE indicates the potential of
the enzyme as a biocatalyst to generate diverse dimeric
molecules. Contrary to the substrate promiscuity, AacuE
appears to perform the dimerization reactions with high
regioselectivity and stereoselectivity; all the dimeric THXs
have the 2−2′ linkage and aS axial chirality. This observation is
consistent with our previous work on the dimerizing enzyme in
the neosartorin pathway, NsrP, which also accepts several
substrates but conducts the dimerization in a regioselective and
stereoselective manner.13
In conclusion, in this study, we aimed the selective total
biosynthesis of a medicinally important natural product,
secalonic acid D (1). Although 1 could not be produced in a
selective manner, we have unintentionally achieved the
heterologous production of several bioactive THX dimers in
A. oryzae. Our study has deﬁned the key factors for selective or
divergent biosynthesis of fungal THX dimers. Because we have
already understood the molecular basis for the biosynthesis of
many THX monomers and obtained some dimerase P450s
with diﬀerent functionalities, these biosynthetic genes could
serve as genetic building blocks to construct artiﬁcial metabolic
pathways for unnatural THX dimers. A future synthetic biology
approach would further expand the THX natural product
diversity, which might lead to the discovery of useful drug
candidates.
■EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
General Experimental Procedures. Organic solvents were
purchased from Anaqua (Hong Kong) Co. Ltd., and other chemicals
were purchased from Wako Chemicals Ltd., Thermo Fisher Scientiﬁc,
Sigma-Aldrich, or J&K Scientiﬁc Ltd., unless noted otherwise.
Oligonucleotide primers (Table S1) were purchased from Beijing
Genomics Institute. Synthetic DNA was purchased from GenScript
Biotech Corporation. PCR was performed using a T100 Thermal
Cycler (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc.) with the Phanta Max Super-
Fidelity DNA Polymerase (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd.). Sequence
analyses were performed by Tech Dragon Ltd. Samples for LC-MS
analysis were infected into a Dionex Ultimate 3000 UHPLC system
(Thermo Scientiﬁc) with a micrOTOF-Q II mass spectrometer
(Bruker Daltonics), using an Accucore C18 column (2.6 μm, 4.6 i.d. ×
100 mm; Thermo Scientiﬁc). Analytical HPLC was performed on a
Dionex Ultimate 3000 UHPLC system (Thermo Scientiﬁc), using a
Kinetex 2.6 μm C18 100 Å column (2.1 i.d. × 100 mm; Phenomenex).
Silica gel column chromatography was performed using Wakogel C-
200. Flash chromatography was performed using an Isolera Spektra
One ﬂash puriﬁcation system (Biotage). Preparative HPLC was
performed on a Waters 1525 Binary HPLC pump with a 2998
photodiode array detector (Waters Corporation), using an XBridge
BEH C18 OBD Prep Column (100 Å, 5 μm, 19 i.d. × 250 mm; Waters
Corporation). NMR spectra were obtained at 600 MHz (1H)/150
MHz (13C) with a Bruker Ascend Avance III HD spectrometer, and
chemical shifts were recorded with reference to solvent signals (1H
NMR = CDCl3 7.26 ppm; 13C NMR = CDCl3 77.0 ppm). Optical
rotations were measured with P-2000 Digital Polarimeter (JASCO
Corporation). CD spectra were obtained with a J-1500 Circular
Dichroism Spectrophotometer (JASCO Corporation). Monodicty-
phenone (3) was obtained in our ongoing project, and blennolide A
(5) was prepared in our previous study.13 The glufosinate solution for
the fungal transformation was extracted from Basta (Bayer) as
previously described22 and used at a 50 μL/mL concentration.
Strains. Aspergillus aculeatus CBS 172.66 was purchased from the
Westerdijk Fungal Biodiversity Institute and used as a source for the
cloning of each gene in the Aacu cluster. Aspergillus oryzae NSAR1
(niaD−, sC−, ΔargB, adeA−)23 was utilized as the fungal heterologous
expression host. Standard DNA engineering was performed with
Escherichia coli DH5α (Takara Bio Inc.).
Construction of a New Fungal Transformation Vector with
a Strong Promoter. To construct a new fungal transformation
vector harboring P-enoA14220 combined with the 5′ untranslated
region of the Hsp12 gene,21 the DNA sequence shown in Figure S2
was synthesized. This DNA fragment was ampliﬁed using the primer
PenoA-F/PenoA-R (Table S1), which was ligated into the BamHI-
digested pTAex3 vector.24 The resultant vector was hereby designated
as pTAex3A.
Construction of Fungal Transformation Plasmids. To
construct fungal expression plasmids for A. oryzae, AacuH, AacuQ,
AacuC, AacuP, AacuF, and AacuE were ﬁrst ampliﬁed from the
genomic DNA of A. aculeatus CBS 172.66 with the primers described
in Tables S1 and S2. Each ampliﬁed DNA fragment was then
introduced into the pTAex3 vector,24 using a ClonExpress Ultra One
Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd.). AacuH was also
introduced into the pTAex3A vector (Table S2). Meanwhile, nsrQ
was ampliﬁed from the genomic DNA of A. novofumigatus IBT 16806
and ligated into the pUNA vector25 to yield pUNA-nsrQ (Tables S1
and S2). Subsequently, DNA fragments harboring the amyB promoter
(PamyB) or P-enoA142/Hsp12 5′-UTR and the amyB terminator
(TamyB) were ampliﬁed from the pTAex3-based plasmids and further
introduced into pPTRI,26 pUNA-nsrQ, or pBARI, as summarized in
Table S2.
Fungal Transformation. Transformation of A. oryzae was carried
out by the previously reported protoplast−polyethylene glycol
method,27 and the transformants created in this study and the
plasmids used for the transformation are given in Table S3. Brieﬂy, for
the introduction of AacuH, nsrG, nsrK, nsrQ, AacuF, and AacuE, the A.
oryzae NSAR1 transformant expressing nsrB, nsrC, nsrE, nsrD, nsrR,
nsrJ, and nsrI13 was transformed using the plasmid, pPTRI-AacuH
+nsrG+nsrK. The resultant transformant was further transformed with
the plasmid, pUNA-nsrQ+AacuF+AacuE, to construct the 13 gene
expressing transformant. Construction of the transformant with
AacuH, AacuQ, AacuC, AacuP, AacuF, and AacuE was performed in
a similar manner using the plasmids, pPTRI-AacuH+AacuQ+AacuC
and pBARI-AacuP+AacuF+AacuE. To construct the transformants
not expressing one or more genes, some of the plasmids were not
used, or the plasmids containing only one or two genes were used
upon performing the transformation.
LC-MS and HPLC Analysis of Each Product from A. oryzae
Transformants. LC-MS analysis was performed with a solvent
system of 35% aqueous acetonitrile containing 20 mM formic acid, at
a ﬂow rate of 1.0 mL/min and a column temperature of 40 °C.

Analytical HPLC was performed with a solvent system of 20 mM

Isolation of Each Metabolite. To isolate each metabolite, A.
oryzae transformants were cultivated on 100 DPY agar plates [2%
dextrin, 1% hipolypepton (Nihon Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd.), 0.5%
yeast extract, 0.5% KH2PO4, 0.05% MgSO4·7H2O, and 1.5% agar] for
7 days at 30 °C, whereas A. aculeatus CBS 172.66 was cultivated on
100 malt extract agar (MEA) plates for 7 days at 25 °C. The resulting
fungal cultures, including agar medium, were crushed into small
pieces, soaked in ethyl acetate, and extracted twice using an ultrasonic
bath. After ﬁltration, ethyl acetate was removed in vacuo. The residue
was fractionated by silica-gel column chromatography or ﬂash
chromatography and further puriﬁed by preparative HPLC.
Puriﬁcation methods for each compound are described in detail
below.
Puriﬁcation Conditions for Secalonic Acids D (1) and F (7). The
extract from A. aculeatus CBS 172.66 (270 mg) was subjected to ﬂash
chromatography with Biotage Sfär Silica 60 μm (50 g) and eluted
stepwise using a chloroform:ethyl acetate gradient. Fractions that
contained 1 and 7 were further puriﬁed by reverse-phase preparative
HPLC (60% aqueous acetonitrile containing 0.05% TFA, 10.0 mL/
min) to yield 0.5 mg of 1 and 0.8 mg of 7.
Puriﬁcation Conditions for Blennolide B (2). The extract from A.
oryzae NSAR1 with nsrB, nsrC, nsrE, nsrD, nsrR, nsrJ, nsrI, AacuH,
nsrG, nsrK, nsrQ, and AacuF (300 mg) was subjected to silica-gel
column chromatography and eluted stepwise using a chloroform:me-
thanol gradient (100:0 to 90:10). Fractions that contained 2 were
then puriﬁed by reverse-phase preparative HPLC (60% aqueous
acetonitrile containing 0.05% TFA, 10.0 mL/min) to yield 19.5 mg of
2.
Puriﬁcation Conditions for Rugulotrosin A (9), 10a′-epi-
Rugulotrosin A (10), Versixanthone H (11), and 10a′-epi-
Versixanthone H (12). The extract from A. oryzae NSAR1 with
nsrB, nsrC, nsrE, nsrD, nsrR, nsrJ, nsrI, AacuH, nsrG, nsrK, nsrQ, AacuF,
and AacuE (2.92 g) was subjected to silica-gel column chromatog-
raphy and eluted stepwise using a dichloromethane:ethyl acetate
gradient (100:0 to 60:40). Fractions that contained 9−12 were then
puriﬁed by reverse-phase preparative HPLC (60% aqueous acetoni-
trile containing 0.05% TFA, 10.0 mL/min) to yield 6.4 mg of 9, 4.1
mg of 10, 10.8 mg of 11, and 3.1 mg of 12.
Bioconversion Experiments. To perform the bioconversion
experiments using an AacuE-expressing strain, A. oryzae NSAR123 was
transformed with pTAex3-AacuE. The resultant transformant was
initially cultivated in 5 mL of DPY medium at 30 °C and 220 rpm for
3 days and then transferred to 35 mL of DPY medium containing 6.25
mg/L of blennolide B (2) and/or blennolide A (5). After cultivation
at 30 °C and 140 rpm for 3 days, medium and mycelium were
separated by ﬁltration. The mycelium was extracted with acetone
using an ultrasonic bath, concentrated, and re-extracted with ethyl
acetate. The extracts were analyzed by HPLC as described above.
