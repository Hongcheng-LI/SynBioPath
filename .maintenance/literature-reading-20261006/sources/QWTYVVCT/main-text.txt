ABSTRACT: Paclitaxel is a famous chemotherapeutic agent, but
its microbial production poses a long-standing challenge due to its
poor product selectivity. Taxadiene-5α-hydroxylase (CYP725A4)
plays a crucial role in the biosynthesis of paclitaxel, catalyzing the
oxidation of taxadiene and iso-taxadiene. This process yields several
products, including the byproducts 5(12)-oxa-3(11)-cyclotaxane
(OCT) and 5(11)-oxa-3(11)-cyclotaxane (iso-OCT), as well as the
target compound taxadien-5α-ol (T5OH). Despite extensive
studies, the molecular mechanism of CYP725A4-catalyzed trans-
formations is still elusive, which could impede our understanding of
further engineering of the paclitaxel biosynthetic pathway. In this
study, the crystal structure of CYP725A4 in complex with taxadiene
is elucidated. Through comprehensive computational analyses, the catalytic mechanisms of CYP725A4 in the biosynthesis of natural
paclitaxel are deciphered. Our calculations indicate that the oxidation of taxadiene affords a zwitterion intermediate, which can
undergo two competing transformation routes. One involves the formation of epoxide, which further undergoes the water-mediated
rearrangement to form the T5OH product. In the alternative pathway, protonation of the oxygen in the zwitterion intermediate
facilitates subsequent hydride transfer and carbon−oxygen coupling, resulting in the side products OCT/iso-OCT. Contrary to
taxadiene, hydroxylation at C5 of iso-taxadiene directly yields the target product T5OH. These crystallographic studies and
computational analyses have yielded valuable insights into the catalytic mechanisms of CYP725A4 and laid the foundation for the
further engineering of CYP725A4.
KEYWORDS: P450, paclitaxel biosynthesis, QM/MM, taxadiene-5α-hydroxylase, crystallography
■INTRODUCTION
Paclitaxel, a globally recognized anticancer chemotherapeutic
agent, is originally derived from the bark of the Pacific yew tree
(Taxus brevifolia).1−4 To date, research has characterized
approximately 16 enzymes that are involved in the biosynthetic
pathway of paclitaxel.5,6 It is generally recognized that the first
two enzymes along the pathways constitute the bottleneck for
the efficient biosynthesis of paclitaxel. The first enzyme, named
taxadiene synthase (TS), catalyzes the cyclization of
geranylgeranyl pyrophosphate (see Scheme 1),7−10 affording
the major product taxadiene and the minor iso-taxadiene at
yields of 92% and 8%, respectively.11−14 The enzyme
CYP725A4, known as taxadiene-5α-hydroxylase,15,16 catalyzes
the hydroxylation at the C5 position of taxadiene, affording
taxadien-5α-ol (T5OH). Nevertheless, T5OH that is required
for the downstream synthesis of paclitaxel just constitutes a
minor product of the CYP725A4 catalysis.17−19 Instead, the
reaction of CYP725A4 leads to the major but the undesired
product 5(12)-oxa-3(11)-cyclotaxane (OCT).17,18,20,21
Interestingly, it was found that CYP725A4 can act on both
isomers of taxadiene.14,22 Especially, CYP725A4-catalyzed
oxidation of taxadiene affords the major product OCT and
the minor products comprising T5OH and other side
products,17,18,20,21 while CYP725A4-catalyzed oxidation of
iso-taxadiene can exclusively lead to the product T5OH.
Despite extensive research, the molecular mechanism under-
lying the CYP725A4-catalyzed transformations remains
elusive, which may impede our understanding of further
engineering of the paclitaxel biosynthetic pathway to improve
the selectivity and yield of key intermediates for paclitaxel
biosynthesis.4,21,23−26

preference for epoxidation over hydroxylation in CYP725A4
remain elusive.
In addition to T5OH, the mechanisms underlying OCT
formation remain largely unexplored. To date, several
mechanistic pictures have been proposed.18,20 One widely
proposed mechanism (Scheme 3) involves the attack of Cpd I
onto the C5 site, which is followed by an inner-sphere ET to
generate the C4-centered carbon cation radical species. Then,
the sequential hydride transfer from C3 to C4 (3 →4), C3−
C11 bond formation (4 →5), and C12−O bond formation
would afford the side product OCT.20 Alternatively, OCT
could be rearranged in water,20,43 whereas the process could be
assisted by acid−base catalysis similar to that of T5OH
formation.
To solve the aforementioned issues, the combinations of X-
ray crystallography, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations,
quantum mechanical/molecular mechanical (QM/MM) cal-

be demonstrated, the crystal structure of CYP725A4 in
complex with taxadiene provided rich structural insights for
CYP725A4-mediated transformations. Further computational
studies demonstrated that the oxidation of taxadiene affords a
zwitterion intermediate, which either converts to the T5OH
product via the epoxide intermediate or leads to the side
products OCT/iso-OCT via the O-protonation of the
zwitterion intermediate. For the iso-taxadiene oxidation, the

■METHODS
System Setup. The taxadiene-containing structure used for
the simulations was constructed from the crystal structure
characterized in our work. The iso-taxadiene-containing
structure was constructed by docking with webvina software,44
for which a cavity box within 5 Å of the original taxadiene
substrate was selected for docking. Each docking iteration
produces 100 poses, with an exhaustive sampling value set to 8.
We assigned the protonation state of titratable residues (His,
Glu, Asp) based on their pKa values in PROPKA software.45
One oxygen atom was manually added above the heme-Fe to
model the Cpd I species. The “MCPB.py” tool from
AmerTools18 was used to parametrize Fe-containing active
sites.46−48 The Amber ff14SB force field49 was used for protein
residues. The general AMBER force field (GAFF)50 was used
for the substrate, while partial atomic charges were obtained by
the BCC method of AmberTools18.48 Finally, the system was
solvated in a rectangular box containing TIP3P water located
16 Å from the protein surface.
Classical MD Simulations. The entire system was
minimized using a combination of the steepest descent
method and the conjugate gradient method. Afterward, the
system was gently annealed from 0 to 300 K for 50 ps under
the canonical ensemble with a weak constraint of 25 kcal mol−1
Å−1 on the substrate and protein. Under the NPT ensemble,
with a target temperature of 300 K and a target pressure of 1.0
atm, density equilibration was performed for 1 ns to achieve a
uniform density after heating dynamics. After removing all of
the constraints on protein, we equilibrated the system under
the NPT ensemble for 4 ns to obtain stable pressure and
temperature conditions. Finally, we performed 100 ns MD
simulations under the NPT ensemble. In all MD simulations,
SHAKE51 was used to constrain hydrogen-containing covalent
bonds, with a step integration of 2 fs. All MD simulations were
conducted utilizing the GPU version of the Amber 18 software
package.48
QM/MM Methodology. For each system, the representa-
tive snapshot extracted from the classical MD trajectory was
used for subsequent QM/MM calculations. All QM/MM
calculations were conducted with ChemShell,52,53 which
interfaces with Turbomole54 for the QM section and
DL_POLY55 for the MM section. The QM/MM coupling
was treated by the electronic embedding scheme,56 while the
QM/MM boundary was treated with hydrogen link atoms with
the charge-shift model. UB3LYP functionals were used for the
QM region,57−59 which were demonstrated to be reliable for
Fe-containing enzymes.29,60−74 The double-ζ basis set def2-
SVP(B1) was used to optimize the geometry, while the larger
basis set of def2-TZVP(B2) was used to correct the energies.75
Grimme’s D3 method was employed to account for dispersion
corrections in all quantum mechanical calculations.76−78 The
QM region in the system included the substrate, the
propionate-truncated heme, and the side chain of Cys383.
The final energies were reported as the single-point electronic
energies at the UB3LYP/B2 level plus zero-point energy
(ZPE) at the UB3LYP/B1 level. All of the transition states
(TSs) were located by relaxed potential energy surface (PES)
scans and determined as the highest point on the potential
energy surface, for which a small increase of 0.02 Å was used
for scanning near the transition states. Then, the highest point
was subjected to the full TS optimizations, using the dimer
optimizer implemented in the DL-FIND code.79
QM Calculations. Density functional theory (DFT)
calculations were performed using Gaussian 09 software.80
For the generation of byproducts OCT and iso-OCT, since
some of the transition state structures can not be located with
UB3LYP, the PBE0 functional has been used,81 while for the
rearrangement of the epoxide intermediate in an aqueous
solution, the UB3LYP functional was used. All geometries were
optimized with the basis set of def2-SVP, while the energies
were further refined with the larger basis set of def2-TZVP.75
The dispersion corrections computed with Grimme’s D3
method76−78 were considered in all QM calculations. All
geometry optimizations and single-point calculations were
conducted under the Truhlar’s universal solvation model
(SMD).82

Construction of CYP725A4 Expression Plasmids.
CYP725A4 was synthesized by GenScript (Nanjing, China)
with codon optimization for Escherichia coli. The 42aa N-
terminal transmembrane domain of CYP725A4 was replaced
with codons for an optimized sequence “MAKKTSSKGK”, and
6× His tag was added to the C-terminal to facilitate
purification. A DNA fragment encoding this construct was
inserted into the NdeI and XbaI sites of the pCWori+ vector.
Expression and Purification of Recombinant
CYP725A4 Protein. The plasmid was transformed into
Trans-Blue competent cells following the manufacturer’s
specification. One fresh colony grown on LB plates containing
ampicillin-Na (100 μg/mL) and tetracyclin-HCl (10 μg/mL)
was inoculated into 50 mL of an LB medium containing
ampicillin-Na (100 μg/mL) and tetracyclin-HCl (10 μg/mL)
and incubated at 37 °C, 220 rpm. Then, 5 mL aliquots of
overnight culture were inoculated into 500 mL of the TB
medium containing ampicillin-Na (100 μg/mL) and tetracy-
clin-HCl (10 μg/mL) in the 2 L Erlenmeyer flasks and
incubated at 37 °C, 220 rpm. Approximately 5 h later, the
protein expression was induced with 0.5 mM IPTG and
0.5 mM ALA when OD600 reached 0.6−0.8. Incubation was
continued with shaking at 32 °C, 180 rpm. Ampicillin (100 μg/
mL) was added in each 24 h incubation. The cells were
harvested 48 h later by centrifugation at 5000g, 4 °C for
10 min, and stored at −20 °C in a freezer.
Harvested cells were thawed in lysis buffer (500 mM
potassium phosphate, pH 7.4, 250 mM NaCl, 0.25% sodium
cholate, 10% glycerol, 10 mM imidazole, 10 mM β-mercaptoe-
thanol) on ice. Cells were lysed by sonication on ice for
30 min, and PMSF was supplemented to a final concentration
of 0.2 mM to inhibit protease. Cell debris was removed by
centrifugation at 29,000 g, 4 °C for 20 min. The supernatant
was applied to a Ni2+-NTA affinity column equilibrated with
lysis buffer at 4 °C. The column was washed with (1) 50
column volumes of buffer A (10 mM potassium phosphate, pH
7.4, 500 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 20 mM imidazole, 10 mM β-
mercaptoethanol) and (2) 100 column volumes of buffer B
(10 mM potassium phosphate, pH 7.4, 500 mM NaCl, 40 mM
imidazole, 10 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 10% glycerol). The
protein was then eluted with elution buffer (10 mM potassium
phosphate, pH 7.4, 500 mM NaCl, 250 mM imidazole, 10%
glycerol, 10 mM β-mercaptoethanol) and checked by SDS-
PAGE. Fractions with the highest purity were pooled for
further purification by size exclusion chromatography on a
HiLoad 16/60 Superdex 200 column with buffer C (10 mM
potassium phosphate, pH 7.4, 500 mM NaCl, 0.2 mM EDTA,
1 mM DTT, 10% glycerol). The fractions with the highest
concentration of CYP725A4 protein were pooled and
concentrated with a Vivaspin 20 concentrator (30 kDa
molecular-weight cutoff, Millipore, USA). The purity of
CYP725A4 was judged by SDS-PAGE and the concentration
of purified CYP725A4 was measured by the BCA method.
Crystallization. The initial crystallization screening was
carried out at 16 °C in 48-well plates using sitting-drop vapor-
diffusion with the commercial crystal screen kits, including
Crystal Screen, Crystal Screen 2, PEG/Ion Screen, and Index
from Hampton Research and Precipitant Synergy P64 from
Rigaku company. The droplets consisted of 1 μL of 40 mg/mL
purified CYP725A4 in gel filtration buffer (10 mM potassium
phosphate, pH 7.4, 500 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, 10% (v/v)
glycerol) and 1 μL of the precipitant solution and were
equilibrated against 300 μL of the precipitant solution. Crystals
were obtained in several conditions from initial screening trials.
To improve the quality of the crystals and save time for
crystallization, optimization of crystallization conditions was
performed using the hanging-drop vapor-diffusion method
with the additive screen kits from Hampton Research. The
crystals were immersed in a cryoprotectant solution (1.6 M
sodium citrate, pH 6.5, 25% (v/v) glycerol) before being flash-
cooled in liquid nitrogen for data collection.
For preparing crystals of CYP725A4 complexed with the
substrate, 0.1 μL of 165 mM taxadiene in DMSO was added to
the droplets containing the crystals and incubated against the
reservoir solution at 16 °C for 1.5 h. The crystals were
immersed in a cryoprotectant solution (1.6 M sodium citrate,
pH 6.5, 25% (v/v) glycerol), and then flash-cooled in liquid
nitrogen.
Data Collection and Structure Determination. Dif-
fraction data were collected on beamlines 17U183 and 19U1 at
the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility for crystals of
ligand-free CYP725A4 and CYP725A4 in complex with
substrate taxadiene, respectively. Data were processed with
XDS and HKL2000. The structure of CYP725A4 was solved
by molecular replacement with the structure of CYP120A1
(PDB code: 2VE4) as a search model. Iterative model building
and refinement were performed using COOT84 and
PHENIX,85 respectively. The CYP725A4/taxadiene complex
structure was solved by molecular replacement using the
CYP725A4 structure as a search model. The ligand was added
with a ligand model obtained from canonical SMILES of
taxadiene.
The figures were prepared in the molecular graphics system
PyMOL.86 Data collection and refinement statistics are given
in Table S1. The coordinates have been deposited in the
Protein Data Bank (PDB codes: 8X1W for CYP725A4, 8X3E
for CYP76AH1 complex).
Identification of Key Residues of CYP725A4. Cyto-
chrome P450 reductase derived from Taxus cuspidate
(TcCPR) was N-terminus truncated and synthesized by
GenScript (Nanjing, China) with codon optimization for E.
coli. The truncated CYP725A4 and TcCPR were fused with the
GSTGS tag, and a small peptide “MALLLAVF” was added to
the N-terminal of truncated CYP725A4 to further improve the
solubility of fusion protein.
The fused gene was cloned to vector pCDFDuet by Gibson
assembly, and this plasmid was used as a template to construct
alanine mutations. The recombinant plasmids were trans-
formed into strain T1 (chromosome-level genome of
Himalayan yew provides insights into the origin and evolution
of the paclitaxel biosynthetic pathway), which produced high-
yield taxadiene previously constructed in the laboratory. The
single colony was incubated in 5 mL of an LB medium
overnight with ampicillin (100 μg/mL) at 37 °C and was
transferred into 50 mL of a 2× YT medium with 100 μg/L
ampicillin, 50 μg/L kanamycin, 34 μg/L chloramphenicol, and
34 μg/L streptomycin. When the OD600 reached 0.6−0.8, 0.1
mM IPTG was added to the culture at 16 °C for 18 h, which
were then cultivated for additional 4 days at 28 °C. The culture
were extracted twice with an equal volume of hexane. The
organic phases were collected and completely evaporated. The
dry residue was resolved with hexane and was transferred to
sample vials for GC-MS.

■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Crystal Structure of CYP725A4 in Complex with
Taxadiene. We have elucidated the structural foundation for
the transformations catalyzed by CYP725A4 by determining its
crystal structure in complex with the substrate taxadiene at a
high resolution of 1.68 Å. Figure 1b illustrates that taxadiene is
positioned above the heme molecule, and the C5 and C20
atoms of the substrate are located near heme-Fe, with distances
of 4.3 and 3.8 Å from heme-Fe, respectively. The substrate-
binding model shows that the stabilization of the substrate
mainly arises from the hydrophobic interactions with
surrounding residues. To ascertain crucial residues for
substrate binding, we conducted alanine scanning experiments
for residues located within a 5 Å distance from the substrate
taxadiene (Figure 1b). We tested the activity of different
mutants in the production chassis of taxadiene (Figure 1c and
Supporting Figure S1). The GC-MS results revealed that
mutations in the following residues, namely M123, W125,
F129, M133, L225, F229, and H305, almost completely
deactivated the enzyme. These residues are positioned in the
first shell of the substrate-binding pocket and play significant
roles in binding and positioning the substrate taxadiene.
Structural analysis further confirmed the positive influence on
substrate binding brought about by the hydrophobic
interactions of these key residues (Figure 1b). In contrast,
other mutations, including S302, L303, T310, P373, V374, and
P484, did not deactivate the enzyme. This suggests that these
residues are not directly involved in the binding of the
substrate. Specifically, the mutation of S302A had almost no
influence on the activity of T5OH.
CYP725A4-Catalyzed Oxidation of Taxadiene. Based
on our characterized crystal structure of CYP725A4 with
taxadiene, further MD simulations and QM/MM calculations
have been conducted to investigate the Cpd I-mediated
oxidation of taxadiene. Unlike the configuration depicted in the
crystal structure, our MD simulations reveal that the C5 site is
closer to the oxo group of the FeIV=O species compared to the
C20 site (Figure S2). For comparison, we also performed two
replicates of the 400 ns MDs, which yielded consistent results
showing that the C5 site is closer to the FeIV=O species than
that of the C20 site (Table S3).

Unlike the crystal structure in the resting state, a new water
chain has been generated and extended to the Cpd I species
(Figures 2 and S5). In addition, the added oxo could exert

displacement of the substrate relative to the crystal structure,
such that the methyl C20 is pushed away from the Cpd I

may either perform HAA from C20 or attack the C4C5
double bond. As shown in Figure 3, the QM/MM calculated
barrier for HAA from C20 is ∼18.7 kcal/mol, while the
electrophilic attack on the C5 site experiences a barrier of 12.2
kcal/mol. Consequently, the HAA/OH rebound mechanism is
unfavored and can be reasonably ruled out. Interestingly, we
found that the electrophilic attack led to the formation of a
charge-shifted zwitterion intermediate (IC1a),88 where the
substrate accumulates a positive charge of 0.97, while the O
site carries a negative charge of −0.69. In line with the
common view, we assume that redox potential of the substrate
is key to the epoxidation reactivity. Due to the strong electron-
donating ability of the alkyl groups in the substrate, the
epoxidation of the double bond can be greatly enhanced,
leading to a relatively low barrier for the process.
Starting from the zwitterion intermediate IC1a, the following
C4−O coupling is found to be facile, which involves a slight
barrier of 0.5 kcal/mol and leads to the epoxide intermediate
(PCa). Starting from IC1a, we also investigated the hydride
transfer from C3 to C4, which may afford the side product
OCT, according to Scheme 3. However, such a process led to
the formation of epoxide (Figure S3), indicating that the
epoxide formation is intrinsically favored over the hydride
transfer from C3 to C4.
Starting from the epoxide intermediate (see Scheme 4), we
further investigated the rearrangement of the epoxide
intermediate in a water solution, where acetic acid was used
to mimic the general acid−base catalysis from the pH buffer.

deprotonation at C20, leading to the T5OH product (the

The subsequent C12−O coupling, which is coupled to the
proton released to acetate, forms the side product OCT.
Clearly, the rearrangement of the epoxide in the pH buffer
would be dominated by the generation of the T5OH product.
Given that acetic acid (a mimic of acid−base catalyst in pH
buffer) can mediate the decomposition of the epoxide with a
very low barrier (7.0 kcal/mol in Scheme 4), we assume that
any general acid−base catalyst in pH buffer may efficiently
mediate such transformations.
Now, we have a clear mechanistic picture of the CYP725A4-
catalyzed conversion of taxadiene into T5OH, where the
reaction involves the epoxide formation, which undergoes the
rearrangement in pH buffer to form the desired product
T5OH. One remaining question is how the side products OCT
and iso-OCT have been generated. As mentioned in the last
section, the direct hydride transfer from C3 to C4 within IC1a
is infeasible (see Figure S3). Inspection of the MD-equilibrated
structure in Figure 2 shows that a well-organized water chain
has been extended to the O atom of Cpd I. In such a case, we
speculate that the water chain may mediate a proton transfer
from the pH buffer to the O anion of the zwitterion IC1a,
leading to the protonated substrate cation species. Since no
suitable proton source can be identified around the substrate
(Figure S8), the proton transfer process may involve many
solvent waters extending from the substrate O atom to the pH
buffer, which can be challenging to study with the QM/MM
approach. As such, we have evaluated these processes with QM
calculations. Figure 4 shows a plausible mechanism for the
generation of side products OCT and iso-OCT from the QM-
model calculations. Our calculations showed that the proton
transfer from acetic acid (a mimic of the proton donor from
the pH buffer) to the O atom of the zwitterion intermediate is
exothermic by 6.7 kcal/mol (IC1a′ →IC2a′). Starting from
IC2a′, the hydride transfer from C3 to C4 can occur
successfully, leading to the C4-centered cation intermediate
IC3a′. The following C3−C11 coupling leads to the
intermediate IC4a′. This process is also quite facile, with a
barrier of 3.3 kcal/mol (IC3a′ →TS2a′). Starting from IC4a′,
two possible routes have been considered. The first, depicted
in blue, involves a C12−O coupling event that is concomitant
with a proton transfer from the hydroxyl group to the acetate
anion, leading to the formation of OCT. In the other pathway
(in red), the reaction is initiated by the alkyl migration from
C11 to C12, leading to IC5a″. The further C11−O coupling,
which is coupled to the proton relay from the OH group to the
acetate anion, generates the side product iso-OCT.
CYP725A4-Catalyzed Oxidation of iso-Taxadiene. In
this section, we proceed to investigate the mechanism of

■DISCUSSION
Plant P450s are crucial in synthesizing and modifying natural
compounds,89−98 encompassing a range of reactions such as
hydroxylation,99−109 epoxidation,110−113 desaturations,114,115

C−O bond formation.131−136 Owing to the limited structural
information, the catalytic mechanisms of many plant p450s are
still elusive. Combining the crystallography, MD, QM/MM,
and QM calculations, we have unraveled the intricate catalytic
mechanisms of CYP725A4 (see Scheme 5), a key enzyme
involved in the biosynthesis of natural paclitaxel. For
CYP725A4-catalyzed oxidation of taxadiene, the attack of
Cpd I onto the C5 site affords a zwitterion intermediate.
Subsequently, two potential pathways emerge. One involves
the conversion of the zwitterion intermediate into epoxide
intermediate, which can undergo the rearrangement in a water
solution to exclusively form the desired product T5OH. The
sequential formation of the epoxide and T5OH intermediates
has also been supported in a recent work.137 In the alternative

computational findings suggest that inhibiting proton transfer
to the zwitterion intermediate could suppress the formation of
these side products, favoring the production of the targeted
compound T5OH. However, the inhibition of such a proton
channel may also inhibit the generation of the Cpd I species, as
both the Cpd I generation and the zwitterion protonation may

the generation of OCT/iso-OCT according to our computa-

Interestingly, recent studies suggested that the T5OH

leading to the formation of an oxetane intermediate.137
However, another recent work demonstrated that the
generation of the oxetane intermediate is mediated by another
novel P450 enzyme.138 The mechanism of the oxetane
formation is worthwhile to gauge with QM/MM in our future
work.
For CYP725A4-catalyzed oxidation of iso-taxadiene, our
calculations show that the reaction follows the HAA/OH-
rebound process, leading to the desired product T5OH.
Though this route is highly efficient for the generation of
T5OH, iso-taxadiene is only a minor product derived from the
catalytic reactions of TS, while the major product goes to
taxadiene. Thus, the engineering of the TS toward efficient
generation of iso-taxadiene can be pivotal to the biosynthesis
of paclitaxel.12,14,139,140
■CONCLUSIONS
Using the combined crystallography, MD simulations, QM/
MM, and QM calculations, we have unraveled the intricate
catalytic mechanisms of CYP725A4 involved in the biosyn-
thesis of natural paclitaxel. The determined crystal structure
showed that the binding of taxadiene results in a hydrophobic
pocket, where the C20 of the substrate is the closest to the Fe
center. However, a water chain can be generated in the Cpd I
state, which can induce the reorientation of the substrate-
binding conformation, bringing the C5 close to the Cpd I
species. Our computational studies indicate that the oxidation
of taxadiene catalyzed by CYP725A4 yields T5OH and side
products OCT/iso-OCT through the same zwitterionic
intermediate, which was generated via the attack of Cpd I
onto the double bond of the substrate. The zwitterion
intermediate may convert to the epoxide intermediate, which
further undergoes the rearrangement in the water solution to
exclusively form the desired product T5OH. Alternatively, the
zwitterion intermediate may undergo the protonation at the O
atom, leading to the substrate cation intermediate. The
following hydride transfer and C−O coupling can occur
within the substrate cation intermediate, resulting in the
formation of side products, namely, OCT and iso-OCT. In
contrast to taxadiene, the positioning of iso-taxadiene favors
the C5-hydroxylation by CYP725A4, yielding T5OH as the
primary product. Consequently, the crystal structure of binary
CYP725A4/taxadiene and the detailed computational analysis
have provided valuable insights into the catalysis of
CYP725A4, which can be instrumental for the biosynthesis
of paclitaxel.
