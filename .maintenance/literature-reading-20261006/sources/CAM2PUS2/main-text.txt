ABSTRACT: Bacterial trans-acyltransferase polyketide synthases (trans-
AT PKSs) synthesize natural products with intricate structures and potent
biological activities. They generally contain various unusual modules or
trans-acting enzymes. Herein, we report the trans-AT PKS-derived
biosynthetic pathway of the shuangdaolide with a rare internal 2-
hydroxycyclopentenone moiety. The multidomain protein SdlR catalyzes
the synthesis of 16,17-epoxide during polyketide chain elongation. The
SdlR contains a ketoreductase, an acyl carrier protein, a flavoprotein
monooxygenase, and a serine hydrolase domain. This online epoxidation
occurs at unusual positions away from the thioester. Then, two tailoring
enzymes, SdlB and SdlQ, convert a methylene to a carbonyl group and
oxidize a hydroxyl group to a carbonyl group, respectively. The following
spontaneous opening of 16,17-epoxide induces the formation of a new C−
C bond to generate the 2-hydroxycyclopentenone moiety. The characterization of the shuangdaolide pathway extends the
understanding of the trans-AT PKSs, facilitating the mining and identification of this class of natural products.
■INTRODUCTION
Trans-acyltransferase polyketide synthases (trans-AT PKSs)
are complex megaenzymes responsible for the production of a
vast diversity of natural products.1 They show a diverse range
of biological activities, such as antibiotic mupirocin and
virginiamycin M1 and antitumor rhizoxin and spliceostatin.2−7
The trans-AT PKS-derived compounds also play important
roles in the bacterial interaction with host or environment,
such as symbiosis8 and pathogenicity.9,10
Trans-AT PKSs are widely distributed throughout the
bacterial kingdom.11 Similar to cis-AT PKSs, they are also
composed of multiple modules harboring various enzymatic
domains.1 Usually, each module is responsible for the addition
and modification of an acyl extender unit. Within one module,
a unit is attached by the acyltransferase (AT) enzyme to the
acyl carrier protein (ACP) domain. The ketosynthase (KS)
domain receives a growing polyketide chain from the ACP
domain in the upstream module and then catalyzes a
decarboxylative Claisen condensation between the unit and
the polyketide chain. The newly synthesized, ACP-bound β-
keto thioester can be further modified by additional domains,
such as a ketoreductase (KR), dehydratase (DH), enoylre-
ductase (ER), methyltransferase (MT), or others, to generate a
product that is translocated onto the KS domain of the
downstream module. Eventually, the full-length polyketide is
released from the PKS by a thioesterase (TE) domain.12
Compared with cis-AT PKS, the trans-AT PKS always lack the
intramodular AT domains, but they can be supplemented by
free-standing trans-acting AT enzymes.13 The module
architecture of cis-AT PKSs often correlates well with the
core structure of the associated polyketide (termed the
colinearity rule), but trans-AT PKSs do not always follow the
rule due to the presence of some domains provided in trans
and nonelongating modules, etc.1 A prediction method for
trans-AT PKS systems based on a close correlation between KS
sequence features and its substrate from the upstream PKS
module was developed previously.14,15 However, the unknown
trans-acting modules or enzymes in trans-AT PKSs may lead to
inaccuracy prediction. Thus, elucidating the function and
mechanisms of these unusual trans-acting enzymes through
experiments is necessary for the further understanding of trans-
AT PKSs, facilitating the mining and identification of more
polyketides.
Shuangdaolides (1−4, 6−7) and dumulmycin (5) represent
a structurally unique group of macrocyclic polyketides,
biosynthesized by a trans-AT PKS assembly line (Figure 1B).

Dumulmycin (5) was initially isolated from Korean river-
derived Streptomyces sp. DM28.17 Compound 5 has shown
antitubercular properties and also exhibited cytotoxicity against
RAW246.7 macrophages but not against many cancer cell
lines.16,17 In addition, the polyketide skeleton and PKS module
structures of formicolides from a wood ant gut bacterium
Streptomyces sp. BA0118 and hamuramicins from Streptomyces
sp. MBP16, a gut bacterium of wasp19, are quite similar to
shuangdaolides (Figure 1). However, compared with hamur-
amicin B, the shuangdaolides and formicolides (Figure 1C)
lack an acetyl (C2) unit due to the presence of a nonelongating
KS domain (KS0) in the module 8 (Figure 1A). Formicolides
have been shown to increase quinone reductase activity and
inhibit angiogenesis.18 Hamuramicins were also found to
inhibit some human cancer cell lines and growth of bacteria
such as Kocuria rhizophia ATCC9341 and Xanthomonas oryzae
pv. oryzae KB88.19,20
Shuangdaolides contain a rare internal five-membered
carbocycle moiety in their skeleton, which is only found in
piceamycin, cremimycin, and hitachimycin (Figure 1D).21−24
However, the formation mechanism for five-membered
carbocyclics remains elusive. Our recent study demonstrated
SdlR, consisting of a KR, an ACP, a flavoprotein mono-
oxygenase (FMO) domain, and a serine hydrolase (FSH1)
domain, participated in the formation of the 2-hydroxycyclo-
pentenone moiety in shuangdaolides by in vivo gene
inactivation.16 Moreover, the absence of the sdlR-like gene in
both the formicolide and hamuramicin BGCs is consistent with
their products lacking the cyclopentenone, providing evidence
for SdlR-involved cyclopentenone formation. However, the
precise mechanism how SdlR produces the 2-hydroxycyclo-
pentenone component, as well as the involvement of other
genes in the shuangdaolide BGC, are unknown. Hence, we try
to elucidate the biosynthetic mechanism of shuangdaolides.

shuangdaolide biosynthesis. The enzymes responsible for the
structural diversity of shuangdaolides include cytochrome
P450 monooxygenase SdlB, oxidoreductase SdlQ, and multi-
domain protein SdlR. SdlB oxidizes the methylene at C-12 to
the carbonyl group, and SdlQ oxidizes the hydroxyl group at
C-25 to the carbonyl group. Bioinformatics analysis and in vivo
point mutations of SdlR revealed that only its FMO domain
has catalytic activity. Substrate feeding and in vitro reactions
suggested that the modification by SdlR occurs during the
chain elongation process. TE domain inactivation, mass
spectrometry analysis, and structural characterization of the
intermediate revealed that SdlR catalyzes the formation of
16,17-epoxide when the polyketide chain extends to module 10
on the PKS assembly line. The subsequent spontaneous
opening of this epoxide results in the formation of the 2-
hydroxycyclopentenone moiety, and alkaline conditions can
accelerate this spontaneous ring opening. These findings
allowed us to propose a biosynthetic model for the
shuangdaolides, deepening our understanding of the trans-AT
PKS pathways.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Systematic Inactivation of Genes in the sdl BGC. The
sdl BGC reveals a potential composition of 18 open reading
frames (ORFs) (Figure 1A).16 In addition to the gene (sdlA)
encoding a 4′-phosphopantetheinyl transferase, it includes six
core PKS genes (sdlD, sdlE, sdlF, sdlG, sdlH, and sdlJ) encoding
polyketide synthase for skeleton biosynthesis, and six genes
(sdlK, sdlL, sdlM, sdlN, sdlO, and sdlP) encoding a
hydroxymethylglutaryl-CoA synthase (HMGS) cassette for
polyketide β-branching. The sdl BGC also contains a
nonfunctional sdlC, sdlB (encoding a cytochrome P450
monooxgenase), sdlI (encoding a 2-nitropropane dioxygenase),
sdlQ (encoding a short-chain dehydrogenase/reductase (SDR)
family oxidoreductase), and sdlR (encoding a multidomain
protein including KR/ACP/FMO/FSH1 domain) (Figure 1A,
Table S11). In our previous study, we inactivated sdlC and sdlR
to investigate their function in vivo.16 Herein, the genes orf1,
orf 2, orf 3, sdlK, sdlI, sdlB, and sdlQ are inactivated to
determine their roles in shuangdaolide biosynthesis, respec-
tively. In-frame deletions of sdl genes on plasmids were
performed in E. coli GBred-gyrA462.25,26 The mutated
plasmids were introduced into S. albus J1074 for expression,
and the resulting biosynthetic intermediates were elucidated by
HRMS and NMR analyses.
The mutants Δorf1/Δorf 2 and Δorf3 produce the same
products as the wild type (Figure S2), suggesting that these
genes do not participate in shuangdaolide biosynthesis. During
polyketide assembly, the HMGS cassette incorporates β-
branching into the product backbone.27 If any of the proteins
in the cassette are inactivated, it will impede the normal flux of
intermediates along the multienzyme complex responsible for
the polyketide backbone, resulting in the release of truncated
intermediates or the absence of detectable products.28,29 When
sdlK (encoding the HMGS) was deleted, the shuangdaolides
disappeared and were replaced by a truncated intermediate
(m/z 327.2166 [M+H]+), denoted by an asterisk in Figure
2A(e). SdlI is very similar to BatK (68%, identity), a trans-
enoyl reductase involved in kalimantacin biosynthesis.28 The
shuanagdaolides were eliminated with the inactivation of sdlI
(Figure 2A(d)). Hence, it is postulated that SdlI may be
responsible for the reduction of an olefin structure during
polyketide assembly.

and SdlR play a significant role in the generation of structural
diversity in shuangdaolides, but the accurate functions of SdlB
and SdlR remains unknown. Then, we constructed the triple

ΔsdlQ/ΔsdlR and ΔsdlB/ΔsdlQ. The triple mutant ΔsdlB/
ΔsdlQ/ΔsdlR mainly produced the shuangdaolide BQR1 (12)
and BQR2 (13) (Figure 2A(h), Table S6). Compound 12
matches the predicted nascent cyclized polyketide,16 whereas
13 has a lower yield and is likely a byproduct. In the mutant
ΔsdlQ/ΔsdlR, compound 14, which is consistent with the
structure of formicolide B,18 and shuangdaolide QR1 (15)
were obtained (Figure 2A(i), Table S7). Compound 14 has an
extra carbonyl at C-12 compared to that of 12 (Figure 2B),
indicating that the P450 enzyme SdlB catalyzes the oxidation

Five new compounds, shuangdaolide BQ1-BQ5 (16-20), were

Compared to product 12 of mutant ΔsdlB/ΔsdlQ/ΔsdlR, the
presence of SdlR may first oxidize the 16,17-double bond to
epoxide followed by epoxide ring-opening to produce 16−20.

function.35 Then, the Cys344 and Ser482 of SdlR were
mutated into Ala to inactivate the KR and ACP domains,
respectively.36 The LC-MS analysis showed that the SdlR-
C344A and SdlR-S482A did not impact the production,

To evaluate the function of SdlR in vitro, we tried to express
SdlR in E. coli BL21(DE3), but many attempts were
unsuccessful. The deletion of the KR-ACP domains (AA1 to
AA521) of SdlR resulted in the successful expression and
purification of the truncated version of SdlR522, which
contains only the FMO-FSH1 domains. The truncated protein
exhibits weak catalytic activity in vivo (Figure 4B), which may
be attributed to protein instability. A maltose-binding protein
(MBP) tag was fused to the N-terminus of SdlR522 to increase
its stability, resulting in the MBP-SdlR522 fusion protein
(Figure S7). The FMO domain of SdlR, which contains a
NAD(P)-binding motif, belongs to the Group B subfamily of
flavin proteins, according to their classification.37 They are
capable of independently transferring electrons from NAD(P)
H to FAD without the involvement of flavin reductase. The
enzyme activity of FMO domain was evaluated by ultraviolet
(UV) spectrophotometry in which the oxidation of NAD(P)H
caused a decrease in absorbance at 340 nm (Figure S7).38
Although the MBP-SdlR522 protein has catalytic activity for
NADPH oxidation, no product was detected following the
addition of 12. This raises the suspicion that 12 may not be the
real substrate for SdlR or that SdlR is not a postassembly line
tailoring enzyme.
To verify whether 12 is the substrate of SdlR, it was added
to mutants ΔsdlK and ΔsdlB/ΔsdlQ/ΔsdlK containing an
intact sdlR but was unable to produce a complete polyketide
chain. After fermentation and metabolic analysis, 12 did not
undergo any conversion in mutant ΔsdlB/ΔsdlQ/ΔsdlK.
Compounds 6, 7, and 10, which are produced by mutant
ΔsdlR, were detected in mutant ΔsdlK (Figure 4C). This
indicates that 12 can enter the cells and be modified by
postassembly line tailoring enzymes SdlB and SdlQ other than
SdlR, suggesting the epoxidation by SdlR may occur during
chain elongation.
Timing of Online Epoxidation by SdlR. The bio-
synthetic intermediates of trans-AT PKS assembly lines can be
released by inactivating thioesterase (TE) domain.39,40 To
verify whether epoxidation occurs on the assembly line, we
tried to compare the biosynthetic intermediates in the TE
mutants between the presence and absence of SdlR. Therefore,
two mutants, ΔsdlB/ΔsdlQ/ΔsdlR/ΔTE and ΔsdlB/ΔsdlQ/
ΔTE, were constructed. The retro-biosynthetic analysis of
shuandaolides suggests the epoxidation may occur on module
6 or downstream modules. Therefore, we analyzed the possible
released free acid intermediates with and without the epoxide
group from ACPM6-ACPM12 in the crude extracts of the two
mutant strains (Figure 5B; Table 1). The results show that the
epoxide-free intermediates 21*, 22*, 23*, 25*, and 27*
released from ACPM7−ACPM12 were detected in the mutant
ΔsdlB/ΔsdlQ/ΔsdlR/ΔTE (Figure 5B (i); Table 1). In mutant
ΔsdlB/ΔsdlQ/ΔTE, 21* and 22* were also present, but 23*,
25*, and 27* were not detected (Figure 5B(ii); Table 1).
Instead, 24*, 26* and 28 with an epoxy group were detected,
which are absent in mutant ΔsdlB/ΔsdlQ/ΔsdlR/ΔTE (Figure
5B; Table 1). These results indicate that SdlR catalyzes the
formation of 16,17-epoxide at module 10 during polyketide
elongation, resulting in intermediates released from ACPM10-
ACPM12 containing the epoxy group (Figure 5A). The free acid
28 was isolated after extensive culture of mutant ΔsdlB/
ΔsdlQ/ΔTE and identified as the epoxide-containing product
by NMR, which matches the structure of the presumed
intermediate from ACPM12 (Figure 5C; Table S10). As the low
yields of 22* and 24* preventing their purification, we
conducted a comparative MS2 analysis of 22*, 23*, 24*, and
28 to further support their structural assignments (Figure 5D).
Thereby, the above results support the timing of this online
epoxidation by the trans-acting enzyme SdlR. More signifi-
cantly, this online epoxidation occurs at the 7 positions away
from the thioester not at the usual α-, β-, γ-carbon positions,
such as methylation, β-carbon processing, and β-branch-
ing.27,41 However, how the SdlR interacts with the PKS is
still unknown, which may require further in vitro experiments
as well as the high resolution of the cocrystal structure of SdlR
and PKS complex.
Recent studies have unveiled several online modifications
involving the insertion of oxygen into the carbon back-
bone.42,43 However, online epoxidation is rare. The formation
of epoxide moiety in spliceostatin occurs on the PKS assembly
line, but it was catalyzed by an integrated FMO domain in the
PKS.7 MmpE in the mupirocin biosynthetic pathway is
considered as a trans-acting enzyme for online epoxidation,
but the timing is still unkown.44 Thus, our findings provide the
evidence for the timing of online epoxidation by a trans-acting
enzymes SdlR in shuangdaolide pathway, deepening the
understanding of online epoxidation in trans-AT PKS
biosynthetic pathways.

Formation of 2-Hydroxycyclopentenone by Non-
enzyme-Catalyzed Epoxide Rearrangement. The SdlR
functions as a trans-epoxidase only through its FMO domain.
However, the mechanism of epoxide ring opening remains
unknown. There is no epoxide hydrolase in the sdl BGC which
suggests that the epoxide ring opening may not require an
enzyme. To validate this hypothesis, compound 9 was
incubated in various pH-phosphate buffer solutions. It was
observed that alkaline conditions accelerated its spontaneous
transformation (Figure 6A). After 12 h at RT in the pH 8
buffer, 9 had almost been converted to 8 and an unidentified
compound 29*. Additionally, purified 29* can be converted to
8, suggesting that 29* could be an intermediate in the
transformation from 9 to 8. The structural transformation from
9 to 8 may involve transesterification and the formation of a 2-
hydroxycyclopentenone moiety mediated by epoxide ring
opening. The formation of the 2-hydroxycyclopentenone
moiety would destroy the conjugated double bonds between
C-6 and C-11, resulting in the absence of the UV absorption of
8 at 330 nm (Figure 6B), while the transesterification has no
effect on this conjugated double bond system. The UV
spectrum of 29* is essentially identical to that of 9 (Figure
6B), and their molecular weights are identical as determined by
high-resolution mass spectrometry (m/z 573.3419 [M+H]+).
Therefore, 29* could be the transesterificated product of 9,
containing the 22-membered lactone with the ester bond
located between C-21 and C-1 (Figure 6C). Subsequently, its
epoxide spontaneously opened to form intermediate 30*
followed by Michael cyclization to form the 2-hydroxycyclo-
pentenone moiety of 8 (Figure 6C).
Epoxide-mediated C−C bond formation is common in the
biosynthesis of lanosterol from 2,3-oxidosqualene and indole
diterpenes, but it requires cyclase catalysis.45,46 Although
intramolecular hydroxyl groups can attack epoxides sponta-
neously to form ether bonds,47,48 this kind of C−C bond
formation mediated by nonenzyme-catalyzed epoxide rear-
rangement has not been seen in natural product biosynthetic
pathways.49
Proposed Biosynthetic Pathway of the Shuangdao-
lides. The multiple sequence alignment of the sdl PKS domain
was conducted in our previous study.16 The results show that
KSM8 and KSM15 lack the conserved His residue (in the
EAHGTG motif) required for decarboxylative condensation.
The Tyr residue of KRM8 (in the YAAAN motif) was replaced
by a Gly residue. The DH domains of loading module (LM)
and module 8 (M8) are truncated variants without active core
motif. These domains are incapable of modifying the
intermediates. The sdlJ encodes an AT with specificity for
malonyl-CoA,16 which provides the substrate for all the
modules. In the loading module, the malonyl-CoA was loaded
and then decarboxylated by KSLM to form acetyl on the
ACPLM.13 Then, each elongating module except module 8 and
module15 is responsible for one C2 unit incorporation and the
extension and modification of polyketide chains.
The retro-biosynthetic analysis of shuangdaolides and their
intermediates suggested that module 3 probably lacks an ER
domain, whereas module 6 is devoid of a KR domain. The
putative trans-enoyl reductase, SdlI, was determined to be
indispensable for the biosynthesis of shuangdaolides. Thus, we
speculated that SdlI exerts ER function at module 3. Module 6
and module 7 look like a variant of a type B bimodule that
produces conjugated dienes in trans-AT PKSs such as module
15/module 16 of rhizopodin PKS,1,50 and the KRM6 function
could be supplemented by the KRM7, though the biochemical
evidence remains enigmatic. When the polyketide chain
extends to module 10, the HMGS cassette encoded by sdlK-
P introduces a β-methyl, and SdlR functions as a trans-
epoxidase to catalyze the formation of 16,17-epoxide. The
HMGS cassette installs another β-methyl ring on module 12.
Finally, the extended chain was cyclized to form a lactone and
released from the PKS assembly line by the TE domain.

■CONCLUSIONS
In this study, we characterized the biosynthetic pathway of
shuangdaolides, with a specific focus on the role of SdlR in
catalyzing the synthesis of the 16,17-epoxide on the PKS
assembly line for the first time, which is highly unprecedented
in that the online epoxidation takes place at 7 positions away
from the thioester rather than the neighboring positions. Then
two tailoring enzymes, SdlB and SdlQ, convert the methylene
at C-12 to a carbonyl group and oxidize the hydroxyl group at
C-25 to a carbonyl group, respectively. The five-membered
carbocyclic moiety in shuangdaolides is formed through
spontaneous epoxide opening followed by Michael cyclization.
Thus, our findings not only provide further insights into the
biosynthetic pathway of shuangdaolide but also greatly expand
the scope of distinctive modifications in trans-AT PKS
assembly lines.
