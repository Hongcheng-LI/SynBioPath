UPLC-Q-TOF-MS/MS-Based Targeted Discovery of Chetomin

ABSTRACT: Chetocochliodin M (5) containing a rare cage-ring
and chetocochliodin N (6) featuring an unusual piperazine-2,3-
dione ring system together with known analogues chetomin (1),
chetoseminudin C (2), chetocochliodin I (3), and oidioperazine E
(4) were targeted for purification from the fungus Chaetomium
cochliodes using a UPLC-Q-TOF-MS/MS approach. The structures
of the new compounds were elucidated using HR-ESI-MS, NMR,
and ECD spectra. Compounds 1, 3, and 6 exhibited strong
cytotoxic activities against A549 and HeLa cancer cell lines.
C
hetomin (1) and its analogues belong to a class of
cyclopeptides with an asymmetrically dimeric epidithio-
diketopiperazine (ETP) skeleton, which is uniquely linked by
an N−C bond. This differs from the symmetrically dimeric
ETPs in verticillins, chaetocins, and leptosins, which are linked
by a C−C bond.1 The dimeric ETP chetomin (1) and its
analogues show a wide range of bioactivities, particularly
cytotoxicity. Studies reveal that chetomin (1) can reduce the
transcriptional activity of hypoxia inducible factor 1 (HIF-1)
by inhibiting the binding of HIF-1 to p300.2,3 The synthesis
and biosynthetic pathways of chetomin analogues are of
interest due to their complex structures and significant
bioactivities. Despite nearly 80 years of research, chetomin
(1) has not been synthesized, although (+)-11,11′-dideox-
yverticillin A and chaetocin4−6 have been. The biosynthesis of
chetomin (1) remains unclear, although the pathways of
several ETPs such as gliotoxin and sirodesmin PL are well-
documented.7−10
Recently, our group isolated several chetomin analogues
containing poly sulfur bridges from the fungus Chaetomium
cochliodes.11 The light sensitivity of these poly sulfur bridges in
chetomin analogues was discovered, solving a long-standing
mystery regarding their rarity and offering a new purification
method for this unique ETP class. Additionally, a series of
monomeric ETPs were isolated and characterized, potentially
serving as intermediates in the chetomin biosynthetic path-
way.12 UPLC-Q-TOF-MS/MS was applied to further explore
minor metabolites of C. cochliodes, which may include new
chetomin analogues or potential intermediates.
The sodium adduct [M + Na]+ (m/z 388) of chetosemi-
nudin C (2) was detected in the UPLC-Q-TOF-MS/MS
spectra. The most abundant fragment ion (m/z 270) was
produced from the protonated molecule (m/z 366) through
sequential loss of two molecules of HSCH3 (−48) (Figure 1,
Figure S4). Detailed mass and fragment ion data, along with
the elemental constituents of chetoseminudin C (2), are
provided in Table S2. The same fragment ion (m/z 270) was
observed in the spectra of chetocochliodin C/D/E (Figure
S2b), originating from the protonated molecule (m/z 350)
through the loss of one molecule each of HOCH3 (−32) and
HSCH3 (−48) (Figure 1, Figure S10, Table S8). Additionally,
chetocochliodin F/G (Figure S2b) demonstrated similar
fragmentation pathways (Figure 1, Figure S11, Table S9),
losing two molecules of HOCH3 (−32) to form the fragment
ion (m/z 270). This fragment ion at m/z 270 may serve as a
key diagnostic ion for these monomeric ETPs.
In the UPLC-Q-TOF-MS/MS spectra, the sodium adduct
[M + Na]+ (m/z 386) of chetocochliodin A/B (Figure S2b)
was observed. The most abundant fragment ion at m/z 332
was likely formed by the protonated molecule [M + H]+ (m/z
364) losing one molecule of HOCH3 (−32). The diagnostic
fragment ion at m/z 284 resulted from the fragment ion at m/z
332 losing a molecule of HSCH3 (−48) (Figure 2, Figure S9,
Table S7). The primary fragmentation pattern of chetoco-
chliodin A/B involves sequential neutral loss of HOCH3 and
HSCH3, resulting in a unique diagnostic ion at m/z 284 due to

The protonated molecule [M + H]+ (m/z 711) of chetomin
(1) was detected in the UPLC-Q-TOF-MS/MS spectra, with
McLafferty rearrangements and neutral loss as predominant
cleavage patterns. The fragmentation pathways suggested that
the fragment ion (m/z 647) resulted from the protonated
molecule [M + H]+ (m/z 711) through the neutral loss of an
S2 molecule (−64). Subsequent McLafferty rearrangement
produced a pair of complementary fragment ions (m/z 348
and m/z 300). However, the fragment ion at m/z 300 was not
observed in the spectra, likely due to rapid loss of one molecule
of H2O (−18) to form the fragment ion at m/z 282 (Figure 3,
Figure S3, Table S1). Notably, chetocochliodin I (3), a
monomeric ETP with a sulfide bridge (S4), exhibited the same
fragment ion (m/z 282) in its spectra, potentially from similar
neutral loss of S4 and H2O (Figure 3, Figure S5, Table S3).
Therefore, the fragment ion at m/z 282 could be a diagnostic
ion for chetomin analogues with poly sulfur bridges, including
both dimeric and monomeric ETPs.

Several chromatographic peaks (tR = 5.77 min, tR = 7.02
min, tR = 12.58 min) were observed with similar cleavage
fragments and diagnostic ions that had never been isolated
from C. cochliodes, suggesting the presence of several
undescribed analogues in the extract (Figure S1). Among
these, two peaks at tR = 7.02 min and tR = 5.77 min were
identified with the protonated molecule [M + H]+ at m/z 332
(4) and m/z 318 (5) in the crude extract. Their molecular
formulas, C16H17N3O3S and C15H15N3O3S, respectively,
indicate an additional degree of unsaturation in compounds
4 and 5 compared to other monomeric ETPs. Furthermore,
the diagnostic ions for compounds 4 and 5, at m/z 284 and m/
z 270, respectively, originated from the protonated molecules
(m/z 332 and m/z 318) through the loss of a single molecule
of HSCH3 (−48) (Figure 4, Figure S6, 7, Tables S4, S5). This
suggests that compounds 4 and 5 may be chetomin analogues
not previously purified from C. cochliodes. Additionally, the
structure of compound 6 (tR = 12.58 min) with a protonated
molecule [M + H]+ at m/z 740 was analyzed using UPLC-Q-
TOF-MS/MS, producing fragment ions at m/z 612, m/z 594,
and m/z 282, m/z 313 through characteristic McLafferty
rearrangements and the neutral loss of S4 (−128) and H2O
(−18) (Figure 5, Figure S8, Table S6). However, the fragment
ion at m/z 313, potentially deriving from the ion at m/z 594
through McLafferty rearrangements, had not been observed in
other dimeric ETP chetomins, indicating that compound 6
might be a novel dimeric chetomin analogue. To further
elucidate the structures of compounds 4−6, they were purified
from the C. cochliodes extract in target.
Compounds 1−4 were identified as chetomin (1),13,14
chetoseminudin C (2),15 chetocochliodin I (3),12 and
oidioperazine E (4)16 based on their NMR analysis.
The molecular formula of chetocochliodin M (5) was
determined to be C15H15N3O3S based on HR-ESI-MS,
indicating 10 degrees of unsaturation. Analysis of 1H NMR,
13C NMR (Table 1), and HSQC data revealed four
exchangeable protons, a methyl group (−SCH3), a methylene
unit, two quaternary carbons (C-3 and C-6), two carbonyl
carbons (C-2 and C-5), and an indole ring lacking the H-9.

The molecular formula of chetocochliodin N (6) was
established as C32H32N7O6S4 via HR-ESI-MS. Wu et al.’s
detailed investigation into the mass fragmentation pathways of
chetomin and its analogues using an HPLC-MS/MS/MS
approach highlighted the critical role of McLafferty rearrange-
ments and neutral losses.18 Characteristic ions at m/z 612 (loss
of S4, [M-128]) and m/z 594 (loss of H2O, [M-18]) in the
UPLC-Q-TOF-MS/MS profile suggested the presence of four
sulfur atoms in a structure similar to that found in compound
3. This was further supported by 2D-NMR spectra (Figure 6).
A diagnostic
1H proton signal at δ = 6.34 indicated the
presence of a pyrrolo-indole ring in the lower part of 6, a
feature consistent across chetomin analogues, confirmed by
2D-NMR experiments (Figure 6). Further structure elucida-
tion was aided by 1H−1H COSY and HMBC correlations. The
1H−1H COSY spectra identified two distinct proton spin
systems in the lower region of compound 6, specifically 14-
NH-15-CH3 and 2-NH-1-CH-12-CH3. The HMBC correla-
tions from 11-CH2 to C-11a and C-13 and from 14-NH and
15-CH3 to C-13 established the structure of the C-11-C11a-
C13−14-NH-15-CH3 fragment. Additionally, correlations from
12-CH3 to C-11a, from 2-NH to C-3 and C-4, and from H-5 to
C-11a and C-4 helped to elucidate the piperazine-2,3-dione
structure fused to the pyrrolo-indole ring at the C/N atoms.
Thus, the planar structure of 6 was determined. The relative
configuration of the molecule was assessed using ROESY
correlations (Figure 6). The correlations from H-5 to H-11′,

were α-oriented, whereas the correlation from H-11b to H-1

Chetomin (1) was previously identified as a potential cancer
chemotherapeutic agent due to its strong cytotoxicity. Our
earlier studies isolated chetomins A−D, four highly photo-
sensitive poly sulfide analogues in darkness with potent
cytotoxicities.11 Furthermore, a series of monomeric ETPs
were tested, in which the polysulfur-bridged analogue
chetocochliodin I (3) was found to be the most active.12 In
the current study, compounds 1−6 were tested against two
cancer cell lines, A549 and HeLa. Compounds 1, 3, and 6
showed inhibitory activities with IC50 values of 3.5, 2.2, and 1.9
μM against A549 and 2.4, 1.9, and 1.6 μM against Hela,
respectively. By comparison, cisplatin has IC50 values of 12 and
18 μM against A549 and HeLa (Table S10). Additionally,
analogues 2, 4, and 5, which contain −SCH3 but no sulfide
bridge exhibited only moderate activities against A549 and
HeLa, highlighting the importance of the sulfide bridge in the
cytotoxicity of chetomin analogues.

were recorded on a UV-2102 (Unico) instrument. ECD spectra were
collected using a J-815 spectropolarimeter (Jasco). Conformational
searches were first performed using the MMFF94S force field, and the
resulting conformers within 0−10 kcal/mol were optimized through
density functional theory (DFT). The benchmark performed suggests
the dispersion-corrected functional B3LYP-D3BJ owns a high
accuracy, and the method is proposed for biochemically relevant
systems. These conformers within 0−10 kcal/mol were optimized
through the B3LYP-D3BJ/6-31G(d) level, and the optimized
structures with relative energies ranging from 0 to 4 kcal/mol were
further reoptimized at the B3LYP-D3BJ/6-311G(2d, p) level. The
ECD computations were performed at the TDDFT/B3LYP/6-
311G(2d, p)/SMD level. All the computations are performed using
Gaussian09. IR spectra were obtained on an FTIR-8400S
spectrophotometer (Shimadzu). NMR spectra were recorded on
either a Bruker AVANCE III 500 MHz or a Varian Inova 600 MHz
spectrometer, using solvent signals from DMSO-d6: δH 2.50/δC 39.5
as references. HR-ESI-MS spectra were acquired on a UPLC-Q-TOF-
MS/MS (Waters Xevo G2-S QTOF), and semipreparative HPLC was
conducted using a SEP LC-52 instrument with a MWD UV detector
(Separation Technology Co. Ltd.), equipped with an ODS column
(YMC-pack ODS-A, 5 μm, 250 × 10 mm).
Fungal Material. The strain of Chaetomium cochliodes
(CGMCC3.17123; GenBank Accession Number: MH856972)
isolated from animal manure was provided by Dr. Bingda Sun from
the China General Microbiological Culture Collection Center at the
Institute of Microbiology, Chinese Academy of Sciences.19
Fermentation and Extraction. C. cochliodes (3.17123) was
cultivated on PDA plates at 25 °C for 7 days. Fresh mycelium was
then transferred to the autoclaved, sterilized solid medium comprised
of rice (60.0 g) and distilled water (80 mL) in Fernbach flasks (500
mL) and fermented at 25 °C for 14 days.
The fermented solid medium was extracted with 75% EtOH three
times with 75% ethanol at room temperature, concentrated under
reduced pressure, and sequentially extracted with CH2Cl2 and EtOAc
to afford CH2Cl2 and EtOAc fractions.
UPLC-Q-TOF-MS/MS Parameters. The ethyl acetate (EtOAc)
fraction from wild C. cochliodes was first examined using a UPLC-Q-
TOF-MS/MS instrument (Waters Xevo G2-S QTOF). Chromato-
graphic analysis was conducted using a Waters Acquity UPLC-PDA
system. A reversed-phase C-18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm,
Acquity BEH, Waters) was utilized, monitoring the UV absorbance
from 200 to 400 nm and maintaining the column temperature at 40
°C. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (A) and
0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (B), with gradient conditions set at
0−2 min, 15% B; 2−17 min, 15%−75% B; 17−20 min, 75%−100% B;
20−21 min, 100%−15% B; 21−24 min, 15% B. The flow rate was 0.3
mL/min. MS/MS was performed in MSe mode with a full MS scan
range from m/z 50 to 1500 in positive ion scan mode. The lock mass
was leucine-enkephalin ([M + H]+ = 556.2771) at a concentration of
200 ng/mL. The desolvation and source temperatures were set to 450
and 100 °C, respectively, with a capillary voltage of 2.5 kV and a
sample cone voltage of 30 V. Collision energy was adjusted to 6 eV for
low-energy scans and ramped from 20 to 40 eV for high-energy scans.
Instrument control was managed using MassLynx 4.1 software.
Purification of Compounds from C. cochliodes. The EtOAc
fraction (30.0 g) was initially analyzed for chetomin analogues using
UPLC-Q-TOF-MS/MS and then fractionated using silica gel column
chromatography with CH2Cl2−CH3OH (100:1 to 5:1) yielding seven
fractions (Fr.1−Fr.7). Fraction Fr.3 (5.5 g) underwent further
fractionation on silica gel with petroleum ether−acetone (50:1 to
1:1) to produce four subfractions (Fr.3.1−Fr.3.4). Subfraction Fr.3.4
(1.2 g) was targeted for further purification of a compound with a
sodium adduct [M + Na]+ at m/z 450 using ODS chromatography
and semipreparative HPLC (70−100% MeOH in H2O over 30 min),
yielding compound 3 (2.0 mg, retention time (tR) = 22.4 min). In
Fr.4 (1.5 g), additional peaks were identified with parent ions [M +
H]+ at m/z 318, 332, 740 and the known peak with the parent ion [M
+ H]+ at m/z 711. This fraction was further separated using ODS
chromatography into five subfractions (Fr.4.1−Fr.4.5). Fr.4.2 was
purified in target by semipreparative HPLC (45%−70% MeOH in
H2O for 30 min) to obtain compound 5 (1.5 mg, tR = 22.3 min) and
compound 4 (4.9 mg, tR = 29.8 min). Fr.4.4 was processed using
semipreparative HPLC (70% MeOH in H2O) to isolate compounds 1
(2.0 mg, tR = 27.8 min) and 6 (2.2 mg, tR = 30.2 min). Finally, Fr.5
(1.0 g) was separated into four subfractions (Fr.5.1−Fr.5.4), and a
known peak with a sodium adduct [M + Na]+ at m/z 388 in Fr.5.2
was purified using semipreparative HPLC (20%−30% CH3CN in
H2O for 40 min), resulting in compound 2 (2.3 mg, tR = 40.6 min).

The half-maximal inhibitory concentrations (IC50) were 12 and 18
μM, respectively.20
