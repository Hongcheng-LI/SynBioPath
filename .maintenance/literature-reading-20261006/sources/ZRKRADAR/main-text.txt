Substrate Promiscuity of the Triceptide Maturase XncB Leads to
Incorporation of Various Amino Acids and Detection of Oxygenated
Products

ABSTRACT: Triceptides are cyclophane-containing ribosomally
synthesized and post-translationally modified peptides. The
characteristic cross-links are formed between an aromatic ring to
Cβ on three-residue Ω1X2X3 motifs (Ω1 = aromatic). Here, we
explored the promiscuity of the XYE family triceptide maturase,
XncB from Xenorhabdus nematophila DSM 3370. Single amino acid
variants were coexpressed with XncB in vivo in Escherichia coli, and
we show that a variety of amino acids can be incorporated into the
Phe-Gly-Asn cyclophane. Aromatic amino acids at the X3 position
were accepted by the enzyme but yielded hydroxylated, rather than
the typical cyclophane, products. These studies show that oxygen
can be inserted but diverges in the final product formed relative to
daropeptide maturases. Finally, truncations of the leader peptide
showed that it is necessary for complete modification by XncB.
R
ibosomally synthesized and post-translationally modified
peptides (RiPPs) are a staple source of bioactive peptide
natural products and interesting biosynthetic enzymes.1,2
Radical SAM/SPASM enzymes have been an important source
of unique post-translational modifying enzymes.3−7 Many
rSAM/SPASM protein families were described in the
TIGRFAMs database, and several of these have been
functionally validated.3,8 A number of rSAM/SPASM
maturases have been found to catalyze chemically difficult
C−C bond formation reactions between an aromatic ring to an
sp3 carbon. Examples include PqqE (Glu-Cγ to Tyr-C3),9,10
MftC (Val-Cβ to Tyr-Cα),11−13 StrB/SuiB (Lys-Cβ to Trp-
C7),14,15 WgkB (Trp-C5 to Lys-Cα and Trp-C6 to Lys-Cδ),16
RrrB (Arg-Cδ to Tyr-C3),17 DarE (Trp-C6 to Lys-Cβ),18,19
DynA (His-N1 to Phe-Cβ),20 and most recently ItfD (Trp-C7
to Ile-Cδ).21 Interestingly, plant BURB-domain proteins can
also catalyze the formation of the aromatic ring-to-C(sp3)
bond.22,23
Our group has previously characterized XncB, OscB, and
MscB, which are representative enzymes from the rSAM/
SPASM protein families TIGR04496 (XyeB), TIGR04261
(GrrM), and TIGR04269 (FxsB), respectively.24 In the
TIGRFAMs database, XyeB, GrrM, and FxsB are described
as rSAM/SPASM protein subfamilies,8 and the reactions they
catalyze are summarized in Figure 1A. These rSAM/SPASM
proteins have been termed 3-residue cyclophane-forming
enzymes (3-CyFEs) or triceptide maturases and catalyze
C(sp2)−Cβ(sp3) bond formation between Ω1 and X3 on
Ω1-X2-X3 motifs.24 This modification represents the class-
defining reaction for triceptides.24 We previously reported the
biosynthetic landscape of triceptides by obtaining 3-CyFE
sequences using the databases RadicalSAM.org25 (UniProt26)
and NCBI RefSeq27 and generated a sequence similarity
network (SSN) (Figure 1B).28 This allowed identification of
∼3900 triceptide precursors containing Ω1-X2-X3 motifs (Ω1
= aromatic).24 Additional 3-CyFEs including SjiB,29 YxdB,28
HaaB,28 YhhB,28 and FwwB30 also catalyze C(sp2)−Cβ(sp3)
bond formation and show that Ω1 can be any aromatic amino
acid.
The XYE BGCs contain a precursor peptide (XyeA,
TIGR04495), rSAM/SPASM maturase (XyeB, TIGR04496),
aspartic protease (XyeC), an HlyD transporter (XyeD), and a
fused protease transporter (XyeE). The end product of this
BGC was named xenorceptide and represents the first
triceptide natural product to be characterized (Figure 1C).

To understand the promiscuity of XncB, we designed
variants of the second motif as we predicted that if accepted by
XncB, the variants would show a −6 Da mass loss, indicating
that three rings had formed. The variants consisted of single
amino acid mutations at Phe5 (Ω1), Gly6 (X2), and Asn7
(X3) positions of His6-SUMO-XncA and were constructed by
molecular cloning or custom gene synthesis. The His6-SUMO-
XncA precursor variants constructed and tested were F5Y/H/
W/A, G6F/R/K/S/A/L/E/Q/N/P, and N7F/R/K/S/A/L/
E/Q/P. The His6-SUMO-XncA variants and the cognate
rSAM/SPASM enzyme XncB were inserted for expression in
pACYCDuet-1 and pRSFDuet-1, respectively (Table S1). The
precursor His6-SUMO-XncA variants were coexpressed in vivo
in Escherichia coli NiCo21 with XncB, purified by Ni-affinity
chromatography, digested with trypsin, and analyzed by LC-
MS (Figure 2A,B). Variants that were accepted by XncB were
detected to contain a mass loss of −6 Da relative to that of the
unmodified sequence. The −2 Da mass shifts at each of the
motifs were localized by LC-MS/MS (Figures S1−S22).
The results for each of the variants were calculated as the
fully modified mass detected relative to the nonfully modified
mass fragments that could be detected and are summarized as
a heatmap (Figure 2A). For the Ω1 position, only native Trp
and Phe were accepted while Tyr and His were not accepted.
For the X2 position, good tolerance for almost all types of
residues except for the aromatic amino acid Phe could be
incorporated. For the X3 position, a fair tolerance was
observed for a range of amino acids including hydrophobic
Leu. Less conversion was observed for Lys and Gln. There is
precedent for the formation of formyl Gly when X3 is Ser.29
However, for N7S we did not detect the presence of formyl
Gly or amino malonate. Generally, cyclophane formation was
assigned by localizing −2 Da mass shifts to three-residue
motifs, and the absence of fragments was observed between
Ω1-X2 and X2-X3. However, we observed an alternate
fragmentation pattern for the N7F variant, which encouraged
us to study this further.
During the analysis of the N7F variant, we detected a mass
corresponding to −6 Da relative to the unmodified sequence,
which we suspected as the cyclophane-containing product.
MSMS fragmentation of this mass fragment was consistent
with cyclophanes at the WIN and WER motifs. However, the
third −2 Da mass loss was localized to F7, whereas
fragmentation within the ring is typically not observed (Figure
S23). The detected fragmentation suggests α,β-dehydro Phe.
In addition to the parent mass corresponding to −6 Da, an m/
z with +12 Da relative to the unmodified sequence was also
detected from the coexpression of His6-SUMO-XncA(N7F)
with XncB (Figure 2B). The +12 Da mass eluted at the same
retention time as the −6 Da product. The +12 Da mass
increase was consistent with the formation of two cyclophane
rings (−4 Da) and addition of one oxygen atom (+16 Da). To
investigate this modification further, we switched to heterol-
ogous expression incorporating cleavage and transporter genes
(xncCDE) to furnish the modified core peptides cleaved after
the Gly-Gly motif, which was more amenable to NMR analysis.
His6-SUMO-XncA(N7F) was coexpressed with XncBCDE
(pCDFDuet-1) in E. coli NiCo21 (DE3). The supernatant was
extracted by solid-phase extraction, and the eluate was
analyzed by LC-MS. The results showed a minor product 2
(−4 Da) and major product 3 (+12 Da) which eluted at a
slightly earlier retention time (Figures 3A and S23). Product 2
(−4 Da) was assigned as the two-ring product. For product 3,
we identified two sets of fragment ions. Both sets contained
two −2 Da mass losses localized to WIN and WER motifs but
differed at the FGF motif. The first set contained a +16 Da

With the modifications localized, we turned toward NMR to
elucidate the structure of product 6. The N7Y variant was
chosen because it was produced in the largest amount relative
to N7F and N7H variants.
His6-SUMO-XncA(N7Y) coexpressed with XncBCDE was
subjected to larger scale fermentation (8 L), and the culture
supernatant was subjected to solid-phase extraction (SPE) and
preparative HPLC to obtain ∼3 mg of product 6. 1D and 2D
NMR data (COSY, TOCSY, NOESY, edited HSQC and
HMBC) were obtained for analysis (Figures S27−S32 and
Table S5). Analysis of 2D NMR (Figure 3B) indicated that
Trp1 was substituted at C6, based on the aromatic spin system
including Trp1-H4 (δH 7.11), Trp1-H5 (δH 6.77), and Trp1-
H7 (δH 7.33). The location of Trp1-H4 was supported by
HMBC correlations to Trp1-C3 (δC 107.5), Trp1-C6 (δC
130.5), and Trp1-C7a (δC 136.7) and a NOESY correlation to
Trp1-Hβ (δH 3.07). Trp1-H5 was assigned based on HMBC
correlations to two carbons, Trp1-C3a (δC 126.8) and Trp1-
C7 (δC 110.7). The assignment of Trp1-H7 was supported by
HMBC correlations to Trp1-C3a and Trp1-C5 (δC 123.7) and
a NOESY correlation to Trp1-H1 (δH 10.81). Ile2 was
assigned as unmodified, and we focused our attention on Asn3.
The spin system for Asn3 showed that Cβ was now a
substituted methine (δH 3.77, δC 52.6). This suggested that a
cross-link was formed between Trp1-C6 and Asn3-Cβ. The
HMBC correlations of Asn3-Hβ to Trp1-C5, Trp1-C6, and
Trp1-C7 and a NOESY correlation to Trp1-H5 further
supported this assignment (Figure 3B). The WER macrocycle
in 6 was also assigned in a similar manner. The assignments of
the WIN and WER macrocycles as well as the planar chirality
support that the first and third rings are formed analogously to
xenorceptide. The spin system for Tyr7 showed that Tyr7-Cβ
was substituted with a hydroxyl group, where the spin system
showed that Cβ was now a substituted methine (δH 5.02, δC
71.4). The location of Tyr7-Cβ was further supported by the
HMBC correlation of Tyr7-H2/6 (δH 7.15). Marfey’s analysis
showed that the configuration was identical to that of
xenorceptide (Table S6). However, β−OH-Tyr was not
detected in Marfey’s analysis and, we suspect, is degraded
during acid hydrolysis; thus, the configuration was not
assigned.
We then asked whether the incorporation of oxygen occurs
on the wild-type substrate. We analyzed full cluster expression
His6-SUMO-XncA + XncBCDE and found that trace amounts
of a peak that correspond to +12 Da can be detected. MSMS of
the peptide showed that +16 Da can be localized to Asn7
(Figures 3A and S33). However, no +12 Da products were
observed for full cluster expression for other nonaromatic
variants His6-XncA(N7R/K/S/A) + XncBCDE (Figures S34−
S37). Currently, it is unclear whether this is an intermediate or
a shunt product and whether the oxygen originates from H2O
or from O2. The incorporation of oxygen can occur by the
formation of a dehydro amino acid at the X3 position and a
Michael-type addition of water. Alternatively, a radical
mechanism involving a hydroperoxide is also possible.32,33 In
addition, we noticed His6-SUMO-XncA + XncBCDE ex-
pression resulted in a mixture of two partially modified
products, where MSMS analyses showed −2 Da with ring
formation at the third motif and −4 Da at the first and third
motifs (Figures 4A and S38). These intermediates suggests
that cyclophane formation by XncB starts at the C-terminal
motif, then the N-terminal motif, and last the center motif.
This directionality is different to that observed by PauB.31

Next, we asked whether an ether cross-linked cyclophane
can be formed by XncB. To test this, we used double mutants
incorporating F5W and N7F/Y/H/W. These were chosen
because ether-containing cyclophanes have only been observed
for Trp at Ω1.34 The extracts from full cluster expression of
His6-SUMO-XncA(F5W,N7F/Y/H/W) + XncBCDE were
analyzed by LC-MS and showed a similar product distribution
as the single mutants (N7F/Y/H/W), but ether cross-linked
cyclophanes were not detected (Figures S39−S42).
The incorporation of oxygen by XncB is intriguing. The
class-defining feature of triceptides is the C(sp2)-Cβ(sp3) bond
that occurs throughout this family. Our previous bioinformatic
analysis identified ∼3300 triceptide precursor peptides; all the
identified motifs do not contain an aromatic amino acid at the
X3 position.28 In contrast, the defining feature of the
daropeptide family (which includes darobactin) is the
incorporation of an ether linkage at Ω1-X2-Ω3 motifs and
utilization of molecular oxygen by the maturases DarE and
homologues.32,33 Therefore, while XncB can process precursor
variants containing an aromatic amino acid at X3 and
incorporate oxygen at Cβ these products are not further
processed to form the ether cross-linked cyclophane. These
data emphasize the difference in enzymatic activity between 3-
CyFEs (triceptide maturases) and daropeptide maturases.
In a final set of experiments, we addressed whether the
leader peptide is essential for processing XncA by XncB. XncA
is 52-aa long, with a 39-aa leader peptide and a 14-aa core
peptide. Two constructs were designed to determine the
importance of the leader peptide in cyclophane formation.
His6-SUMO-XncAtrunc1 contains −15 to −1, and His6-SUMO-
XncAtrunc2 contains −5 to −1. Coexpression of both His6-
SUMO-XncAtrunc1/2 (pACYCDuet-1) with XncB (pRSFDuet-
1) showed that −2 and −4 Da products are formed consistent
with one- and two-ring products, respectively (Figures 4B, S43,
and S44). This suggested a full-length precursor peptide is
required by XncB to install all three cyclophane rings.
In conclusion, we further investigated the triceptide
maturase XncB to understand the substrate promiscuity. Our
results show that XncB has a preference for Trp/Phe at
position Ω1 but is relatively promiscuous at both X2 and X3
positions. The ability to accept a variety of amino acids at X2
and X3 is an encouraging step toward applying XncB in library
generation. Throughout this study, we only observed the
formation of three-residue rings, indicating this may be a
limitation but an important aspect to address by enzyme
engineering. On the other hand, strict formation of three-
residue rings allows a sense of predictability when the number
of products becomes too large for analysis. We discovered that
hydroxylation is detected, which can be increased when X3 is
an aromatic amino acid. However, the aromatic-containing side
chains do not lead to cyclophane formation. Further studies to
elucidate the mechanism for cyclophane formation will allow
for a greater understanding of cyclophane formation of
triceptide maturases. Both the production of cyclophane-
containing libraries and the enzyme mechanism are intriguing
avenues for further study.
