Pyrroline Ring Assembly via N‑Prenylation and Oxidative
Carbocyclization during Biosynthesis of Aeruginosin Derivatives

ABSTRACT: Aeruginosins are linear peptide natural products isolated from cyanobacteria and contain various arginine derivatives
at their termini. 1-Amino-2-(N-amidino-3-Δ3-pyrrolinyl)ethane (Aeap) is a structurally unique arginine derivative, as it has an
unusual pyrroline ring with two additional carbon atoms of unknown biosynthetic origin. Here, we demonstrate that Aer3, a member
of a newly identified subfamily of prenyltransferases, catalyzes selective isopentenylation of the internal N atom of agmatine. Rieske
oxygenase AerC then catalyzes both carbocyclization and C−C bond cleavage to construct the pyrroline ring in Aeap. This pyrroline
ring formation in Aeap biosynthesis, involving two novel enzymes, represents a unique route for heterocycle formation in nature.
A
eruginosins (such as 1−6), a class of linear peptide
natural products derived from cyanobacteria and marine
sponges, are known for their potent inhibitory activities against
various serine proteases (Figure 1).1 The chemical structures
of aeruginosins are characterized by the 2-carboxy-6-hydroxy-
octahydroindole (Choi) or 4-amino-5,7,9-trihydroxy-2-
azabicyclononane-3-carboxylic acid (Abn) moieties within
peptide scaffolds.2 The carboxylic acid of Choi or Abn is
typically conjugated with various arginine derivatives, such as
argininal (7 in aeruginosin NAL2 (1) or its cyclic form 7′ in
aeruginosin 686 (2)),3 argininol (8 in aeruginosin 298A (3)),4
agmatine (9 in aeruginosin 126B (4)),5 and 1-amino-2-(N-
amidino-3-Δ3-pyrrolinyl)ethane (Aeap, 10 (in aeruginosin
126A (5)5 and suomilide (6)),2b,6 thereby enhancing the
structural diversity and modulating the serine protease
inhibitory activities of these natural products (Figure 1).
The biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) responsible for
several aeruginosin congeners have been identified.3,5−7 The
Choi moiety is presumably biosynthesized from prephenate, an
intermediate in the shikimate pathway, with the involvement of
AerD (prephenate decarboxylase), AerE (isomerase), and AerF
(NADPH-dependent reductase), and is incorporated into
peptide chains by cluster-specific nonribosomal peptide
synthetases (NRPSs) (Figure S1).8 Although the exact
mechanism has not been determined, two possibilities can be
envisioned for the incorporation of the terminal amine unit,
depending on the NRPS domain organization. Since the BGCs
for aeruginosin NAL 2 (1) and aeruginosin 686 (2) encode an
NRPS containing a C-terminal reductase domain, the
tetrapeptide products with argininal (7) (or its cyclic N,O-
acetal form 7′) may be released via reduction of the peptidyl
carrier protein (PCP)-linked thioester of the arginine-
containing tetrapeptide (Figure S2).3 Argininol (8) may be
produced via further reduction of the aldehyde group in 7 by
an unknown reductase (Figure S2). Alternatively, aeruginosin
tetrapeptides containing agmatine (9), such as aeruginosin
126B (4), may be formed through a releasing mechanism
involving the nucleophilic attack of agmatine at the PCP-linked
tripeptide thioester intermediate (Figure S3).5 Aeap (10) may
be incorporated into tetrapeptides such as aeruginosin 126A
(5) and suomilide (6) via an analogous mechanism. However,
the construction of the unusual pyrroline ring in Aeap (10)
remains unclear, despite its potential to significantly enhance
the protease inhibitory activities of aeruginosins.6
Aeap (10) is likely derived from agmatine (9) due to their
structural similarity, although the biosynthetic origin of the
additional two carbon atoms remains enigmatic. To identify
the gene(s) responsible for Aeap formation, a comparative
analysis of available aeruginosin-type BGCs was conducted
(Figure S4).5−7 The analysis revealed that aer3 (previously
named orf 3)5 and aerC (or suoC in the suomilide BGC) are
conserved only in the BGCs for Aeap-containing aeruginosins,
such as aeruginosin 126A (5)5 and suomilide (6),6 suggesting
that the corresponding gene products, Aer3 and AerC, are
responsible for Aeap formation from agmatine.
Aer3 has been annotated as a hypothetical protein, while
AerC was previously proposed to be a Rieske oxygenase
potentially involved in Choi biosynthesis,5 although this has
not been experimentally verified. Analysis of the primary
sequence of Aer3 did not provide useful information.
Nevertheless, structural prediction using Alphafold9 and
subsequent analysis with the Dali server10 suggested that
Aer3 exhibits structural similarity to type I terpene synthases
such as CyS (with a 3.6 Å root-mean-square deviation
(RMSD) for the Cα atoms; PDB 7Y88).11 Notably, the
conserved motifs DDXXD and NTE, responsible for binding
the magnesium ion and the prenyl pyrophosphate moiety in
CyS, are replaced by DXXXE and DDD, respectively, in Aer3

To biochemically characterize Aer3, the corresponding gene,
Scaer3, was cloned from the commercially available strain
Scytonema sp. NIES-4073,12 which encodes a suomilide-like
BGC (Figure S4), and N-His6-tagged ScAer3 was hetero-
logously overexpressed and purified from Escherichia coli
(Figure S7). When agmatine (9, 0.5 mM) and dimethylallyl
diphosphate (DMAPP, 0.4 mM) were incubated with ScAer3
(20 μM) and magnesium chloride (5 mM) in 2-[4-(2-
hydroxyethyl)piperazin-1-yl]ethanesulfonic acid (HEPES) buf-
fer (pH 8.0, 50 mM), a new peak with a mass consistent with
isopentenylagmatine (11, calcd m/z for C22H34N5O2S+ [M
+H]+ 432.2428; found 432.2428) was detected in the liquid
chromatography−mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analysis after
dansyl chloride (Dns-Cl) treatment (Figure 2A and Figure S8).
Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) analysis revealed a
heteronuclear multiple bond coherence (HMBC) correlation
between H-4 and C-6 of the derivatized product, indicating
that the enzymatic product is N4-isopentenylagmatine (11)
(Figure S9). No other isomers were produced under the tested
conditions. An LC-MS analysis showed that ScAer3 could
utilize geranyl pyrophosphate (GPP) to generate geranyl-
agmatine, although with significantly reduced activity, while
farnesyl pyrophosphate (FPP) was not accepted (Figure S10).
A competitive assay using DMAPP, GPP, and FPP produced
11 as a major product with generaylagmatine as a minor
product (selectivity >90% based on LC-MS analysis, Figure
S10C). Arginine, putrescine, and a synthetically accessible
tetrapeptide containing agmatine (S1, see Supporting
Information) were not accepted as substrates (Figure S11).
Collectively, the results suggest that Aer3 catalyzes the N4-
selective prenylation of agmatine prior to peptide formation.
The related prenylation of arginine residues in the cyclic
argicyclamide and autumnalamide peptides is catalyzed by the
cyanobactin-type prenyltransferases AgcF and AutF, respec-
tively (Figure S12).13 Aer3 is unique, as it catalyzes prenylation
at the internal N atom of the guanidine moiety, in sharp
contrast to the terminal N selectivity of AgcF and AutF. To
gain insights into the evolutionary relationship between Aer3

both encoded in the BGC of microguanidines produced by

in silico analysis of the microguanidine BGC, the two enzymes
were previously annotated as proteins of unknown function,
possibly due to their low sequence similarity to functionally

on the internal guanidine N atom in microguanidines (Figure
S13), AgdS and AgdT may catalyze the geranylation of an

M. aeruginosa PCC981014 both catalyzed internal N-selective
geranylation of agmatine when incubated with GPP (see
Figure S14). These enzymes also accepted arginine as a
substrate, although the relative timing of the geranylation
versus N-methylation and esterification during microguanidine
biosynthesis remains to be determined (Figure S15). These
results suggest that the enzymes in the newly identified clade
(named the aeruginosin-type prenyltransferases) have evolved
to perform the regioselective prenylation of the internal
guanidine N atom of arginine/agmatine substrates. Similarly,
this aeruginosin-type prenyltransferase subfamily may also
participate in the biosynthesis of another N-prenylated
aeruginosin, KB676 (Figure S16).15
The observed activity of ScAer3 implies that the installed
isopentenyl group in 11 is the source of the two additional
carbon atoms of the pyrrolidine ring in Aeap (10). The
conversion of 11 to 10 would necessitate both C−C bond
formation and C−C bond cleavage events that remove three
carbon atoms. Therefore, we investigated whether the putative
Rieske oxygenase AerC is involved in these processes. Rieske
oxygenases are a functionally diverse class of enzymes typically
containing an [Fe2S2] cluster and a mononuclear catalytic iron
center. The members of this protein family function as either
dioxygenases or monooxygenases,16 catalyzing various oxida-
tive transformations such as C-hydroxylation,17 dearomatiza-
tion,18 desaturation,19 and N-oxygenation.20 Furthermore, the
Rieske oxygenase RedG and its homologues are known to
catalyze C−C bond-forming cyclization during the biosyn-
thesis of prodiginines and marineosins.21 Given these catalytic
capabilities of the Rieske oxygenase family members, Aer3 was
hypothesized to perform C−C bond formation between C3
and C7 in 11.
To test this hypothesis, AerC from S. sp. NIES-407312
(named ScAerC) was heterologously expressed and purified
from E. coli (Figure S17). The ultraviolet−visible absorption
spectrum of the recombinant ScAerC purified by Ni-nitrilo-
triacetic acid (NTA) chromatography exhibited absorption
bands around 330, 460, and 560 nm, which are consistent with
a Rieske [Fe2S2] cluster (Figure S18).22 Fe/S quantification
assays23 indicated that ScAerC binds 3.00 ± 0.02 Fe and 2.2 ±
0.2 S per monomer.
To perform in vitro assays using ScAerC, we screened a
series of biological reducing systems that may be necessary to
reduce the Rieske [Fe2S2] cluster to initiate catalysis by
ScAerC. The tested reductases included ferredoxin/ferredoxin
reductase from Synechococcus elongatus PCC 7942 (SeFdx/
FdR),24 flavodoxin/flavodoxin reductase (Fld/FldR) from E.
coli BL21 (DE3),25 Fdx/FdR from S. sp. NIES-4073 (ScFdx/
FdR), and VanB from Pseudomonas sp. strain HR199.26 AerC
(20 μM) and Aer3 (20 μM) were incubated with 9 (0.5 mM),
FeSO4(NH4)2SO4 (200 μM), NADH (0.5 mM), DMAPP (0.4
mM), and MgCl2 (5 mM) under air in the presence of the
various reductase components. The LC-MS analysis following
derivatization with Dns-Cl showed that a new peak was
produced only when SeFdx/FdR was utilized (Figure 3A and
3B). The new product had a mass consistent with the dansyl-
derivatized Aeap (Dns-10) (calcd m/z for C19H26N5O2S+ [M
+H]+ 388.1802; found 388.1802) (Figure 3B), and its identity
was further verified by coelution with the synthetic standard in
LC-MS (Figure 3B). The same product (10) could be
produced using synthetic substrate 11 and AerC (Figure
S19). Product 10 was not formed when any of the reaction
components mentioned above except Fe, including molecular
oxygen, was omitted from the assay conditions (Figure 2A and
Figure S20). Consistent with the other reported Rieske
oxygenase activities,27 the inclusion of an NADH regeneration
system involving a thermostable mutant of glucose dehydro-
genase DN46 (E170K/K252L)28 from Bacillus subtilis

Related enzymatic conversions are reportedly performed by
distinct types of iron enzymes.29 For instance, the pyrrolidine
ring in kainic acid is formed by the nonheme α-ketoglutarate-
dependent enzyme KabC from an N-isopentenyl L-glutamic
acid substrate (prekainic acid), which is suggested to proceed
via a radical-mediated mechanism (Figure S24).30 In
bergamottin biosynthesis, the furan ring fused with the
coumarin core is constructed through the C−O bond-forming
cyclization of isopentenyl-containing demethylsuberosin, fol-
lowed by oxidative C−C bond cleavage (Figure S25).31 These
steps are separately catalyzed by the two P450 monoox-
ygenases, marmesin synthase and psoralen synthase. (Figure
S26).31b,c However, the reaction catalyzed by AerC is
unconventional, as it involves both C−C bond-forming
cyclization and C−C bond cleavage events, mediated by a
single Rieske oxygenase. Whether the cyclization mechanism of
AerC shares similarities with those of the above-mentioned
iron enzymes remains unknown, and the detailed mechanism
of AerC action requires further investigation (Figure S27).
In summary, this study elucidated the biosynthetic pathway
for the pyrroline ring of Aeap (10), a structurally unique
component found in the aeruginosin family of natural products
from cyanobacteria. The pathway begins with the N4-selective
isopentenylation of agmatine, catalyzed by Aer3, a member of a
new clade of bacterial prenyltransferases. Subsequently, the
Rieske oxygenase AerC facilitates both C−C bond-forming
ring closure and C−C bond cleavage, resulting in the
construction of the pyrroline ring in Aeap (10). These findings
broaden our understanding of the functional diversity of
prenyltransferases and Rieske oxygenases while also high-
lighting a unique biosynthetic strategy employed by nature to
form nitrogen heterocycles.
