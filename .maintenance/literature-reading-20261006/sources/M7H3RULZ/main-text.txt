ABSTRACT: Emodin (Emo) glycosides are significant bioactive constituents of Polygonum cuspidatum, a well-known dietary plant
in China and Japan. However, the role of uridine diphosphate glycosyltransferases (UGTs) in Emo glycoside biosynthesis in P.
cuspidatum remains elusive, which greatly hampers their industrial production. Here, first, we successfully identified an Emo
glycosyltransferase, PcUGT73BF6, capable of regioselectively forming Emo 6-O-glucoside, and exhibited sugar donor promiscuity
with a preference for UDP-Glc. Then, G148 in a flexible loop correlated with the donor selectivity in PcUGT73BF6 was revealed as
the key contributor to the abovementioned donor selectivity and preference. Besides, we found that the mutant S394G showed a
novel enzymatic characteristic of glycosylating multiple hydroxyl groups of Emo, and the spacious binding pocket of S194G/S394G
was the main underlying basis of enhanced catalytic efficiency. Our study revealed the molecular mechanism of PcUGT73BF6 for
donor selectivity and also provided promising catalysts for the industrial production of structurally diverse Emo O-glycosides.
KEYWORDS: Polygonum cuspidatum, emodin O-glucosides, sugar donor promiscuity, donor selectivity, catalytic efficiency
1. INTRODUCTION
Polygonum cuspidatum Sieb. et Zucc., a well-known dietary and
medicinal plant, belongs to the Polygonaceae family as a
perennial herb.1 The younger stems and leaves, which taste
sour and crisp, have been used in stir-fries, soups, and drinks in
China and Japan. Its roots and rhizomes have gained significant
attention as functional food and traditional medicine due to
their extensive pharmacological effects, such as antiviral,
hemostatic, analgesic, bacteriostatic, antiinflammatory, and
antitumor activities.2,3 These effects are attributed to the
presence of abundant secondary metabolites, such as
anthraquinones, stilbenes, and flavonoids.4,5 Emodin (Emo),
a crucial component of anthraquinones, is one of the primary
bioactive compounds found in the roots and rhizomes of P.
cuspidatum and contributes to broad pharmacological
activities.6,7 However, the utilization of Emo is severely
restricted due to its poor solubility, scarce bioavailability, and
notable toxicity in vivo. Notably, Emo glycoside derivatives,
such as Emo 8-O-glucoside (E8G), Emo 6-O-glucoside (E6G),
and Emo 6-O-rhamnoside (E6R), have been reported to
enhance antiinflammatory and antitumor properties and also
serve as significant ingredients in drugs, cosmetics, and
nutraceuticals.8−11 However, currently, less knowledge is
available about Emo glycoside biosynthesis in P. cuspidatum.
Glycosylation, catalyzed by glycosyltransferases (GTs), is a
crucial process and has a remarkable effect on improving the
solubility and stability of natural products in plants.12,13
Uridine diphosphate glycosyltransferases (UGTs), which
belong to the GT1 family, are the major glycosylation enzymes
for plant metabolites. Generally, UGTs can use six different
UDP-sugars as sugar donors, including UDP-glucose (UDP-
Glc), UDP-glucuronic acid (UDP-GlcA), UDP-galactose
(UDP-Gal), UDP-xylose (UDP-Xyl), UDP-arabinose (UDP-
Ara), and UDP-rhamnose (UDP-Rha).14−16 Among them,
most glycosylations are inclined to employ UDP-Glc as the
sugar donor, including UGT73CR1, UGT80A33, and
UGT80A34.17 Up to now, several members of the UGT
family with the preference of UDP-Rha or UDP-Xyl have been
characterized in plants and microorganisms, such as
AtUGT89C1, Cm1,2RhaT, At3GGT, GuUGT73F4, and
EriJ.18−23 It was reported that the sugar donor preference
was linked to a flexible loop close to the glycosyl moiety, which
is composed of two amino acid residues.24 In Centella asiatica,
amino acid residues at positions 146 and 147 in CaUGT4
played a key role in recognizing sugar donors.25 In Stevia
rebaudiana, the substitution of V155T located near the glycosyl
group significantly improved the donor specificity of
UGT91D2 toward UDP-Glc.26 At present, though UGTs
required for the biosynthesis of Emo glycosides have been
identified in different plant species, UDP-Glc is found to be the
sole donor. For example, CtUGT73AE1, derived from
Carthamus tinctorius, was capable of glycosylating Emo to
conduct E8G using UDP-Glc as the donor.27 FtUGT73BE5 in

Fagopyrum tataricum was shown to catalyze the glycosylation
of Emo in the presence of UDP-Glc.28 RpUGT73BE14
(Rheum palmatum) participated in the formation of E6G by
transferring the glycosyl group of UDP-Glc to the C6−OH
group in Emo.29 Therefore, exploring the Emo glycosyltrans-
ferases capable of accepting a wide range of UDP-sugars is
much desired for the development of novel Emo glycosides
with structural diversity.
Site-directed mutagenesis presents an efficient strategy for
improving the catalytic efficiency of glycosyltransferases for
natural products.30 Guo et al. designed a combined mutant
with a 12-fold enhancement in the catalytic activity of
UGT94E13 toward rebaudioside M2, utilizing a structure-
guided engineering strategy.31 Li et al. successfully generated
mutants UGTM1−3 and UGTM2−4 through the directed
modification of glycosyltransferases UGTM1 and UGTM2,
resulting in an enhancement of catalytic activity by 2.88-fold
and 3.60-fold for mogrol, respectively.32 Additionally, the
engineered I83A/F285-M variant significantly increased the
yield of Rh1 from 15% to 92%, due to its extensive binding
pocket entrance.33 To date, the identified emodin glycosyl-
transferases are characterized by low catalytic efficiency with
UDP-Glc as the sole sugar donor. Consequently, screening for
more efficient UGTs, along with their engineering through
site-directed mutagenesis, represents a promising strategy for
the industrial production of emodin glycosides as functional
foods.
In this work, we successfully identified two UGTs in P.
cuspidatum, namely PcUGT73BF6 and PcUGT95D10, both of
which were able to catalyze Emo to form E6G, with
PcUGT73BF6 exhibiting higher catalytic efficiency than
PcUGT95D10. The enzymatic function of PcUGT73BF6
was also confirmed in P. cuspidatum using an established hairy
root transformation system. In addition, the sugar donor
selectivity of PcUGT73BF6 was investigated, and its key
residues were further explored by molecular docking and site-
directed mutagenesis. Furthermore, mutants were designed to
facilitate the introduction of multiple hydroxyl groups into
Emo. One particular mutant, S194G/S394G, which demon-
strated improved production of E6G, was constructed, and its
molecular mechanism was subsequently validated. In summary,
the current study not only advances the understanding of the
molecular mechanism of PcUGT73BF6 for sugar donor
selectivity and preference of UDP-Glc but also provides new
promising catalysts for the industrial production of E6G and
other Emo O-glycosides with structural diversity. Besides, the
established hairy root transformation system also offers an
efficient bioengineering approach for significantly increasing
the yield of functional food and medicinal constituents in P.
cuspidatum.
2. MATERIAL AND METHODS
2.1. Plant Materials and Chemical Regents. The seeds
of P. cuspidatum, collected from the Institute of Botany,
Chinese Academy of Sciences, Beijing, China, were germinated
in the Murashige and Skoog (MS) culture medium for 1
month and then transferred to a mixture of nutrient soil and
vermiculite for 6 months under the conditions of 12/12 h
(light/dark), with a temperature of 22 °C and a relative
humidity of 60%. Roots, stems, and leaves of 6-month-old P.
cuspidatum were used to detect the content of Emo glucosides.
For the transgenic lines, the seedlings of P. cuspidatum and
tobacco (Nicotiana benthamiana) were cultivated in the
greenhouse under the same aforementioned growing con-
ditions. UDP-Glc, UDP-GlcA, UDP-Gal, UDP-Xyl, UDP-Rha,
and UDP-Ara were purchased, respectively, from Coolaber
Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China), Hao Yuan Biological
Medicine Technology Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China), MREDA
Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China), and Pinxinuo
Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Tianjin, China). The authentic
reference standards involved in this study are all of HPLC
grade.
2.2. Screening of UGTs Related with the Biosynthesis
of Anthraquinone Glycosides. By comparative transcrip-
tomic and metabolomic analysis of the data obtained in our
laboratory from P. cuspidatum under H2O2 stress, the Pearson
correlation coefficient (PCC) was used to measure the
correlation between the metabolites and genes. Associations
with |PCC| > 0.8 and p < 0.05 were selected, and the network
of genes and metabolites was performed utilizing Cytoscape
software (Cytoscape Consortium, San Diego, CA).
2.3. Real-Time Quantitative Reverse Transcription
PCR. Total RNA was extracted using the RNAprep Pure Plant
Kit (Polysaccharide and Polyphenolic-rich) (TIANGEN,
Beijing, China), and then cDNA was synthesized using Hifair
III First Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix for RT-qPCR
(gDNA digester plus) (YEASEN, Shanghai, China). Specific
primers for real-time quantitative reverse transcription PCR
(RT-qPCR) were determined utilizing Primer Premier 5.0
software, as shown in Table S1. The relative expression level of
the PcUGT73BF6 gene was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt
algorithm. PcNDUFA13 was used as the internal reference
gene in P. cuspidatum and Ntactin as the internal reference
gene in tobacco.
2.4. Sequence Analysis and Phylogenetic Analysis of
PcUGT Proteins. A multiple sequence alignment among P.
cuspidatum UGTs, Arabidopsis thaliana 117 UGTs, and 3
UGTs (CtUGT73AE1, FtUGT73BE5, and RpUGT73BE14)
was carried out using BioEdit software. The amino acid
sequences of these UGT proteins are listed in Table S2. A
phylogenetic tree was constructed using MEGA X software
with the neighbor joining, in which the bootstrap method was
used, and the bootstrap value was 1000 replicates.
2.5. Heterologous Expression and Purification of
PcUGT Proteins in Escherichia coli. The open reading
frame of PcUGTs was cloned using a polymerase chain
reaction (PCR) and ligated to the pGEX-4T-3 vector using the
One Step Cloning Kit (YEASEN, Shanghai, China) to
construct a recombinant protein that was expressed in
Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3). The primers are shown in
Table S1. The recombinant protein was incubated in Luria−
Bertani (LB) liquid medium supplemented with ampicillin (50
μg/mL) and shaken at 37 °C until an OD600 value of 0.6, was
reached. It was then induced by isopropyl β-D-thiogalactoside
(IPTG, 0.1 mM) at 16 °C and 160 rpm for 16 h. The bacteria
were collected using centrifugation, and the sediments were
resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) buffer (50
mM, pH 7.0) by adding phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride
(PMSF, 1 mM). The cells were broken up by ultrasonic
waves in an ice bath. The suspension obtained after
centrifugation (4 °C, 12,000 rpm, 30 min) was purified using
glutathione sepharose (GE, USA). The concentration of
purified recombinant proteins was determined using the
bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay, and the proteins were
subsequently maintained at a concentration of 1 mg/mL.

2.6. Enzyme Activity and Enzyme Kinetics Analysis.
For the enzyme assay of recombinant protein, the reaction
system (200 μL) included 50 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 8.0),
10 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), 10 mM MgCl2, 0.25 mM UDP-
Glc, 0.2 mM substrate, and 20 μg of recombinant protein, and
then, the mixture was carried out at 40 °C for 1 h. The ice
methanol (200 μL) was added to terminate reaction. After
centrifugation (4 °C, 12,000 rpm, 15 min), the suspension
filtered by a 0.22 μm filter membrane was detected by HPLC,
LC-MS, and NMR. For the sugar donor selectivity of
recombinant protein, the enzyme assay was performed under
the conditions as 50 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 8.0), 10 mM
DTT, 10 mM MgCl2, 0.25 mM UDP-sugar (UDP-Glc, UDP-
GlcA, UDP-Gal, UDP-Rha, UDP-Xyl, or UDP-Ara), 0.2 mM
substrate, and 20 μg of recombinant protein at 40 °C for 1 h,
and the ice methanol terminated reaction. For the enzyme
kinetic parameter of PcUGTs, the reaction system consisted of
50 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 8.0), 10 mM DTT, 10 mM
MgCl2, 0.25 mM UDP-Glc, 10−700 μM substrate, and 20 μg
of recombinant protein, also at 40 °C for 0.5 h, and terminated
with ice methanol. The Vmax and Km values were computed
using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software.
For HPLC, the mobile phase contained (A) acetonitrile
(ACN) and (B) water supplemented with 0.1% formic acid
(HPLC grade). The gradient conditions were as follows: 0−20
min, 10%−70% A; 20−25 min, 70%−90% A; 25−33 min,
90%−10% A; 33−38 min, 10% A.
2.7. LC-MS Analysis of Glycosylated Products.
Glycosylated products were analyzed using the Acquity
UPLC system (Waters, USA), combined with a Synapt G2
quadrupole time-of-flight (Q-Tof) high-resolution mass
spectrometer (Waters, UK) equipped with an electrospray
ionization (ESI) source. 5 μL of each sample were injected
onto the Acquity UPLC BEH C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7
μm, Waters, USA). The gradient for 0.05% (v/v) acetic acid in
water (A) and 0.05% (v/v) acetic acid in acetonitrile (B) was
set as follows: 0 to 14.0 min, 5.0% B to 30% B; 14.0 to 17.0
min, 30% B to 90% B; 17.0 to 18.0 min, 90% B to 90% B; 18.0
to 19.0 min, 90% B to 5.0% B; and 19.0 to 20.0 min, 5.0% B.
The flow rate was set at 0.3 mL/min. The ESI source
parameters were set as follows: capillary voltage, 2.1 kV;
sampling cone voltage, 15 V; extraction cone voltage, 3 V;
source temperature, 110 °C; desolvation temperature, 350 °C;
desolvation gas flow rate, 800 L/h; and cone gas flow rate, 30
L/h. Q-Tof MS worked in negative resolution mode to provide
a resolution of 18,000. To ensure the accurate mass
measurement, leu-enkephalin was used as a lock mass in MS
mode (m/z 554.2615 for negative ion mode) at a
concentration of 100 pg/μL with a flow rate of 10 μL/min.
All glycosylated products in the enzyme-catalyzed reaction
were extracted from the MS full scan spectra with the mass
tolerance window at 0.02 Da.
2.8. Molecular Docking and Mutagenesis. The 3D
structures of PcUGT73BF6 and its mutants were constructed
by using the AlphaFold2 server. The molecular docking of
PcUGT73BF6 or its mutants with Emo and UDP-Glc was
carried out by using AutoDock Vina. The complex model of
molecular docking was selected according to the lowest
binding energy and visualized using PyMOL software. The
mutants of PcUGT73BF6 were obtained using a Mut Express II
Fast Mutagenesis Kit V2 (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) according
to the operating instruction, and their primers are shown in
Table S1. Subsequently, the mutants were heterologously
expressed in E. coli BL21 (DE3) and were purified according to
the aforementioned method.
2.9. Molecular Dynamics Simulations. The molecular
dynamics (MD) simulations of the PcUGT73BF6 complex and
its mutant complex were conducted using GROMACS 2020.6
software with the AMBER14 force field34 for all of the atoms.
The protein was put into a 10 Å cubic box with around 22693
SPC water molecules.35 Counter ions (Na+/Cl−) were mapped
to the solvent for neutralizing the charge. Prior to the 1 μs
production simulation, the system was energy-minimized and
balanced based on the default procedures. The molecular
mechanics-Poisson Boltzmann surface area (MM-PBSA)
approach with AMBER14 was used to calculate the binding
free energy.
2.10. Transient Expression of PcUGT73BF6 in the
Tobacco. The coding sequence (CDS) of PcUGT73BF6
without a termination codon, was amplified into the
pCAMBIA1300-DsRED vector and transformed into Agro-
bacterium tumefaciens GV3101 strain. The primer is shown in
Table S1. The bacteria were resuspended in penetrating fluid
buffer (10 mM MES-KOH, 10 mM MgCl2·6H2O, and 150 μM
acetosyringone), and then, the OD600 value was adjusted to
0.8. After dark culture at room temperature for 3 h, the
bacterial solution was mixed with p19 in equal volume and
injected into the lower epidermis of tobacco leaves. After 5
days of greenhouse growth, 100 μM Emo was injected into the
injection site of the leaves for an additional 2 days of growth.
The tobacco leaves were harvested in liquid nitrogen and
stored at −80 °C. The samples were freeze-dried and 50 mg
was weighed in 80% methanol to extract metabolites. Then, the
metabolites were detected by HPLC.
2.11. Expression of PcUGT73BF6 in the Hairy Roots of
P. Cuspidatum. The hairy root transformation system of P.
cuspidatum was performed. The recombinant vector pCAM-
BIA1300-PcUGT73BF6-DsRED was transformed to the Agro-
bacterium rhizogenes K599 strain, and the sediments were
resuspended in penetrating fluid buffer (10 mM MES-KOH,
10 mM MgCl2·6H2O, 200 μM acetosyringone) until the OD600
value reached 0.8−1.0, and then, the strain was cultured in the
dark at room temperature for 3 h. 2-month-old P. cuspidatum
leaves were beveled at the petiole base as explants that were
soaked in infiltrating fluid, but not completely immersed, and
vacuumed for 40 min. Then, the explants were inserted into
sterilized vermiculite and grown in an environment with high
humidity after dark culture for 24 h. Transgenic hairy roots of
P. cuspidatum were identified using a RT-qPCR and DsRED
visualization flashlight (LUYOR-3280) after 30−60 days of
infection. The transgenic hairy roots were collected in liquid
nitrogen and ground into a powder using a mortar. The
samples were freeze-dried and 50 mg was weighed in 80%
methanol to extract metabolites. Then, the metabolites were
detected by HPLC.

3.2. Enzymatic Activity of PcUGTs In Vitro. Four
recombinant PcUGT proteins were expressed in the E. coli
BL21 (DE3) strain and purified using a GST-tag, and then
detected by SDS-PAGE (Figure S1). In order to test the
catalytic activity of PcUGTs toward Emo, an enzyme assay was
carried out using Emo as the sugar acceptor and UDP-Glc as
the sugar donor. The results showed that only PcUGT2 and
PcUGT3 could catalyze Emo to produce E6G, as confirmed by
the authentic reference standard and LC-MS (Figure 2a and
S2), suggesting that the similar regioselective catalytic property
for Emo was shared by PcUGT2 and PcUGT3, by specifically
glycosylating the C6−OH group in Emo. Therefore, PcUGT2
and PcUGT3, designated as PcUGT73BF6 (GenBank
Number: PP129545) and PcUGT95D10 (GenBank Number:
PP129546) by the UGT Nomenclature Committee, respec-
tively, were chosen for further testing. To explore the substrate
specificity of PcUGT73BF6 and PcUGT95D10, anthraqui-
nones, resveratrol (Res), and diverse flavonoids, including

3.4. Sugar Donor Selectivity and Preference of
PcUGT73BF6. The most members of UGTs utilize UDP-
Glc as their preferred sugar donor, and other UDP-sugars such
as UDP-GlcA, UDP-Rha, UDP-Ara, UDP-Xly, and UDP-Gal
are also accepted by plant UGTs.14,46 In order to investigate
the sugar donor specificity of PcUGT73BF6, an enzyme assay
was employed using six different UDP-sugars as potential sugar
donors, including UDP-Glc, UDP-GlcA, UDP-Gal, UDP-Rha,
UDP-Xyl, and UDP-Ara (Figure 4a). When Emo, Que, Kae,
Nar, and Api were used as sugar acceptors, PcUGT73BF6
showed the ability to accept UDP-Glc, UDP-Xyl, and UDP-
Rha, with a preference for UDP-Glc (Figure 4b and S13),
suggesting a pivotal role of the C6−OH group of UDP-Glc in
the catalytic efficiency. Previously, Yin et al. tested the catalytic
performance of FtUGT73BE5 for UDP-Glc, UDP-GlcA, and
UDP-Rha, and they showed that FtUGT73BE5 could accept
UDP-Glc as the sugar donor but not UDP-GlcA and UDP-
Rha.28 Here, when Emo was used as the substrate and UDP-
Rha was used as the donor, a product was generated by
PcUGT73BF6 (Figure 4c). Based on the authentic reference
standard and LC-MS, the product was characterized as E6R
(Figure 4d,e). Thus, PcUGT73BF6 is the first identified
glycosyltransferase able to synthesize E6R. The new compound
formed by PcUGT73BF6 using UDP-Xyl as the donor was
identified through 1D and 2D NMR analysis. The results
indicated that a long-distance interaction between the
anomeric proton of xylose (δH 5.14) and the C-6 carbon
signal (δC 163.6) was observed. Thereby, xylose was attached
to the C-6 carbon, demonstrating the formation of Emo 6-O-
xyloside catalyzed by PcUGT73BF6 (Figures S14−S18). In
general, the majority of glycosyltransferases with promiscuous
sugar donors could accept both UDP-Glc and UDP-Gal due to
their similar steric conformations, such as CaUGT3,
UGT71AP2, and UGT74AN3.25,47,48 However, we observed
that PcUGT73BF6 exhibited no activity with UDP-Gal (Figure
4b). This lack of activity appears to be attributable to the
upward orientation of the C4−OH group, which prevents
UDP-Gal from accessing the long and narrow channel within
the PcUGT73BF6 structure.
3.5. A Flexible Loop Domain Correlated with the
Donor Selectivity of PcUGT73BF6. It was reported that the
sugar donor selectivity and preference of glycosyltransferases
were linked to a flexible loop in proximity to the glycosyl
moiety of sugar donors.20,25 To probe the key residues for
sugar donor selectivity of PcUGT73BF6, the molecular
docking of PcUGT73BF6 with Emo and UDP-Glc/-Xyl/-
Rha was conducted using AutoDock Vina. The 3D structure of
PcUGT73BF6, predicted by AlphaFold2, possessed two β/α/β
Rossmann-like domains that are characteristic of the structure
of glycosyltransferase family B (Figure 5a). Notably, a similar
loop structure consisting of Gly and Ile at positions 148 and
149 in the PcUGT73BF6 structure was observed close to the
C4−OH and C6−OH of the glucose group in UDP-Glc
(Figure 5a). Meanwhile, the hydrogen-bond interactions
between G148 and the C4−OH and C6−OH groups of the
glucose moiety in UDP-Glc were generated, while G148 only
interacted with the C4−OH group in UDP-Xyl or UDP-Rha
(Figure S19). The stronger hydrogen bonds formed between
G148 and UDP-Glc enhanced the stability of the transition
state. Additionally, a shorter distance between G148 and the
hydroxyl groups of the glucose moiety in UDP-Glc was
observed during the 100 ns MD simulation (Figure S20a).
These findings suggested a more stable binding of
PcUGT73BF6 to UDP-Glc than to UDP-Xyl or UDP-Rha,
thereby providing evidence on the preference of PcUGT73BF6
for UDP-Glc. Next, to further understand the mechanism of
the donor selectivity and the preference for UDP-Glc in
PcUGT73BF6, sequence alignments between PcUGT73BF6
and glycosyltransferases favoring UDP-Rha (RhaGT) or UDP-
Xyl (XylGT) were conducted. The results showed that
hydrophobic residues (Val, Ile, or Pro) in RhaGT were
positioned at the same one as G148 in PcUGT73BF6 (Figure
5b). When G148 was substituted by Val, Ile, or Pro, all
mutants abolished the ability to utilize UDP-Glc, UDP-Xyl,
and UDP-Rha (Figure 5c). This finding suggested that the
hydrophilic characteristic of G148 in the loop domain was
essential for the recognition of sugar donors by PcUGT73BF6,
as a rigid loop structure generated by the hydrophobic residues
hampered the formation of indispensable hydrogen bonds with
the sugar donor in PcUGT73BF6. In C. asiatica, it was
reported that by swapping the hydrophobic residues of the
loop structure in CaUGT4 for the corresponding hydrophilic
residues in CaUGT3, UDP-Glc and UDP-N-acetylglucosamine
(UDP-GlcNAc) activities were produced.25 Our study further
elucidated the importance of the hydrophilic/hydrophobic
flexible loop in the donor selectivity of UGTs. In addition, the
replacement of G148 in PcUGT73BF6 with Ser at the
corresponding position in XylGT resulted in a loss of activity
for UDP-Rha and altered the donor preference from UDP-Glc
to UDP-Glc/-Xyl, albeit with a reduced conversion rate
(Figure 5c). However, when G148 and I149 were individually
mutated to Thr and Ser that were commonly found in donor-
promiscuous glycosyltransferases, the expected wider donor
scope of PcUGT73BF6 did not occur. The double mutant
G148T/I149S exhibited an inability to utilize any sugar donors
(Figure S21). Hence, we thought that the existence of a simple
relationship seems less possible between the residues of the
loop domain and donor selectivity. In addition, we found that

3.6. Enhancement of the Catalytic Activity of
PcUGT73BF6 for Emo. PcUGT73BF6 was proven to catalyze
the glycosylation of Emo with a low conversion rate using
UDP-Glc/-Xyl/-Rha as sugar donors, which will severely
restrict its industrial-scale application. In order to gain in-depth
insights into the key amino acid residues required for the
catalytic activity of PcUGT73BF6 for Emo and improve the
conversion efficiency, the interactional residues between
PcUGT73BF6 and Emo were analyzed based on molecular
docking. The analysis of the PcUGT73BF6 complex with Emo
indicated that Q17, S194, and N195 around the binding
pocket could form the hydrogen bonds with Emo, while I95,
F393, and F421 were implicated in the hydrophobic
interactions (Figure 6a). These forces stabilized the substrate
Emo and promoted glycosylation reactions. Their alanine
scanning mutagenesis revealed that the catalytic activity of
variants Q17A, I95A, N195A, F393A, and F421A toward Emo
was either substantially decreased or completely abolished,
while the S194A mutant exhibited a mildly increased activity in
comparison with PcUGT73BF6 (Figure 6b and S23a).

the entrance of the binding pocket, and its substantial steric
hindrance elicited by the elongated side chain severely blocked
the entry of Emo into the active pocket (Figure S23b). The
residue S194 was subsequently replaced with Gly to reduce the
steric hindrance, and an enzyme assay was performed using
UDP-Glc, UDP-Xyl, and UDP-Rha as sugar donors. The
results showed that the introduction of the S194G mutation
could increase the conversion rate of PcUGT73BF6 for Emo

transfer of the glucose moiety from UDP-Glc to the hydroxyl
group of the substrate Emo. Hence, we would try to introduce

of S394 to further enhance the catalytic activity of
PcUGT73BF6 toward Emo. When UDP-Glc was used as the
sugar donor, the variants S394A and S394G showed a more
remarkable improvement in conversion efficiency and were
also capable of catalyzing the formation of two additional Emo
O-glucosides, Emo 1-O-glucoside (E1G) and E8G (Figure 6e

emerged in the mutations of S394A (52.2%) and S394G

mutant S394A did not yield any new products for UDP-Rha,
despite demonstrating increased catalytic activity with
conversion rates of 22.4%, compared to 10.5% for
PcUGT73BF6 (Figure S24). These findings demonstrated
that S394A and S394G were conducive to producing the Emo
glycosides with structural diversity. Next, the rational design of
a double mutant (S194G/S394G) was conducted to further
heighten the catalytic efficiency for Emo, and enzyme assays
were performed. The results indicated that the introduction of
the mutation S194G/S394G led to a substantial increase of
E6G production with a conversion rate of 94.7% and nearly
complete consumption of Emo (Figure 6d). RpUGT73BE14
from R. palmatum, which belongs to the Polygonaceae family
along with P. cuspidatum, is also involved in the formation of
E6G, while its application in industrial production faces the
inefficiency problem due to the low catalytic activity for
Emo.29 To the best of our knowledge, the variant S194G/
S394G represents the most efficient glycosyltransferase
currently available for the yield of E6G. Thus, the variant
S194G/S394G possesses significant potential for the produc-
tion and application of E6G in enzyme engineering.
Emo contains three glycosylated hydroxyl groups, designated
as the 1-position, 6-position, and 8-position. Currently, known
Emo glycosyltransferases can catalyze the glycosylation of only
one or two hydroxyl groups in Emo. For example,
CtUGT73AE and RpUGT73BE14 regioselectively glucosy-
lated at the C8−OH and C6−OH of Emo, respectively.27,29
FtUGT73BE5 displayed the catalytic activities for the C8−OH
and C6−OH of Emo.28 In our study, swapping the 394th
position in PcUGT73BF6 from Ser to Ala or Gly catalyzed the
formation of E1G, E6G, and E8G simultaneously. Hence,
S394A and S394G represent the first identified glycosyl-
transferases able to catalyze three hydroxyl groups of Emo
(C1−OH, C6−OH, and C8−OH). Moreover, the variants
S394A and S394G could also synthesize three products using
UDP-Xyl as the sugar donor. Thus, S394A and S394G are
promising catalysts to increase the structural diversity of Emo
O-glycosides and develop various novel drugs.
3.7. Mechanism of the Enhanced Catalytic Activity of
PcUGT73BF6. To dissect the molecular mechanism of the
double mutant S194G/S394G for improving the catalytic
properties toward Emo, molecular docking and dynamic
simulation were carried out. The S194G/S394G structure
showed a spacious entrance and center of the substrate binding
pocket, with sizes around 9.1 Å and 9.3 Å, which were wider
than those of the PcUGT73BF6 complex (7.8 Å and 6.9 Å)
(Figure 7a,b and S26). The above feature facilitated the entry
and exit of both the substrate and sugar donor into the binding
pocket of S194G/S394G, and rendered some intrinsic support
for the considerable enzymatic activity. In terms of the catalytic
mechanism of UGTs,49,50 the reaction was initiated by
histidine (His) at position 20 (H20) in PcUGT73BF6,
which extracted a proton from the hydroxyl group at the C6
position of Emo. This deprotonation facilitated a nucleophilic
attack on the C1 position of UDP-Glc, leading to the
formation of E6G through the establishment of a 1,6-glycosidic
bond. Thus, the distance between H20 of PcUGT73BF6 or
S194G/S394G and C6−OH in Emo was measured in this
work. We found that the distance between H20 and the
nucleophilic oxygen of Emo was shorter in the S194G/S394G
complex (3.5 Å) compared to PcUGT73BF6 (7.7 Å) (Figure
7c,d). Simultaneously, in the S194G/S394G complex, a smaller
distance between the C6−OH group of Emo and the C1
position of UDP-Glc was also observed (5.7 Å vs 9.0 Å)
(Figure 7c,d). In order to further determine the dynamic
interactions among the catalytic residue, substrate, and sugar
donor, distance variations between the catalytic residue H20
and the nucleophilic oxygen of Emo, along with that between
the C6−OH group of Emo and the C1 position of UDP-Glc
during the 100 ns MD simulation, were also measured. The
results showed that the nucleophilic oxygen of Emo exhibited
an obvious tendency to approach H20 in the S194G/S394G
complex (Figure 7e), suggesting that the C6−OH group of
Emo in the S194G/S394G complex had more proper
potentials for deprotonation in contrast to the C6−OH
group of Emo in the PcUGT73BF6 complex. It was
consistently observed that the distance between the C6−OH
group of Emo and the C1 position of UDP-Glc in the S194G/
S394G complex was shorter than that of the PcUGT73BF6
complex (Figure 7f), indicating that the glucosyl moiety of the
sugar donor was easier to transfer into the sugar acceptor in the
S194G/S394G complex. Correspondingly, the binding free
energy analysis revealed that the remarkably enhanced binding
affinity for Emo in the S194G/S394G complex occurred due to
the reduced binding free energies (−0.3 kcal mol−1 for S194 in

In summary, this study successfully identified a glycosyl-
transferase, PcUGT73BF6, capable of regioselectively catalyz-
ing the C6−OH group of Emo, with UDP-Glc, UDP-Xyl, and
UDP-Rha as sugar donors showing a preference for UDP-Glc.
Notably, PcUGT73BF6 is the first glycosyltransferase identi-
fied in the literature to synthesize E6R to the best of our
knowledge. The analysis of PcUGT73BF6 in complexes with
Emo and the sugar donors indicated that the hydrophilic
characteristic of G148 in the flexible loop was responsible for
the sugar donor selectivity. To test the hypothesis that the
narrow substrate-binding pocket in the PcUGT73BF6
structure may restrict the catalytic reaction for Emo, mutants
aimed at expanding the active pocket were constructed. The
results demonstrated that variants S394A and S394G not only
showed remarkable improvement in the catalytic efficiency but
also catalyzed the formation of structurally diverse Emo O-
glycosides. Additionally, the double mutant S194G/S394G
exhibited higher production of E6G in comparison with
PcUGT73BF6 and other characterized plant Emo UGTs.
Taken together, our study offers mechanistic insights into the
sugar donor selectivity of PcUGT73BF6 and provides
potentially valuable biocatalysts for yielding valuable E6G
and other Emo O-glycosides.
