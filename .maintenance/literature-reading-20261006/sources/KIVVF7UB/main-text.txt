ABSTRACT: Celastrol is a bioactive pentacyclic triterpenoid with
promising therapeutic effects that is mainly distributed in Celastraceae
plants. Although some enzymes involved in the celastrol biosynthesis
pathway have been reported, many biosynthetic steps remain unknown.
Herein, transcriptomics and metabolic profiles of multiple species in
Celastraceae were integrated to screen for cytochrome P450s (CYPs) that
are closely related to celastrol biosynthesis. The CYP716 enzyme,
TwCYP716C52, was found to be able to oxidize the C-2 position of
polpunonic acid, a precursor of celastrol, to form the wilforic acid C.
RNAi-mediated repression of TwCYP716C52 in Tripterygium wilfordii
suspension cells further confirmed its involvement in celastrol biosyn-
thesis. The C-2 catalytic mechanisms of TwCYP716C52 were further
explored by using molecular docking and site-directed mutagenesis
experiments. Moreover, a modular optimization strategy was used to construct an engineered yeast to produce wilforic acid C at a
titer of 5.8 mg·L−1. This study elucidates the celastrol biosynthetic pathway and provides important functional genes and sufficient
precursors for further enzyme discovery.
T
riterpenoids are widely distributed plant natural products
with diverse structures, among which tetracyclic and
pentacyclic triterpenoids represent the dominant triterpenoid
structures.1 Tetracyclic triterpenoids mainly include dammar-
ane-, lanostane-, cycloartane-, cucurbitane-, and protostane-
type structures,2 while pentacyclic triterpenoids include
oleanane-, ursane-, lupane-, and friedelane-type.3,4 Pentacyclic
triterpenoids have a higher abundance in comparison to
tetracyclic triterpenoids in medicinal plants. Pentacyclic
triterpenoids are often sources of therapeutic agents with
anticancer,5 anti-inflammatory,6 antidiabetic,7 and other bio-
logical activities, which play an important role in clinical
treatment. Generally, triterpenoids are obtained via extraction
from the plant material. However, this approach has many
limitations.8 Heterologous sustainable production of target
compounds by microbial hosts might be an alternative. Many
natural products with therapeutic effects have been biosynthe-
sized in heterologous hosts, for example, ginsenosides,9
oxygenated taxanes,10 artemisinic acid,11 carnosic acid,12
levopimaradiene,13 and cannabinoids,14 which opens up
more possibilities for the heterologous biosynthesis of valuable
compounds.
Celastrol is an important friedelane-type triterpenoid, mostly
extracted from the Celastraceae family of plants, like
Tripterygium wilfordii,15 Tripterygium regelii,16 Celastrus orbicu-
latus,17 and Celastrus scandens.18 Celastrol showed potential
applications in treating diseases of global concern, given its
diverse bioactivities, including anticancer activities,19 antiobe-
sity effects,20 and therapeutic effects against neurodegenerative
diseases.21 The excellent therapeutic potential of celastrol has
prompted recent widespread attention.
However, the knowledge of the enzymes involved in the
celastrol biosynthetic pathway is incomplete. 2,3-Oxidosqua-
lene cyclases (OSCs) catalyze the first committed reaction of
triterpenoid scaffold formation, converting the substrate 2,3-
oxidosqualene into diverse triterpenoid skeletons.22,23 The
triterpenoid skeletons are then oxidatively modified by
cytochrome P450s (CYPs), further enriching the structural
diversity of triterpenoids.24,25 Friedelin, a catalytic product of
friedelane-type OSCs, is the key scaffold for celastrol
biosynthesis.26 The molecular mechanisms of friedelane-type
triterpene cyclases were further explored.27 It is speculated that

Further exploration of the relationship between the structure

Here, CYP candidates were mined based on multiple
transcriptomes and celastrol tissue distribution patterns.
Functional characterization in yeast demonstrated that
TwCYP716C52 and its two orthologues could oxidize C-2

microbial incubation experiment confirmed the catalytic

molecular docking, and site-directed mutagenesis experiments
were conducted to explore their effect on enzyme functions,
revealing the C-2 catalytic mechanism to some extent. A cell
factory that could produce wilforic acid C was constructed
through synthetic biology. This study sheds light on the
biosynthetic pathway of celastrol and provides some necessary
gene modules from multiple species for its complete
biosynthesis.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Multiple Transcriptome Analysis Identified 31 Can-
didate CYP Genes. Previous studies showed that celastrol
was specifically accumulated in the roots of T. wilfordii, T.
regelii, T. hypoglaucum, and C. orbiculatus. Therefore, tran-
scriptomes of these four species were used to identify CYP
genes specifically expressed in roots. In addition, considering
the similar distribution pattern of celastrol in these species,
CYPs with homologous sequences in at least one other species
were regarded as key candidate genes. As shown in Figure 1, a
total of 96 CYPs in these four plants were found to be
specifically expressed in roots. A multiple sequence alignment
of these 96 CYPs was conducted to verify the orthologs.

Finally, 31 CYPs, marked in orange in Figure 1, were identified
as key candidates for which isoenzyme genes could be found in
at least one other species in our study. For example, Tw10150
exhibited the same tissue expression profile as its probable
isoenzyme genes Tr1782 and Th1604, although they were
from three different species. In addition, the sequence
similarities among Tr15696, Th1683, and Co1559 were as
high as 90%, showing similar tissue expression patterns. Thus,
combining cross-species transcriptome analysis and metabolic
profiling opens a way to efficiently screen candidate genes.
Phylogenetic Analysis of CYPs. Of the 31 candidates
from transcriptomics, 21 full-length cDNA clones were
obtained. CYPs from T. wilfordii were cloned and reported
in previous studies.30 Phylogenetic analysis showed that they
were clustered in branches representing different families
belonging to the CYP71, -72, and -85 clans (Figure 2). It was
reported that CYPs in families like CYP716, CYP72, and
CYP71 were closely related to the triterpenoid biosynthesis.24
Among them, Co1869 and Tr1839 were clustered with
Tw1286 (TwCYP712K1)30 and Th1674 (ThCYP712K1),32
which were reported to oxidize friedelin at the C-29 position to
generate polpunonic acid. Additionally, Tw10150, Tr1782, and
Th1604 clustered in the CYP716 family of the CYP85 clan
were likely related to triterpenoid structural modifications.
Furthermore, Tw13766, Tr1806, and Th1683 clustered in the
CYP71 family and Co1599 clustered in the CYP72 family were
also considered to be possibly involved in triterpenoid
biosynthesis.
CYP716C52s Catalyze the C-2 Hydroxylation of
Polpunonic Acid to Form Wilforic Acid C. To investigate
the function of CoCYP712K1 (Co1869) and TrCYP712K1
(Tr1839), they were coexpressed with TwOSC1 and TwCPR3
in yeast, respectively. UPLC/Q-TOF-MS analysis of all the
fermentation extracts showed that compared with the control
group, the products 29-hydroxy-friedelin (peak 1, Figure S1)
and polpunonic acid (peak 2, Figure S2) both appeared. The
results demonstrated that CoCYP712K1 and TrCYP712K1
could also catalyze the oxidation of friedelin at the C-29
position to generate 29-hydroxy-friedelin and polpunonic acid,
which was consistent with the catalytic functions of
TwCYP712K1 and ThCYP712K1. To screen for CYPs that
could further oxidize polpunonic acid, candidates were
coexpressed with TwOSC1, TwCPR3, and TwCYP712K1 in
yeast. Metabolite profiling of yeast fermentation products was
also conducted using UPLC/Q-TOF-MS. In contrast to the
control group, a unique peak (peak a) was observed at 24.0
min with a [M + H]+ of 473.36 m/z in yeast harboring
Tw10150, Tr1782, or Th1604 (Figure 3A). These CYPs were
renamed by the nomenclature committee as TwCYP716C52,
TrCYP716C52, and ThCYP716C52, respectively. The Gen-
Bank accession numbers are given in Table S1. Based on its
mass, we speculate that peak a may be a hydroxylation product
of polpunonic acid. Purification and NMR analysis (in
CD3OD) demonstrated the presence of a hydroxyl group at
the C-2 position of the polpunonic acid scaffold, allowing us to
identify peak a as wilforic acid C (Figures S3, S4 and Table
S2). NMR analysis was repeated in C5D5N to permit
comparison with literature values, and these data were
consistent with previously reported data of wilforic acid C
isolated from T. wilfordii (Figure S5 and Table S3).33
To further validate the catalytic function of TwCYP716C52,
a microbial incubation experiment was conducted. Due to
insufficient polpunonic acid standard, 29-hydroxy-friedelin was
used as the substrate for incubation. As shown in Figure 3B,
when supplemented with 29-hydroxy-friedelin (peak c),
polpunonic acid (peak b) could be formed by TwCYP712K1.
Then, the final product, wilforic acid C (peak a), could be
synthesized upon the oxidation of polpunonic acid by
TwCYP716C52. The mass fragmentation spectrum of peak a
is shown in Figure 3C, and the putative catalytic process is
shown in Figure 3D.
Therefore, a CYP enzyme (TwCYP716C52) with a C-2
hydroxylation function toward polpunonic acid was identified,
whose hydroxylation product is wilforic acid C, a compound
found in T. wilfordii,33 consistent with the recent report of
Zhao et al.31 Additionally, two TwCYP716C52 orthologues
(ThCYP716C52 and TrCYP716C52) from another two
species were also identified, which enriched the species

RNAi Indicates a Role for TwCYP716C52 in Celastrol
Biosynthesis. A previous study reported that following
induction with methyl jasmonate, the content of celastrol in
the suspension cells of T. wilfordii significantly increased
compared with that in the untreated group.34 Correspondingly,
the transcript level of TwCYP716C52 in the suspension cells
could be significantly promoted by the induction of methyl
jasmonate (Figure S6), implying an internal relationship
between TwCYP716C52 and celastrol biosynthesis.
To further evaluate the potential biological role of
TwCYP716C52, an RNAi experiment was performed. The
vector RNAi-TwCYP716C52 was constructed (Figure 4A) and
transformed into T. wilfordii suspension cells. The Egfp
fragment was amplified using specific primers (Table S4) as
shown in Figure 4B, which proved that the vectors were
successfully transformed into suspension cells. Subsequently,
qRT-PCR analysis suggested that the transcript level of
TwCYP716C52 was significantly downregulated, resulting in
a significant decrease in the accumulation of celastrol
compared with the control group (Figure 4C, 4D). The
results presented above support our suspicion that
TwCYP716C52 participates in celastrol biosynthesis.
Exploring Key Amino Acids in the Active Pocket of
TwCYP716C52. To study the key amino acids that affect the
catalytic activity of TwCYP716C52 and further explore its
catalytic mechanism, the predicted protein structure of
TwCYP716C52 (Figure S7) was constructed. The substrate
polpunonic acid was docked into TwCYP716C52, showing
that the C-2 position of polpunonic acid was oriented close to
the active center of the heme (Figure S8A). In addition, 15
amino acid residues located within the active pocket were
identified, including H110, F118, Y119, K122, Q141, A143,
L144, I221, E288, A289, G292, S295, A296, T300, and L364
(Figure S8B). In particular, a hydrogen bond between the
L144 residue and the substrate might be crucial for stabilizing
the conformation of the substrate within the active pocket.
The effect of active residues on enzyme activity was studied
by alanine scanning. Twelve residues (each of the 15 active
residues except for A143, A289, and A296) were exchanged
with alanine by site-directed mutagenesis. The mutants
H110A, F118A, Y119A, G292A, and A296H lost their catalytic
functions (Figure 5A). These five sites were further mutated to
residues with the same or opposite polarities. As shown in
Figure 5B, the enzymatic activities of most mutants were
hindered, except for F118C. This phenomenon indicated that
the five active sites, H110, F118, Y119, G292, and A296, are
essential for the catalytic function of TwCYP716C52.
Although the other 10 mutants (K122A, Q141A, A143E,
L144A, I221A, E288A, A298F, S295A, T300A, and L364A)
retain catalytic function, their activities were reduced
compared with the wild type (WT) (Figure 5A). These active
residues were also mutated to residues with the same or
opposite polarities. Results (Figure 5C) showed that most of

the C-2 catalytic mechanism of CYP716C52 and the enzymatic
structure−activity relationship, which also demonstrated the
catalytic plasticity of the CYP enzyme and provided the
possibility for enzyme engineering to obtain high-yield
variants.35
Biosynthesis of Wilforic Acid C in Yeast. To construct
an engineering strain of S. cerevisiae capable of producing
wilforic acid C, the friedelin synthase module and the
combinatorial CYP module were introduced into BY-T3, a
yeast strain that produces 2,3-oxidosqualene in high yield.
First, TwOSC1 was identified as the most active friedelin
synthase among the nine different friedelin synthases (Figure
6A). The TwOSC1T502E mutant showed a higher catalytic
activity than the WT, so TwOSC1T502E was identified as the
optimal friedelin synthase. Second, different combinations of
CYPs were set to obtain the optimal module. By screening the
optimal polpunonic acid synthase (groups A−D), we found
that
when
TwCYP716C52
was
coexpressed
with
ThCYP712K1 from T. hypoglaucum, the production of wilforic
acid C was the highest (group D, Figure 6B). On this basis,
ThCYP712K1 was coexpressed with wilforic acid C synthases
from different species (groups D−F). As a result, when
ThCYP712K1 was coexpressed with TrCYP716C52 from T.
regelii (group E, Figure 6B), the yield of wilforic acid C was
maximized. Therefore, these two CYPs were identified as the
optimal CYP combinatorial module.
Genes of these two modules were codon-optimized and
incorporated into the genome of BY-T3 to obtain strain LY2
(Figure 6C). By fermenting in shake flasks, we found that the
highest yield of wilforic acid C was 0.28 mg·L−1 in strain LY1,
while strain LY2 exhibited a higher yield of 2.5 mg·L−1,
representing a 790% increase in production (Figure S9). To
further increase the yield of LY2, high-density fermentation
was performed in a 10 L bioreactor. As shown in Figure 6D,
strain LY2 yielded a maximum titer of 5.8 mg·L−1, an increase
of 130% compared with fermentation in shake flasks.
Although the engineered S. cerevisiae could heterologously
produce wilforic acid C with a yield of 5.8 mg·L−1, it is possible
to apply other bioengineering technologies to further increase
its production. For example, studies have shown that the
subcellular distribution of terpenoids in cells affects the
accumulation and tolerance of terpenoids in organisms.
Peroxisomes and lipid droplets are two important organelles
for storing lipophilic components, among which peroxisomes
have been shown to store large amounts of squalene and are
involved in cellular detoxification.36 Since precursors for
celastrol biosynthesis, like 2,3-oxidosqualene and friedelin,
are hydrophobic, anchoring related enzymes in specific
organelles through subcellular localization may efficiently
facilitate the production and storage of the precursors, thereby
increasing precursor supply. Furthermore, in heterologous
expression systems, the low catalytic efficiency of CYPs usually
limits the synthesis of target compounds, which may be
because CYPs are located in the endoplasmic reticulum of
yeast, separated from the organelle compartment, where the
upstream precursors are located. It has been reported that
CYPs were targeted to lipid droplets using the yeast perilipin 1
protein, thereby significantly increasing the production of
ginsenosides.37 Therefore, the above-mentioned potential
strategies could be applied to increase the production of
wilforic acid C, which might open an avenue for the efficient
production of friedelane-type triterpenoids.
■CONCLUSION
We combined multispecies transcriptional databases to mine
candidate CYP genes in the celastrol biosynthetic pathway, and
functional validation in the heterologous system permitted
further elucidation of the C-2 oxidation step in celastrol
biosynthesis. Functional CYP genes from multiple species were
also obtained, further enriching the gene sources. Together
with the RNAi experiment of TwCYP716C52, we recon-
stituted an important biosynthetic step from polpunonic acid
to wilforic acid C in plants, which lays the foundation for
elucidating the entire biosynthetic pathway of celastrol and
might provide insights into the functional study of enzymes in
the CYP716C subfamily. Moreover, engineering strategies
optimized for different functional enzyme modules were
applied in this study to realize the heterologous production
of wilforic acid C, which could provide chassis cells for the
subsequent generation of celastrol intermediates.

Screening of CYP Candidate Genes. The specific distribution
of celastrol in roots has been confirmed in several species of
Celastraceae.27 Therefore, CYPs specifically expressed in roots were
likely to be considered to be related to celastrol biosynthesis. The
expression of transcripts of each CYP was analyzed based on the
transcriptomes. The raw RNaseq data have been deposited under
BioProject numbers as follows: T. wilfordii (PRJNA542587), T. regelii
(PRJNA763346), T. hypoglaucum (PRJNA763998), and C. orbiculatus
(PRJNA763779). The expression level of CYP genes was represented
by the fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads
(FPKM) according to the transcriptome files. The gene expression
heatmap was constructed using TBtools,39 and cluster analysis was
performed using the default parameters. Then, the BLAST GUI
Wrapper of the TBtools software was used to align the CYPs
specifically expressed in roots from different species, determining the
sequence homology of CYPs.
Cloning of CYP Candidate Genes. Total RNA was extracted
using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide method,40 and the first-
strand cDNA was reversed using the same method.27 According to the
transcriptome sequences of CYP genes, specific primers were
designed as shown in Table S5, and the CYP genes were amplified,
ligated into the cloning vector, and transformed into chemically
competent cells using a previously described method.30 After
screening with ampicillin (100 mg·L−1), single colonies were picked
and verified by sequencing.
Phylogenetic Analysis. The neighbor-joining tree was con-
structed in Mega 7.0 by using the method of Poisson correction. The
bootstrap value was set to 1000. The resulting tree was processed with
iTOL (https://itol.embl.de/).41
Functional Characterization in Yeast. The open reading frames
(ORFs) of TwOSC1 and TwCPR3 were constructed into the pESC-

promoters, respectively.30 The ORFs of CYP712K1s were constructed
into the pESC-His vector (Agilent Technologies) using a seamless
cloning kit (TransGen Biotech). Additionally, the ORFs of other
CYPs were constructed in the pYES2 vector using the same method.
The primers are listed in Table S6.
For the functional characterization of TwCYP712K1 isoenzymes,
the genes TwOSC1 and TwCPR3 were separately coexpressed with
TwCYP712K1 or genes encoding for its isoenzymes in lanosterol
synthase-deficient yeast (-erg7, -ura; ATCC) as previously
described.30 After cultivation, the cells were collected and extracted
with ethyl acetate. Samples were dissolved in methanol and detected
using UPLC/Q-TOF-MS as described above.
For the functional characterization of other CYP candidates, the
lanosterol synthase-deficient yeast containing TwOSC1, TwCPR3, and
TwCYP712K1 was made into chemically competent cells, and the
pYES2 vector (Invitrogen) containing CYP candidate genes was
transformed into the strain, respectively. Moreover, the pYES2
plasmid was transformed into the control group. After selection on
solid Leu His Ura Minus Media (FunGenome) with 2% glucose,
positive colonies were incubated in 30 mL of Leu His Ura Minus
Media (2% glucose) at 30 °C (200 rpm) for 48 h. Then, it was
replaced with YPL medium of equal volume for induction for 3 days.
Cells were processed and analyzed using UPLC/Q-TOF-MS as
described above.
Incubation Experiment in Yeast. For microbial incubation
experiments, the pESC-Leu vector containing TwCPR3, the pESC-His
vector containing TwCYP712K1, and the pYES2 vector containing
TwCYP716C52 were transformed into lanosterol synthase-deficient
yeast. The yeast containing TwCPR3, TwCYP712K1, and the pYES2
vector was also set as the control group. After selection on solid Leu
His Ura Minus Media (2% glucose), positive colonies were incubated
in 20 mL of Leu His Ura Minus Media (2% glucose) at 30 °C (200
rpm) until the OD600 reached 0.8−1.0. Media were discarded, and
strains were induced with 20 mL of YPL medium for 12 h. The strains
were supplemented with the substrate 29-hydroxy-friedelin (Bio-
BioPha) at a final concentration of 30 μM and cultured for another 36
h. Then, cells were extracted and analyzed using UPLC/Q-TOF-MS
as described above.
Fermentation, Extraction, and Isolation of Wilforic Acid C.
The lanosterol synthase-deficient yeast transformed with pESC-Leu::
(TwOSC1 + TwCPR3), pESC-His::TwCYP712K1, and pYES2::Tw-
CYP716C52 was named strain LY1. To enrich the product, strain LY1
was cultured in a 10 L bioreactor (Eppendorf). A single colony was
incubated in 40 mL of Leu His Ura Minus Media (2% glucose) at 30
°C with a shaking speed of 200 rpm for 24 h. Then, the mixture was
inoculated into 400 mL of the same medium and cultivated for 16 h.
The seeds were inoculated into a bioreactor containing 3 L of Leu His
Ura Minus Media (2% glucose) with an initial OD600 of 0.5. Yeast was
cultured at 30 °C with the pH maintained at 5.5. The dissolved
oxygen level was kept at a minimum of 30%. Then, 6× His Ura Minus
Media (48 g·L−1) was added to the mixture (0.1 mL·min−1). After the
mixture was cultivated for 48 h, 40% galactose was added (0.1 mL·
min−1) to induce expression. Fermentation culture was collected at 96
h.
A total of 36 L of the fermentation culture was obtained. Cells were
separated from the culture mixture by centrifugation, resuspended in
distilled water, and ultrasonically extracted with ethyl acetate several
times. The culture medium was concentrated by using rotary
evaporation, and ethyl acetate was added for ultrasonic extraction
several times. The extract was dissolved in methanol, and the crude
product was purified using mass spectrometry-guided preparative
liquid chromatography (Agilent 1260 diode array detector).
Separation was performed using a SunFire C18 OBD prep column
(19 × 150 mm, 5.0 μm, Waters) with a flow rate of 10 mL·min−1. The

400 nm. The total ion chromatogram (TIC) was monitored in
positive ion mode (100−500 m/z). In addition, the precursor ion m/z
473 and the product ion m/z 455 were detected in single-ion
monitoring (SIM) mode.

Molecular Docking and Site-Directed Mutagenesis. The 3D
model of TwCYP716C52 was constructed using Alphafoldv243 in the
local server. The protein 4NKV44 was downloaded from the RCSB
PDB database, and the heme structure was extracted from the
complex using PyMOL software. Molecular docking of heme with
TwCYP716C52 protein was performed using the AutoDock4.2.6
program with a genetic algorithm, and the maximum of evals was set
to 2,500,000. Protein and ligand were set as rigid and flexible,
respectively. Gasteiger charges were added, and the atom type was
assigned as the AD4 type. The output results were converted into a
pdb format using Open Bable2.4.1 and visualized in PyMOL.
Molecular docking of polpunonic acid with the TwCYP716C52-
heme complex was performed using the AutoDock4.2.6 program as
described above. The docking results were converted into a pdb
format using Open Bable2.4.1 and analyzed using PyMOL. The cavity
within 3.5 Å from the substrate served as the active pocket. Amino
acid residues located within the active pocket were subsequently
investigated.
The plasmid pYES2::TwCYP716C52 served as the template. The
mutated plasmids were constructed according to the guidance of the
Fast Mutagenesis System kit (TransGen Biotech) with the
corresponding primers (Table S7). Mutated plasmids that we were
unable to construct using the above method were instead constructed
according to the instructions of the pEASY-Basic seamless cloning and
assembly kit (TransGen Biotech) with the primers in Table S8.
For quantitative product analysis, a chassis strain capable of stably
producing the substrate, polpunonic acid, was constructed. The
codon-optimized (opt) genes, including TwOSC1T502Eopt and
TwCPR3opt, as well as TwCYP712K1, were incorporated into S.
cerevisiae BY-T345 at the chromosome XVI 15 site using Goldengate
Assembly.46 The tail arms and homologous head were constructed as
previously described.47 Gene expression was initiated under strong
constitutive promoters, including PTDH3, PADH1, PPGK1, and PTEF2,
obtaining chassis strain PA1 (Figure S10) using the primers shown in
Table S9. The mutated and WT plasmids were individually
transformed into strain PA1. Cells were cultured and extracted
using the same method. Samples were detected using Q Exactive HF
LC-MS/MS as described above.
Engineering of S. cerevisiae for Heterogeneous Production
of Wilforic Acid C. To heterologously produce wilforic acid C in S.
cerevisiae, the two modules (friedelin synthase module and CYP
module) were introduced into the chromosome of BY-T3, an
engineered strain with a high production of 2,3-oxidosqualene. First,
the yield of nine friedelin synthases reported in our previous studies27
was compared: TwOSC1, TwOSC3, ThOSC1, ThOSC2, CoOSC1,
CoOSC2, TrOSC2, TrOSC3, and TrOSC6. The samples were
detected by gas chromatography−mass spectrometry using a
previously reported method.27 The CYP module comprised CYPs
from two adjacent catalytic steps, including polpunonic acid and
wilforic acid C synthases. To identify the polpunonic acid synthase
with the highest yield, we compared the activities of TwCYP712K1
and its isoenzymes TrCYP712K1, CoCYP712K1, and ThCYP712K1.
Owing to the insufficient authentic standard of polpunonic acid, they
were separately coexpressed with TwCYP716C52, resulting in four
groups (A−D, Table S10). The yield of wilforic acid C was detected
using Q Exactive HF LC-MS/MS as described above. Based on the
above results, the enzymatic activities of TwCYP716C52,
TrCYP716C52, and ThCYP716C52 were further compared. They
were coexpressed with the highest-producing polpunonic acid
synthase ThCYP712K1, respectively, resulting in another three
groups (D−F, Table S10). Then, the production of wilforic acid C
was also detected.
The highest-producing friedelin synthase genes TwOSC1 and CYPs
(ThCYP712K1 and TrCYP716C52) were codon-optimized and
integrated into the genome of BY-T3. Since the module “L1-TTPI1-
TwOSC1T502Eopt-PTDH3-PADH1-TwCPR3opt-TPGI-L2” has been success-
fully constructed as described above, the modules “PPGK1-ThCYP712-
K1opt-TADH1-L2” and “PTEF2-TrCYP716C52opt-TCYC1-L3” were then
constructed using the seamless cloning kit with specific primers
(Table S11). The gene modules, homologous head, and tail arms
were electroporated into strain BY-T3, thereby obtaining the strain
LY2 (Table S12 and Figure S11).
Fermentation. For the production of wilforic acid C, three
individual colonies of strain LY2 were picked into 2 mL of Leu His
Minus Media (2% glucose) and cultivated for 24 h (30 °C, 200 rpm).
This starter culture was used to inoculate 20 mL of Leu His Minus
Media (2% glucose) with an initial OD600 of 0.1 and cultivated for
another 6 days. For comparison with the yield of strain LY1, LY1 was
also fermented under the same conditions except using Leu His Ura
Minus Media (0.2% glucose and 1.8% galactose). As described above,
cultures were extracted with ethyl acetate and injected into Q Exactive
HF LC-MS/MS. The standard solutions of wilforic acid C were
prepared and detected using the same method. The yield of wilforic
acid C was calculated according to the standard curve.
To further improve the yield of wilforic acid C, the strain LY2 was
cultured in a 10 L bioreactor (Eppendorf). The seed was cultured as
described above and then inoculated into a bioreactor containing 3 L
of YPD medium (1% yeast extract, 2% peptone, and 2% glucose) with
an initial OD600 of 0.1. The strain was cultured at 30 °C with the pH
maintained at 5.5. In addition, dissolved oxygen was maintained at
least at 30% by aeration and agitating. In addition, 6× optimized YPD
medium (6% yeast extract, 12% peptone, 4.8% KH2PO4, and 3.6%
MgSO4) was supplemented uniformly (0.1 mL·min−1). After 24 h,
40% glucose was supplied to the culture mixture (0.1 mL·min−1) to
induce expression. Three duplicate samples were collected every 12 h
to detect their OD600 values and yields of wilforic acid C.
