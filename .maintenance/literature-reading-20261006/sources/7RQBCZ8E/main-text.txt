Unveiling the Biosynthetic Logic of Nosiheptide Based on
Reconstitution of Its Bicyclic Thiopeptide Scaffold

ABSTRACT: Thiopeptides, which share a macrocyclic framework
characterized by a six-membered, nitrogen heterocycle central to
multiple (thi)azol(in)es and dehydroamino acids, represent one of
the most structurally complex groups of ribosomally synthesized
and post-translationally modified peptides (RiPPs). Although post-
translational modifications (PTMs) necessary for common frame-
work formation were established, how bicyclic thiopeptides, which
depend on additional specific enzyme activities to afford a side ring
system, are formed remains poorly understood. Using the
biosynthesis of nosiheptide as a model system, here, we report
the first PTM logic to achieve a bicyclic thiopeptide based on in
vivo and in vitro structural reconstitution. Eleven biosynthetic
proteins are employed, processing the precursor peptide through the proper coordination of five PTM steps, of which three are
common and two are specific: (1) formation of five thiazoles, (2) incorporation of an indolic moiety, (3) dehydration of five Ser/
Thr residues, (4) indolic side ring closure, and (5) pyridine formation to establish the thiopeptide framework. Heterologous
expression and biochemical characterization validated that the two macrocyclic ring systems are established in an interdependent and
alternating manner. Distinct from tailoring PTMs, this study unveils a paradigm of a new PTM introduction for expanding the
chemical and biological spaces during the establishment of the group-defining framework.
■INTRODUCTION
Thiopeptide antibiotics constitute a large class of sulfur-rich,
ribosomally synthesized and post-translationally modified
peptides (RiPPs) that are now grouped into the family of
pyritides (Figure 1A).1,2 Because of potent anti-infective
activities and unusual modes of action,3−5 these structurally
related peptide antibiotics have long been the subjects of
(bio)synthetic efforts aiming to improve their physicochemical
properties for clinical use.6−10 As with many bacterial RiPPs,
thiopeptide antibiotics result from post-translational modifica-
tions (PTMs) of a precursor peptide sequence that is
composed of a leader peptide (LP) at the N-terminus and a
core peptide (CP) at the C-terminus. The CP sequence is
transformed into mature product(s) through a process by
engaging the activities of PTM enzymes that are often LP-
dependent. The PTM reactions necessary for a common
thiopeptide framework have been established, including
cyclodehydration and dehydrogenation, dehydration, and a
formal [4 + 2] cycloaddition to achieve the macrocyclic ring
system, in which a nitrogen-containing, six-membered hetero-
cycle domain is central to multiple (thi)azol(in)es and
dehydroamino acids.11−17 In contrast, how a bicyclic
thiopeptide, which relies on additional specific enzyme
activities to furnish a side ring system, is formed remains
poorly understood. Decoration of the shared thiopeptide
framework with a side ring system expands the chemical and
biological spaces, as exemplified by nosiheptide and
thiostrepton, both of which are referred to as the parent
members of thiopeptide antibiotics and have important
applications in the field of sustainable agriculture.3
Nosiheptide (Figure 1A) is a bicyclic thiopeptide widely
used as a feed additive for animal growth.3,18,19 This
thiopeptide has a 2,4-dimethylindolic acid (DMIA) moiety,
which conjugates residues Glu6 and Cys8 in the 26-membered,
main macrocyclic ring system (i.e., the precursor-peptide-
derived, characteristic thiopeptide framework) to give a 19-
membered side ring system.20,21 In 2009, we reported the
biogenesis of nosiheptide in Streptomyces actuosus ATCC
25421 (Figure 1B) and showed that 16 genes are clustered
together to encode the biosynthesis of this thiopeptide and
associated regulation.22 DMIA originates from an independent
Trp precursor, which is processed to 3-methyl-2-indolic acid
(MIA) by NosL (Figure 1C), a radical S-adenosylmethionine
(SAM) protein.23−27 Subsequent MIA incorporation can occur

In this study, using the biosynthesis of nosiheptide as a
model system, we report the first PTM logic to achieve a
bicyclic thiopeptide. Based on in vivo and in vitro structural
reconstitution, we uncovered the proper coordination of
related common and specific PTMs in the biosynthetic
pathway of nosiheptide. To achieve the expected bicyclic
scaffold, the main and side ring systems of this thiopeptide
proved to be established in an interdependent and alternating
manner, exemplifying the strategy of nature by which structural
complexity is developed progressively from a relatively simple
monocyclic framework.

Notably, careful HPLC-HR-MS analysis revealed a trace of
LP-NH2 (C190H301N47O70S2, ESI m/z [M + 4H]4+ mode: calcd
1107.2792, observed 1107.2875 and error 7.50 ppm) from the
above S. lividans strain that harbors the genes nosM* and
nosDEFGHO (Figures 2A and S6). This carboxamidated
peptide sequence was believed to be a co-product of the
monocyclic thiopeptide 5 by transforming penta-dehydrated
2−5 × H2O with NosO (Figure 2B). The transformation
might be initiated by a [4 + 2] cycloaddition reaction that
occurs between the two dehydroamino acids derived from Ser1
and Ser10 to produce a Bycroft-Gowland intermediate,16,35
followed by an aromatization process composed of dehy-
dration and LP-elimination to provide a pyridine-containing
thiopeptide.36 Likely due to poor yield or instability,
thiopeptide 5 failed to be examined in this S. lividans strain.
To experimentally evidence the production of 5, we overex-
pressed NosD, NosE, and NosO in Escherichia coli and purified
these proteins individually to homogeneity for in vitro
biochemical assays (Figure S7).
We started with reconstituting the activity of NosDE by
examining the dehydration of pentathiazolyl 2. In the
biosynthesis of the thiopeptide thiomuracin, Ser dehydration
proved to be conducted in a tRNAGlu-dependent manner by
two proteins: TbtB, the counterpart of NosE that activates Ser
by glutamylation with Glu-tRNAGlu, and TbtC, the counterpart
of NosD that catalyzes the following elimination reaction to
produce dehydroalanine (Dha).11,37 Considering species
difference that might have an impact on the NosE-catalyzed
glutamylation step, we prepared the iso-acceptor tRNAGlu
(CUC) from the nosiheptide-producing strain S. actuosus by
in vitro transcription (Figure S8) and used the associated
glutamyl-tRNA synthetase (GluRS) to produce Glu-tRNAGlu
in situ. Consequently, the incubation of purified 2 with NosDE
in the presence of Glu, ATP, Mg2+, S. actuosus tRNAGlu
(CUC), and S. actuosus GluRS resulted in a dehydration
profile similar to that observed in vivo. Over a 3 h period at 30
°C, ∼96% of 2 was shown to be transformed, producing
dehydrated 2−1 × H2O, 2−2 × H2O, 2−3 × H2O, 2−4 ×
H2O, and 2−5 × H2O (Figure S9). Under the same
conditions, unmodified NosM (1) cannot be processed
(Figure S10), confirming that thiazole formation occurs prior
to Ser/Thr dehydration. Next, the pyridine synthase NosO was
added into the above reaction mixture, leading to the
consumption of 2−5 × H2O (∼17% after additional
incubation at 30 °C for 3 h, rather than 2−1 × H2O, 2−2 ×
H2O, 2−3 × H2O, and 2−4 × H2O, which lack Dha1 and thus
are not amenable to achieving the 26-membered, main
macrocyclic ring system) (Figure 2C). In this partial
transformation, LP-NH2 was observed along with monocyclic
thiopeptide 5, the identity of which was supported by detailed
HPLC-HR-MS and HR-MS/MS analyses (Figure S11).
Clearly, the common PTM enzymes NosDEFGHO do have
the capability to transform the precursor peptide into a
thiopeptide framework. This capability appears to be limited
because 5 and its potential derivatives that arise from treatment
with downstream PTM enzymes (e.g., NosA,38,39 for C-
terminal amide formation via C−N bond cleavage; and
NosC,40 pyridine C5-hydroxylation) were not identified in
wild-type S. actuosus or its mutant strains deficient in side ring
formation.22 Interestingly, thiazomycins E1-E3, the monocyclic
counterparts of 5 derivatives, were reported in Amycolatopsis
fastidiosa MA7332, where the main products are bicyclic
nocathiacins, the naturally occurring analogs of nosiheptide
that are derived from a similar precursor peptide (NocM) but
differ by possessing a Ser residue (instead of Cys) at position 8
(Figure 1A).41,42 We reason that compared with the SH group
of Cys8 in NosM, the OH group of Ser8 in NocM is less
reactive and might lower the efficiency of MIA incorporation
via ester bond formation. This inefficiency can cause the
accumulation of MIA-lacking shunt intermediates and
accordingly the production of relatively more 5-like,
monocyclic thiopeptide products that are amenable to
identification and characterization. Thus, instead of occurring
as degradation derivatives from bicyclic nocathiacins, it is likely
that thiazomycins E1-E3 are the products generated through a
branch from the pathway of nocathiacins by omitting certain
specific PTM steps in A. fastidiosa.
MIA Incorporation Facilitates Thiopeptide Frame-
work Formation. Based on the above experimental
observations, constructing the thiopeptide framework of
nosiheptide might benefit from the biosynthetic steps of
MIA incorporation and/or side-ring closure to build the 19-
membered side ring system. To evaluate the importance of the
specific PTMs related to these two steps, we first introduced
nosIJKL, the genes responsible for MIA formation and
incorporation, into the S. lividans NOS02 strain that contains
nosM* and nosDEFGHO. In the resultant recombinant strain S.
lividans NOS03, 2 and its dehydrated derivatives (i.e., 2−1 ×
H2O, 2−2 × H2O, 2−3 × H2O, 2−4 × H2O, and 2−5 × H2O)
were not observed, suggesting that the inefficient dehydration
of 2 might be prevented because of MIA introduction. Instead,
a distinct thiopeptide (6) was produced along with LP-NH2
(Figures 3A and S12). This thiopeptide, proved to be a MIA-
containing but side ring-opening analog of nosiheptide, was
previously characterized in the mutant S. actuosus strain where
the radical SAM protein-encoding gene nosN was inacti-
vated.22,33 Consequently, whereas side-ring closure might not
be a step indispensable for thiopeptide framework formation,
MIA incorporation could be a PTM more conductive to the
formation of the 26-membered, main macrocyclic ring system,
as proposed here by transforming 3−5 × H2O (i.e., the linear
penta-dehydrated pentathiazolyl precursor with Cys8-S-MIAy-
lation, more than 2−5 × H2O by 157 Da) with NosO to
produce 6 and LP-NH2 in an effective manner (Figure 3B,C).

Related genes were organized in different operons to produce

With the supplementation of MIA, the expected, fully
dehydrated product 3−5 × H2O was observed and
characterized to be a dominant product in E. coli after 24 h

common and specific PTMs appear to work well and
effectively process the precursor peptide. Shortening the
incubation time to 16 h resulted in a small amount of partially
dehydrated derivatives, including 3−1 × H2O, 3−2 × H2O, 3−

detailed HR-MS/MS analysis and structural comparison with
other derivatives characterized in the following studies (e.g.,
3−4 × H2O + DMIA and 4−4 × H2O), the dehydration
positions and patterns of partially dehydrated 3−1 × H2O, 3−
2 × H2O, 3−3 × H2O, and 3−4 × H2O were shown to be
respectively different from those of 2−1 × H2O, 2−2 × H2O,
2−3 × H2O, and 2−4 × H2O (Figures 2B, 3C and S13−S20
and Supporting results), the MIA-lacking counterparts
produced by transforming 2 with NosDE. These results
support that rather than the products obtained by installing
MIA in 2 derivatives, 3−1 × H2O, 3−2 × H2O, 3−3 × H2O,
and 3−4 × H2O are intermediates during the NosDE-catalyzed
transformation of the MIA-containing pentathiazolyl precursor
3 to 3−5 × H2O (Figure 3C). This PTM was deduced to
proceed from Ser12 through Ser1, Thr4, and Ser13 to reach
Ser10, with an order distinct from that in the dehydration of 2.
Clearly, Cys8-S-MIAylation can significantly change the
dehydration sequence of NosDE, although the target residues
remain unchanged. This finding is important and indicates that
new PTM incorporation gives rise to structural differences in
peptide substrate and thus might influence the behaving
manners of following biosynthetic enzymes in RiPP biosyn-
thesis.
To support the notion that the dehydration activity of
NosDE follows NosK activity for MIA incorporation, we
overexpressed the adenylation protein NosI, the thiolation
protein NosJ, and the α/β-hydrolase fold acyltransferase NosK
in E. coli for in vitro biochemical assays (Figure S7). Consistent
with previous observations,30 the incubation of pentathiazolyl
2 with synthetic MIA and ATP in the presence of purified
NosI, the holo-form of NosJ and NosK led to the ∼76%
transformation of 2 into 3 by S-acylation of the Cys8 residue
over a 10 min period at 30 °C (Figure S21). The
acyltransferase activity of NosK appears to be flexible, as
Cys8-S-MIAylation also occurred when replacing 2 with the
mixture containing 2−1 × H2O, 2−2 × H2O, 2−3 × H2O, or
2−4 × H2O (Figure S22). The resultant S-conjugates 2−1 ×
H2O + MIA, 2−2 × H2O + MIA, 2−3 × H2O + MIA, or 2−4
× H2O + MIA differ from 3−1 × H2O, 3−2 × H2O, 3−3 ×
H2O, or 3−4 × H2O in dehydration position and pattern,
suggesting that they are shunt peptide derivatives generated in
vitro. In contrast, when purified 3 was incubated with NosDE,
the substrate was consumed after 10 min at 30 °C, and
dehydrations of all five target residues were completely finished
over a 1 h incubation period to achieve 3−5 × H2O (Figures
3D and S23). In addition, compared to the poor trans-
formation of 2−5 × H2O (with ∼17% consumption after
incubation for 3 h at 30 °C), 3−5 × H2O was shown to be
readily converted into the MIA-containing, side ring-opening
thiopeptide 6 and LP-NH2 by NosO under the same
conditions (Figure 3D), confirming that this pyridine synthase
favors 3−5 × H2O over 2−5 × H2O. Thus, the Cys8-S-
MIAylation of 2 greatly facilitates Ser/Thr dehydration and
pyridine formation to give a thiopeptide framework. The above
results support the idea that the specific PTM for MIA
incorporation occurs before the two common PTMs for Ser/
Thr dehydration and pyridine formation in the biosynthetic
pathway of nosiheptide.
Side Ring Closure Occurs between Ser/Thr Dehy-
dration and Pyridine Formation. We introduced nosN, the
gene encoding a radical SAM protein for side ring closure, into
the S. lividans NOS03 strain that contains nosM*, nosDEFGHO
(for thiopeptide framework building) and nosIJKL (for MIA
formation and incorporation). In the resultant strain S. lividans
NOS04, instead of thiopeptide 6, which was not produced, the
co-product of LP-NH2 was observed to be 7 (more than 6 by
12 Da), a distinct thiopeptide sharing the same molecular
weight with a less functionalized, bicyclic intermediate assigned
at the late biosynthetic stage of nosiheptide (Figure 4A,B).38,40
To prepare a standard for structure determination, we purified
this intermediate accumulated in the mutant S. actuosus strain
where nosABC (i.e., the genes coding for post-thiopeptide
modifications) were deleted and then characterized its
structure based on detailed nuclear magnetic resonance data
(Figure S24 and Table S3). Subsequent HPLC-HR-MS
analysis supported the structural identity of 7 to this
thiopeptide intermediate, which has a completed side ring
system (Figures 4A, B and S25). Intriguingly, although side
ring closure is not necessary for thiopeptide framework
construction, the radical SAM activity of NosN can be
arranged properly with the activities of other PTM enzymes
in the pathway, leading to the expected bicyclic thiopeptide
scaffold.
According to the observation that NosN cannot transform
the side ring-opening thiopeptide 6 into the nosiheptide
intermediate 7 by closing the side ring system,33 this radical
SAM protein might function before the NosO-catalyzed
formation of the 26-membered main macrocyclic ring system.
For the timing of this PTM with NosDE-enabled Ser/Thr
dehydration, we took advantage of the heterologous E. coli

■DISCUSSION
Benefiting significantly from recent advances in genome
mining, RiPPs have emerged as a source of chemical
inspiration to drive the discovery and development of new
biological tools for addressing synthetic challenges. Thiopep-
tide antibiotics represent one of the most structurally complex
groups of RiPPs. To individually achieve over 120 naturally
occurring members, multiple biosynthetic steps in a pathway
often include diversity-oriented specific PTMs in addition to
the common PTMs for shared framework formation. For
precise and effective formation of these thiopeptides in
bacterial producers, how to arrange various PTMs in a proper
order is a key issue that needs to be addressed by evolution;
otherwise, enzyme promiscuity might cause a potential
substrate competition and thus branch the pathway to produce
unexpected shunt products.
Compared with monocyclic members, bicyclic nosiheptide
requires specific PTMs to form a DMIA-containing side ring
system that is conjugated with the common thiopeptide
framework. Distinct from the tailoring PTMs at the post-
thiopeptide stage, these PTMs proved to proceed at the
prethiopeptide stage and occur during the establishment of the
characteristic framework (Figure 6). Eleven biosynthetic
proteins are employed in total, to process the precursor
peptide NosM (1) through five PTM steps in a coordinated
and orderly manner (i.e., three are common to establish the
thiopeptide framework and two are specific to build the side
ring system of nosiheptide), using (i) the cyclodehydratase
complex NosGH and the dehydrogenase NosF to install five
thiazoles in 1, yielding pentathiazolyl 2; (ii) the α/β-hydrolase
fold protein NosK to transfer MIA, which is formed by the
radical SAM protein NosL and then is activated through

Clearly, the main and side ring systems of nosiheptide are
constructed in an interdependent and alternating manner. It
should be noted that PTM enzymes such as NosK, NosDE,
NosN, and NosO are catalytically flexible (particularly under in
vitro conditions). This flexibility does cause competition in
peptide substrates and lead to branches of the pathway to a
number of shunt products (Figure S30), including less
dehydrated variants (2−1 × H2O, 2−2 × H2O, 2−3 × H2O,
and 2−4 × H2O) and their further S-MIAylated derivatives
(2−1 × H2O + MIA, 2−2 × H2O + MIA, 2−3 × H2O + MIA,
and 2−4 × H2O + MIA), non-dehydrated but side ring-closed
peptide (4), monocyclic thiopeptides (5 or 6, produced by
processing 2−5 × H2O or 3−5 × H2O with NosO) and
unexpected bicyclic thiopeptide (9, produced by processing 4
with NosDE and then the resultant, less dehydrated derivative
4−4 × H2O with NosO). Interestingly, in native S. actuosus
and even heterologous S. lividans or E. coli, where related
biosynthetic proteins work together, the production of these
shunt products can be effectively prevented or minimized. We
reason that protein−protein interactions between peptide
substrates and PTM enzymes are crucial to establish the
expected biosynthetic order. In addition to the LP sequence,
whose function in PTM engagement has been recognized, the
CP sequence appears to play an increasingly important role
with the proceeding of bicyclic thiopeptide framework
formation. As exemplified by the Ser/Thr dehydration step,
Cys8-S-MIAylation in 2 not only greatly facilitates the
following action of NosDE but also completely changes the
behaving sequence of this dehydratase pair in processing the
five target residues, highlighting the coevolution of related
enzymes and their peptide substrates, which can alter/improve
the compatibility of a thiopeptide pathway to accommodate
additional PTMs. While the activity of NosO appears to be not
sensitive to the side ring being closed or open, the SAM radical
protein NosN must be competitive and function ahead of the
pyridine synthase to ensure the transformation of intermediate
3−5 × H2O into the expected intermediate 8 of nosiheptide
rather than directly into the unexpected side ring-opening
analog 6 by NosO.
■CONCLUSIONS
Based on in vivo and in vitro structural reconstitution, we
clarified the biosynthetic logic by which multiple PTMs are
organized in a pathway to form a bicyclic thiopeptide scaffold,
leading to nosiheptide. The study reported here provides a
natural model for the development of a basic RiPP framework
by introducing additional PTMs to increase the chemical
complexity to meet potential biological requirements, either
internal for biochemical process regulation or external for
addressing environmental concern. This study deepens the
understanding of RiPP biosynthesis and, eventually, will
enhance our capability of rational design, development, and
production of functional peptide agents using synthetic biology
strategies.
