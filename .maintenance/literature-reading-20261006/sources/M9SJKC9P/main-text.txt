Reconstitution of the Early Stage of Chetomin Biosynthesis in
Aspergillus fumigatus Leads to the Production of
Epipolythiodioxopiperazines

ABSTRACT: Using CRISPR-Cas9 technology and a micro-
homology-mediated end-joining repair system, we substituted
genes of the gliotoxin pathway in Aspergillus fumigatus with genes
responsible for chetomin biosynthesis from Chaetomium cochliodes,
leading to the production of three new epipolythiodioxopiper-
azines (ETPs). This work represents the first successful endeavor
to produce ETPs in a non-native host. Additionally, the
simultaneous disruption of five genes in a single transformation
marks the most extensive gene knockout event in filamentous fungi
to date.
E
pipolythiodioxopiperazines (ETPs) are a class of naturally
occurring compounds that feature a characteristic 2,5-
diketopiperazine scaffold bridged by a polysulfide moiety.
Exclusively produced by fungi, ETPs have attracted consid-
erable attention for their broad spectrum of biological
activities, which include antimicrobial, antiviral, and antitumor
effects.1,2 The unique structure of ETPs is crucial, particularly
the polysulfide bridge, which engages in redox reactions that
are often vital to their biological functions. These compounds
are capable of generating reactive oxygen species and can form
covalent bonds with thiol groups in proteins, contributing to
their bioactivity. However, the therapeutic application of ETPs
is tempered by their toxicity and potential adverse effects. The
chemical synthesis, modification of ETPs, and pathway
engineering attempts aimed at elucidating their mechanisms
of action and enhancing their safety profile for medical
applications are active research fields. The chemistry involved
in synthesizing these molecules can be complex, owing to the
structural complexity and the need to form the defining
polysulfide bridge under controlled conditions.3−5
Recent advances in heterologous expression platforms have
boosted the understanding of biosynthetic pathways and the
discovery of microbial natural products.6,7 Fungi such as
Saccharomyces cerevisiae, Aspergillus oryaze, and Aspergillus
nidulans have been widely used as heterologous expression
hosts. The biosynthetic pathways of ETPs have garnered
significant attention with recent advancements in deciphering
the pathways that govern the synthesis (or degradation) of
both regular (gliotoxin8 and acetylaranotin;9 Figure 1) and
unconventional (aspirochlorine10 and pretrichodermamide
A11,12) ETPs. Nevertheless, transplanting ETP biosynthetic
pathways into heterologous systems can be a challenging task
as a result of the complexity of the pathway, protein
interaction, localization issues, and possible toxicity of the
products to non-native cells. While limited reports have delved
into the initial stages of ETP biosynthesis through heterolo-
gous expression in A. nidulans, to the best of our knowledge,
there are no successful reports where complete ETP synthesis
has been achieved in heterologous cell systems.13,14

The opportunistic human pathogen Aspergillus fumigatus is
known to produce various secondary metabolites, including the
notorious ETP prototype gliotoxin, whose biosynthesis has
been extensively explored.15 More recently, the microhomol-
ogy-mediated end-joining (MMEJ) pathway has emerged as a
novel DNA repair mechanism in eukaryotic cells where short
regions of sequence homology (microhomology, typically
spanning 5−25 bp) near the DNA double-strand breaks
(DSBs) are used to facilitate the repair process.16 Although the
detailed mechanisms of MMEJ remain incompletely under-
stood, researchers have successfully harnessed this process for
genetic manipulations in several filamentous fungi.17−20
Here, we reconstituted the early stage biosynthetic steps of
chetomin in A. fumigatus by employing CRISPR-Cas9-assisted
marker-free genome editing along with the MMEJ repair
process. Through this approach, we were able to generate A.
fumigatus mutants capable of producing new ETPs featuring
disulfide, trisulfide, and tetrasulfide linkages that had not been
previously identified. Additionally, we achieved the simulta-
neous deletion of five genes in a single genetic transformation,
representing a new benchmark in filamentous fungi research,
which simplifies the identification and purification of new
compounds as a result of a significant reduction of byproduct
formation.
In our initial efforts to genetically manipulate Chaetomium
cochliodes, the native producer of chetomins, we encountered
significant challenges. Despite several transformation attempts
using different methodologies, we were unable to generate
stable and genetically clean mutants (data not shown).
Consequently, we shifted to a heterologous expression strategy
to elucidate the biosynthetic pathway of chetomins.21 A.
fumigatus, a known producer of gliotoxin with a well-
characterized biosynthetic process, emerged as the most
suitable host for our studies. We employed the widely used
CRISPR-Cas9 techniques22,23 to introduce two DSBs in the
proximity of the start codon and stop codon of gliP in the
starting strain A. fumigatus CEA17 ΔakuB pyrG+ (hereafter
referred to as AfuB001 for simplicity).24,25 For the repair
process, we utilized microhomology with 50 bp flanking
regions adjacent to the gliP gene to facilitate MMEJ repair
(Figure 2). Remarkably, a high success rate was observed, with
14 of the 16 transformants screened via diagnostic polymerase
chain reaction (PCR) exhibiting the expected band size
(Figure S1 of the Supporting Information), demonstrating the
efficacy of employing microhomologous sequences (50 bp) for
the replacement of large genes (gliP = 6.5 kb). The resultant
PCR-confirmed and marker-free mutant was designated as
AfuB002 (Figure S2 of the Supporting Information).
Metabolomic analysis by liquid chromatography−mass
spectrometry (LC−MS) revealed that AfuB002 produces
diketopiperazine (DKP) cyclo-Trp-Ser by comparing its
retention time, high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS),
and tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) fragmentation
patterns and isotopic distributions with a synthetic reference
standard. As the cheP gene was amplified from the gDNA
sequence of C. cochliodes, the identification of cyclo-Trp-Ser
indicates that A. fumigatus correctly splices the introns of cheP
at the appropriate positions. Further scrutiny of the LC−MS
data led to the discovery of three new ETP monomers with
disulfide, trisulfide, and tetrasulfide bridges that exhibited
similar MS/MS fragmentations with chetocochliodin I (Figure
3 and Figure S5 of the Supporting Information). In the MS/
MS spectra, the protonated parent ion [M + H]+ (m/z 368) of
trisulfide ETP was detected, readily losing one fragment of S3
(Δ96) to produce the fragment ion at m/z 272. Subsequently,
this ion could lose one molecule of H2O (Δ18), forming a
fragment ion at m/z 254. Additionally, the loss of an indole
moiety from m/z 272 resulted in the generation of a product
ion at m/z 155 (Figure 3C).26 The MS/MS spectra of disulfide
and tetrasulfide ETPs also showed product ions at m/z 272,
254, and 155, consistent with these fragmentation patterns. In
continuity with a preceding report, these three compounds
were designated as chetocochliodins J, K, and L, respectively.27
The
1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) data of
chetocochliodin L were similar to those of chetocochliodin I,
except that the signals of both −NCH3 in chetocochliodin I
were replaced by two −NH signals in chetocochliodin I (Table
S1 of the Supporting Information). The stereochemistry of
chetocochliodins J, K, and L was proposed to possess the
identical (3S,6S) configuration observed in chetocochliodin I
and chetomins.27,28
The detection of chetocochliodin J implies that the disulfide
bridge has been introduced into the cyclo-Trp-Ser core
through a route analogous to that of gliotoxin biosynthesis
involving the following steps: bishydroxylation (GliC),
glutathione transfer (GliG), sequential glutathione backbone
degradation and C−S bond cleavage (GliK, GliJ, and GliI),
and oxidation (GliT). Interestingly, the heterologous ex-
pression of gliP in A. nidulans leads to the production of
both cyclo-Phe-Ser and cyclo-Trp-Ser.13 The substrate
specificity of the first A domain of GliP accommodating both
L-Phe and
L-Trp may explain the enzymatic machinery
downstream encoded by the gli cluster genes that can process
the CheP product. However, neither cyclo-Trp-Ser nor the

that the sulfide bridge formation can happen prior to the
intermolecular dimerization in chetomin biogenesis, contra-
dicting a previous hypothesis.14
The cytochrome P450 monooxygenase GliF was reported to
catalyze N-heterocyclization in gliotoxin biosynthesis, a step
not required for the synthesis of chetomins. Given the
existence of a second hypothetical P450-CheD within the che
cluster, it was postulated that CheD might be responsible for
the dimer formation. Thus, we hypothesized that replacing gliF
with cheD in AfuB002 could potentially lead to the production
of ETP dimers. To test this hypothesis, gliF in AfuB002 was
targeted and replaced by the coding sequence of cheD, whose
transcription start and termination sites were determined by
rapid amplification of cDNA ends (see the Supporting
Information). The resulting mutant was named AfuB003.
Unexpectedly, after incubation and extraction, the LC−MS
data of AfuB003 did not show any anticipated signals for the
ETP dimers. Previous reports on gliotoxin biosynthesis showed
that GliN-mediated amide methylation is crucial for maintain-
ing the stability of the ETPs and preventing the decomposition
of N-desmethylgliotoxin.29 The substrate specificity of GliN in
A. fumigatus may account for the absence of the ETP dimer in
the AfuB003 strain. In addition, overexpression of the
transcription factor GliZ was reported to increase the
production of gliotoxin in A. fumigatus.30 According to this
knowledge, we carried out further genetic manipulations based
on AfuB003 by replacing gliN with cheN and/or overexpressing
gliZ with the strong constitutive gpdA promoter. After
transformation and verification, three mutants were obtained,
including AfuB004, AfuB005, and AfuB006 (Table S2 of the
Supporting Information). Notably, the dual mutant AfuB006
was created in one transformation event, indicating that
CRISPR-Cas9 combined with the MMEJ strategy can be used
for replacement and insertion purposes concurrently. However,
no ETP dimers were detected in any of the three mutants
based on the LC−MS analysis.
To increase the production of the new ETPs and to enable
the detection of minor intermediates, we decided to delete
potentially competing pathways in the host strain. The
existence of a S-adenosyl-L-methionine-dependent methyl
transferase (S-MT) TmtA29 (also called GtmA31) outside
the gli cluster is a strategy of producers to irreversibly inactivate
ETP toxins, and this may compete with the biosynthetic route
toward ETPs. Among the various secondary metabolites
produced by A. fumigatus, some products use tryptophan as
a biosynthetic precursor. During the examination of the
genome sequence of A. fumigatus using antiSMASH,32,33 we
identified four tryptophan-consuming biosynthetic pathways,
including fumigaclavine C, hexadehydroastechrome (HAS),
fumiquinazolines, and fumitremorgin C. Accordingly, four
homologous genes were targeted, including the dimethylallyl-
tryptophan synthase gene fgaPT2/dmaW,34,35 and three NRPS
genes hasD,36 AFUA_6g12060/fmqB,37 and Afu8g00170/
ftmA.38
We therefore set out to disrupt these five genes
simultaneously. A sizable pFC332-orientated CRISPR-Cas9
vector was assembled by USER cloning, and this constructed
plasmid consists of 10 protospacers, with each pair targeting
two distinctive sites on a single gene. The repair templates
consisted of five oligonucleotides. Despite the low efficiency,
this ambitious endeavor finally resulted in the creation of a
quintuple mutant named AfuB007 (Figure S3 of the
Supporting Information). Notably, during tissue PCR screen-
ing, no single, double, triple, or quadruple mutants were
detected. To the best of our knowledge, this achievement
marks the most extensive simultaneous gene deletion event
reported in filamentous fungi thus far. The previous record was
the simultaneous deletion of four genes in Myceliophthora
thermophila using CRISPR-Cas9 and homology-directed
repair.39 A subsequent metabolic analysis of the AfuB007
LC−MS data with Global Natural Products Social Molecular
Networking (GNPS)40 revealed a potential ETP dimer that we
termed checpd671, with a [M + H]+ ion 671 clustered with the
three ETP monomers. Inspection of its MS/MS fragments
showed characteristic product ions at m/z 272, 254, and 155,
which are consistent with those found in chetocochliodins J, K,
and L. Meanwhile, its retention time differed from those of the
three above-mentioned ETP monomers (Figure S6 of the
Supporting Information). Thus, checpd671 was regarded as a
chetomin pathway product in A. fumigatus.
Previously, less than 6 mg of disulfide-cyclo-Phe-Ser
(indicated as compound 7 in the original publication) were
isolated from 120 L of A. fumigatus ΔgliF mutant culture
broth.41 Here, with the removal of five competitive pathways,
we aimed to increase the yield of chetocochliodins J, K, and L.
Indeed, we isolated 2.5 mg of chetocochliodin L from 5 L of
AfuB007 culture broth.
Altogether, we have performed extensive genetic engineering
using CRISPR-Cas9 and MMEJ methods to reconstitute
chetomin biosynthetic steps in A. fumigatus. By substitution of
gliP with cheP, the new-to-host product cyclo-Trp-Ser and new
ETP monomers chetocochliodins J, K, and L were produced in
the A. fumigatus mutant AfuB002. However, further genetic
replacements within the gli cluster, specifically exchanging gliF
and gliN with cheD, and cheN, did not yield the anticipated
compounds. The strategic deletion of five genes that could
compete for metabolic resources resulted in a cleaner
metabolic profile, revealing a potential ETP dimer checpd671.
From a biosynthetic perspective, the newly identified ETP
monomers are posited to be authentic natural products of the
native producer, C. cochliodes, despite their prior undiscovered
status. These compounds may serve as intermediates or direct
precursors in the biosynthesis of chetomins in C. cochliodes.
With all of the information, a plausible route for synthesizing
chetomins in C. cochliodes is proposed (Figure 4): CheP
catalyzes the formation of DKP, which next goes through
biosynthetic reactions similar to gliotoxin biosynthesis to form
the ETPs before final N-methylation and C−N dimerization to
produce chetomins. All three introduced genes cheP, cheD, and
cheN have been transcribed, as we observed the expected PCR
band sizes with primer pairs spanning an exon−exon junction
using the cDNA of AfuB007 as the template (Figure S4 of the
Supporting Information). The transporter gene in the ETP
cluster might impact the synthesis and exports of chetomins
and, therefore, could explain the lack of chetomin production
