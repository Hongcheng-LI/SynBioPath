ABSTRACT: Hymeglusin (1) is a fungal polyketide consisting of a β-lactone ring with a unique (3R,4R) configuration. 1 is a potent
3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA (HMG-CoA) synthase inhibitor. Because it circumvents drug resistance in methicillin-resistant
Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), combination therapy using 1 would be a promising strategy for the treatment of infectious diseases
caused by MRSA. Despite its pharmaceutical importance, the biosynthesis of 1, with respect to the formation of the characteristic β-
lactone ring that is essential for its activity, had not been elucidated to date. During our genome mining of fungal highly reducing
polyketide synthases (HR-PKSs), we identified a biosynthetic gene cluster for 1 in Nigrospora fungi. Heterologous expression and
biochemical analysis of recombinant HR-PKS revealed that the ketosynthase (KS) domain of HR-PKS catalyzes the β-lactonization
of the mature polyketide chain in the termination step. This study unveiled the previously unknown programming of HR-PKS
catalysis and added one unique example to the noncanonical function of KS domains in type I PKS systems.
β-Lactones are strained four-membered lactones with high
chemical reactivity. Due to their characteristic properties, β-
lactones are considered privileged synthetic fragments and
pharmacophores. In natural products, more than 30 β-lactone-
containing molecules have been isolated from microorganisms,
plants, and animals.1 Representative examples include
salinosporamide A,2 an irreversible proteasome inhibitor, and
lipstatin,3,4 an inhibitor of human pancreatic lipase (Figure 1).
More recently, alligamycin A, an antifungal β-lactone-
containing macrolide, was identified by the genome mining
approach.5 β-Lactone moieties are indispensable for their
biological activities, clearly demonstrating their significance in
pharmaceutical development. However, the biosynthesis of β-
lactones has not been understood until recently. After the first
report of enzymatic β-lactone formation in the biosynthesis of
long-chain olefinic hydrocarbons, only four examples of β-
lactone biosynthesis have been experimentally verified as
enzymatic steps.6−10
Among the natural β-lactones, hymeglusin (1, also known as
1233A, F-244, or L-659,699) was one of the three compounds
discovered before 1975 and has a distinct trans-β-lactone of
which the configuration is (3R,4R) (Figure 1). Compound 1
was originally isolated by Aldridge et al. in 197111 and
rediscovered as a potent inhibitor of 3-hydroxy-3-methylglu-
taryl-CoA (HMG-CoA) synthase, a key enzyme in sterol
biosynthesis.12−14 Recent studies have shown that 1 circum-
vents drug resistance in methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus
aureus (MRSA). The asymmetric total synthesis of 1 and its
unsaturated congener fusarilactone A and subsequent
structure−activity relationship studies revealed that the β-
lactone ring with the hydroxymethyl group and aliphatic side
chain is important for the biological activity of 1.15 Isotope
labeling experiments suggested polyketide-derived β-lactone
formation in the biosynthesis of 1.16,17 In 2020, the
biosynthetic gene cluster (BGC) of 1 was identified in the

the mechanism of β-lactonization remained unknown due to

Fungal genomes contain a large number of biosynthetic
genes for cryptic natural products, making fungi attractive
targets for genome mining.19 We have been working on the
genome mining of HR-PKSs in fungal strains20−22 and have
identified several unique chain-releasing machineries, such as
trans-acting thioesterases (TEs) that form macrolides,23
acyltransferase-like hydrolase,24 and type III PKS synthesizing
alkylresorcinol.25 In the present study, we identified the BGC
of 1 and revealed that the HR-PKS NghhA is solely responsible
for the construction of the core scaffold of 1, including the
characteristic β-lactone ring. Notably, NghhA consists of
catalytic domains that are typical for fungal HR-PKSs and does
not have the specialized catalytic domain for chain releasing
such as TE. Although ketoreductase (KR)-domain-catalyzed
reductive cleavage of the polyketide (PK) chain was known in
the biosynthesis of fungal alkyl salicylaldehyde,26 the
involvement of the other canonical HR-PKS domains in
chain release has not been identified to date. Our biochemical
analysis unequivocally demonstrated that the intrinsic KS
domain of HR-PKS catalyzes the β-lactonization of the mature
PK chain, showing a new paradigm for the programming of
complex HR-PKS catalysis.
First, we explored HR-PKSs in our in-house genome
database, focusing on the off-loading mechanism, and
identified one BGC in the genome of the fungal endophyte
Nigrospora sp. TCN-5. The identified HR-PKS gene, nghhA,
was clustered with genes encoding HMG-CoA synthase
(HCS) (nghhB) and cytochrome P450 (nghhC) (Figure 2A
and Table S1). Because known chain-releasing enzymes were
not encoded in this BGC, we expected that the unprecedented
off-loading mechanism of PKs could be involved. This BGC
was similar to that for the biosynthesis of 1,18 suggesting that it
exerts a similar function. To identify the metabolites derived
from the nghh cluster, we conducted heterologous expression
using Aspergillus oryzae as a host strain. First, we constructed
the transformant AO-nghhABC, which harbors three genes:
nghhA, nghhB, and nghhC (Tables S2−S4). This transformant
produced compound 1 (Figure 2B). The spectral data of 1
were in agreement with those in the literature (Table S5). AO-
nghhAC also produced 1, showing that nghhB is not involved in
the biosynthesis (Figure 2B). It has been suggested that the
HCS homologue g431 is responsible for self-resistance of the
producing strain based on the observation that the knockout
mutant exhibited increased sensitivity to 1.18
Next, we focused on the formation of a characteristic β-
lactone ring. Through HPLC analysis of the mycelial extract of
transformant AO-nghhA, we identified 2 as a product (Figure
2C). Structural elucidation by HRMS and NMR analyses
revealed that 2 is a deoxy derivative of 1 (Figure 2D and Table
S6). This result indicated that the HR-PKS NghhA is solely
responsible for the formation of the β-lactone ring. We then
constructed the transformant AO-nghhC and conducted a
feeding experiment of 2. LC-MS analysis of the culture broth
revealed the formation of 1 (Figure S1), showing that 2 is a
biosynthetic intermediate. Based on these observations and the
previous report, we propose the biosynthetic pathway of 1 as
shown in Figure 2D: the HR-PKS enzyme NghhA synthesizes
2 (prehymeglusin), followed by NghhC-mediated oxidations
to yield 1.
Considering the strained nature of the β-lactone ring, we
assumed that non-enzymatic lactonization is unlikely. To gain
insight into this enigmatic reaction, we expressed C-terminal
His-tagged NghhA (NghhA-His6) in A. oryzae. GC-MS
analysis of the transformant revealed that it produced 2,
showing that NghhA-His6 retained the same activity as native
NghhA (Figures 3A and S2). We then purified NghhA-His6
(Figure S3) and incubated the enzyme in the presence of
malonyl-CoA, S-adenosyl-L-methionine (SAM), and β-
NADPH. GC-MS analysis revealed the clear formation of 2,
demonstrating that NghhA is solely responsible for the
formation of 2 and catalyzes the β-lactonization of the mature
polyketide chain (Figure 3A).
We subsequently focused on the mechanism of β-
lactonization. Compound 2 was chemically transformed to
N-acetylcysteamine (SNAC) derivative 3, which is a mimic of a
mature PK chain tethered to the acyl carrier protein (ACP)
(Figure 3B). Incubation of 3 with NghhA-His6 resulted in the
formation of 2 (Figure 3A). Note that non-enzymatic
lactonization of 3 was also observed during GC-MS analysis,
likely due to heating in the ion source of the GC (Figure S4A).
Therefore, the extract was pretreated by silica gel chromatog-
raphy to remove the remaining 3 from the mixture. In addition,

we confirmed that 3 was not converted to 2 in the reaction
buffers at pH 7.0 or below, although trace conversion was
observed in the buffer at pH 9.0 (Figure S4B). Non-enzymatic
conversion of 3 at pH 7.5, which was used for the following
enzymatic assays, was not observed. Because no specific
domains other than canonical ones of PKS were detected in
NghhA, we assumed that one of them is responsible for this
process. Among the canonical domains, we hypothesized that
KS is involved in this process for the following reasons. After a
cycle of chain elongation, the acyl chain is transferred to the
catalytic cysteine residue of the KS domain to form an acyl−
KS complex. While this acyl−KS complex usually undergoes
Claisen-type condensation during chain elongation, this
species is also a plausible precursor of β-lactonization when
the PK chain contains a β-hydroxy group.
To test this hypothesis, we expressed the KS domain in
Escherichia coli (Figure S3). Although the expressed protein
was mostly insoluble, the cell-free extract of recombinant E. coli
showed β-lactonization activity (Figure 3C), indicating that the
KS domain exerted the expected function. To obtain insights
into the catalytic mechanism of KS-mediated β-lactonization,
we constructed a structural model of the acyl−KS complex by
using AlphaFold2. In the obtained model, the β-hydroxy group
interacts with the catalytic His residue (Figures 3D and S5).
Given this docking model, we speculated that the catalytic His
residue increases the nucleophilicity of the β-hydroxy group.
Although the KS domain was insoluble or unstable when
expressed as a truncated single domain, the KS−AT didomain
was successfully obtained as a soluble protein (Figure S3).
Given that the purified protein exhibited the same β-
lactonization activity (Figure 3C), we introduced site-directed
mutagenesis into the catalytic Cys and His residues of the KS
domain. All of the mutations abolished the enzymatic activity,
thus confirming the involvement of these residues (Figure 3C).
Conversely, a mutant having the mutation in the AT domain
still showed β-lactonization activity (Figure 3C). We also
examined the β-lactonization of KS domains of PemlA and
MpmlA, which are HR-PKSs involved in the biosynthesis of
fungal macrolides.27,28 Both KSs did not catalyze β-
lactonization (Figure S6), suggesting that this function is
specific to those involved in β-lactone biosynthesis. While the
KS domains of NghhA and closely related homologues fell into
a distinct clade in the phylogenetic analysis (Figure S7), the
putative binding site residues were highly conserved in other
KSs (Figure S8), and further analysis is required to identify the
amino residues related to the β-lactonization activity.
We herein identified the BGC for 1 and fully elucidated its
biosynthetic pathway. Through the heterologous expression
and biochemical analyses, we identified that the KS domain
catalyzes the β-lactonization and proposed its reaction
mechanism as follows. After six rounds of chain elongation
by the HR-PKS, the mature PK chain tethered to ACP is again
transferred to the catalytic Cys residue of the KS domain
(Scheme 1A). Note that the mature PK chain contains a β-
hydroxy group, which is requisite for β-lactonization, while the
acyl-ACP intermediate generated after each chain elongation
step does not. In the active site of the KS domain, the catalytic
His residue interacts with β-hydroxy group of the PK chain and
increases its nucleophilicity to enable the β-lactonization.
Consequently, 2 is released from the HR-PKS as a product
(Scheme 1A). While the standalone KS-like enzyme SalC
catalyzes β-lactone formation in salinosporamide biosynthesis,8
the inherent KS domain of the HR-PKS exerting such a
function is unprecedented (Scheme 1B). In addition, the
identified pathway is clearly different from other β-lactone
biosyntheses, such as TE-domain-mediated transesterification
in obafluorin biosynthesis,7 ATP-dependent ligation by OleC,6
hydrolase-mediated reaction in vibralactone biosynthesis,9 and
esterase-mediated β-lactone formation in globilactone biosyn-
thesis10 (Scheme 1B).
HR-PKSs iteratively use a single set of catalytic domains
with different combinations in each catalytic cycle.29 Although
the highly programmed catalytic cycle of HR-PKSs has been
studied, predicting the exact function of each catalytic domain
from their amino acid sequences remains difficult, necessitating
experimental verification. Our finding in the biosynthesis of 1
suggested that the KS domain of NghhA recognizes the chain
length or modification pattern of the substrate, playing a
crucial role in PK chain release. KS-domain-dependent product
release would be a new example of HR-PKS programming that
determines the outcome of the catalytic cycle and might be
involved in other HR-PKS systems that do not possess
specialized domains for product release.30 Our findings also

add one unique example of noncanonical functions of KS
domains embedded in type I PKS systems.31 Similar to the KS
domain of NghhA, the terminal KS domain of the type I PKS
EbeG likely catalyzes the β-lactonization in the ebelactone A
biosynthesis because other domains or enzymes such as TE
were not found in the PKSs and the BGC.32 Although non-
enzymatic cyclization was discussed in that case, further
biochemical analysis of this issue would be worth investigating,
as Liu and Tang et al. also pointed out previously.31
In summary, we identified an unprecedented biosynthetic
strategy for β-lactone formation, which is catalyzed by the KS
domain of the fungal HR-PKS system. Although sequence-
based discrimination of such KSs from canonical ones is
difficult at the current stage, further studies including structural
analysis will reveal sequence motif or structural features
specific to these KSs, enabling the further exploration of related
natural products.
