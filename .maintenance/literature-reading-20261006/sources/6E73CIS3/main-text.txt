ABSTRACT: Nonribosomal peptides serve as pivotal sources for drug discovery. Accurate prediction of the substrate specificity of
adenylation domains in nonribosomal peptide synthetases is crucial for genome mining of nonribosomal peptides, yet current
prediction methods fall short in accuracy. In this work, we analyzed 4,100 adenylation domains from documented nonribosomal
peptide synthetases and found that the flavodoxin-like subdomain universally governs substrate specificity in all bacterial adenylation
domains and that its phylogenetic analysis can correlate the sequences of adenylation domains and their substrate specificity.
Leveraging the sequences within the flavodoxin-like subdomain, we developed a substrate specificity prediction algorithm using a
protein language model, achieving 92% overall prediction accuracy for 43 frequently observed amino acids, significantly improving
the prediction reliability. The efficacy of our prediction tool was validated through targeted genome mining, which led to the
discovery of novel antimicrobial peptides. Our work lays a foundation to understand the sequence-to-function relationship of the
bacterial adenylation domain and will facilitate the exploitation of nonribosomal peptides. NRPStransformer is available at http://
www.nrpstransformer.cn.
1. INTRODUCTION
The rapid emergence and spread of antimicrobial resistance
pose a significant threat to global public health, making the
discovery of new antimicrobial compounds an urgent prior-
ity.1−3 The discovery and development of antibiotics have been
largely driven by natural products, and nonribosomal peptides
(NRPs) constitute a pivotal source for clinical antibiotics
exemplified by penicillin, vancomycin, and colistin, a last-resort
treatment against multidrug-resistant bacterial infections.4
Recent discoveries of teixobactin5 and clovibactin6 with a
unique mode of action further highlight the privileged
molecular property of nonribosomal peptides.
Genome mining is a powerful approach for the targeted
discovery of natural products, which aims to discover natural
products based on the analysis of their biosynthetic gene
clusters.7−11 The discoveries of teixobactin and clovibactin
both benefitted from genomic analysis of nonribosomal
peptide synthases (NRPSs) that are responsible for NRP
biosynthesis.12,13 As NRPSs consist of multiple domains and
have large protein size, they are easily identifiable in genome
sequences; metagenomic and pangenomic analyses have
revealed the widespread distribution of NRPSs among most
phyla of microbes in soil, ocean, and even the human
microbiome, leading to the continuous discovery of new
NRPs with potent antibiotic activity.14−19
The structure of an NRP is primarily controlled by NRPS’s
adenylation (A) domain that selectively activates an amino
acid as a substrate for peptide formation.13,20,21 Thus,
prediction of the substrate specificity of A domains is of
critical importance in connecting the biosynthetic genes to the
peptide product structures. A pioneering work by Stachelhaus

et al. reported that the 10 amino acid residues forming the

Stachelhaus code, are indicative of its substrate specificity.22
The Stachelhaus code has been widely applied for the substrate
prediction of A domains and has facilitated the development of
machine learning-based prediction tools such as NRPSpre-
dictor2, which expanded the Stachelhaus code to 32 amino
acid residues within 5 Å of the substrate binding pocket.23
Such bioinformatic predictions have even enabled bioinfor-
matics-guided chemical synthesis for the rapid discovery of
bioactive (pseudo)natural peptide antibiotics.24,25 Neverthe-
less, the accuracy and applicable range of the Stachelhaus code
have not been systematically assessed, and the existing tools for
A domain substrate prediction also have limitations in their
accuracy and predictable amino acid classes.26,27
Here, we report a large-scale bioinformatic analysis of 4,100
NRPS A domains and the development of an artificial
intelligence-based A domain substrate predictor, NRPStrans-
former. NRPStransformer achieves a significantly improved
overall accuracy of 93% for 43 frequently observed amino
acids, including a dozen of nonproteinogenic amino acids. We
also demonstrate the discovery of novel bioactive peptides by
targeted genome mining based on the NRPStransformer
prediction. Our results uncover the global landscape of
NRPSs’ A domains and accelerate the exploitation of natural
bioactive peptides.
2. RESULTS
2.1. Comprehensive Characterization of NRPS A
Domains. Although current A domain prediction tools have
been widely applied to NRPS genome mining, they are trained
by a limited data set and may contain sampling biases and
overfitting issues.23,26,27 To better understand the sequence-to-
substrate relationship of A domains, we first curated bacterial
NRPSs whose product peptides are documented from the
MIBiG database and by manual literature search (as of May
2024).28 The curated data set includes a total of 756 NRPS-
containing gene clusters and 4,100 A domains from most of
the major bacterial phyla (Figure 1, Supporting Information set
S1), which brings a 77% increase compared to the previous
largest data set.29 We annotated the substrate information on
each A domain based on the product peptide structure or
biochemical characterization results. For those A domains
whose product peptides have variable amino acid residues, we
annotated the most representative amino acid by considering
the major peptide product structure. If no major product could
be determined, the phylogenetic distribution of the A domain
was considered to guide the annotation, thus giving one-to-one
labeling of the A domain sequences and its substrate amino
acids.
A characteristic feature of nonribosomal peptides is the
incorporation of nonproteinogenic amino acids. They can be
introduced either by the A domain itself or by modification
after loading of a proteinogenic amino acid. For these A
domains whose product corresponds to a nonproteinogenic
amino acid, we annotated their substrate based on the final
product structure, except if they are biochemically charac-
terized or a reasonable assumption can be made based on
bioinformatic analysis (see later results), resulting in a total of
181 amino acid substrates (Supporting Information set S1).
With the curated A domain data set in hand, we first
analyzed the amino acid substrates they accept and their
taxonomic origin. The most abundant amino acid used by A
domains was Leu (353), followed by Thr (352), Val (332),
and Ser (325), whereas His (24) and Met (7) were the least
used proteinogenic amino acids in NRPs (Figure 1). Some
nonproteinogenic amino acids were distributed in specific
phyla, as seen in hydroxyphenylglycine (Hpg) and piperazic
acid (Piz) in Actinomycetota, homoserine (Hse) in Pseudo-
monadota, and homotyrosine (Hty) in Cyanobacteriota,
suggesting their recent emergence in these phyla (Figure 1b).
Next, we conducted a sequence logo analysis for each amino
acid substrate. The result showed that each substrate class has
distinct conserved residues (Figures 2a and S1), and because
each substrate class contains A domains from various
taxonomic origins, these distinctive conservation patterns
strongly indicate their relevance with substrate specificity.
These substrate specificity-conferring residues are mainly
distributed in the flavodoxin-like subdomain region30 between
the A3 and A6 motifs31,32 of an A domain. Notably, the
analysis revealed that the specificity-conferring residues are not
restricted to the residues near the substrate binding pocket. For
example, Cys-accepting A domains share a conserved
WxSIPYG motif at the loop region between the A5 and A6
motifs (Figure 2a), indicating that the substrate binding pocket
may not be the only factor to determine the substrate
specificity. In addition, the sequence logo analyses did not
reveal any substrate-specific conserved residues far from the

2.2. Phylogenetic Analysis of NRPS A Domains. To
further study the sequence-to-substrate correlation of A
domains, we constructed a phylogenetic tree using the
sequence region between the A3 and A6 motifs that covers
the entire flavodoxin-like subdomain. The phylogenetic tree of
the 4,100 A domains formed clear substrate-specific clades for
amino acids such as Ser, Cys, Ala, Gly, Thr, and Pro and clades
with similar molecular properties such as hydrogen bond
forming, basic, and hydrophobic clades (Figures 2b and S2−
S3). Overall, the tree showed a strong correlation between the
phylogenetic clades and the substrate type an A domain
accepts. Within a clade, A domains that accept the same
substrate tend to form subclades according to the taxonomic
classification of their host strains. The mixed clades such as the
hydrophobic clade are also indicative of the inherent
promiscuity of some A domains accepting multiple amino
acids. We note that fungal A domains (Supporting Information
set S2) do not follow this substrate-depending cladogram
(Figure S3), suggesting that their substrate specificity may not
be clearly learned by the sequence analysis of the flavodoxin
subdomain.

mechanism of nonproteinogenic amino acids. For example, we

The phylogenetic tree also illustrates the potential evolu-
tionary history of the A domains. The clear substrate-
dependent cladogram indicates that different amino acid-
accepting A domains emerged at an early point in the evolution
of NRPS prior to taxonomic diversification.34 Generally, it can
be inferred that there were a few key ancestral A domains that
served as templates for each substrate class. For example, the
Asp/Asn clade locates adjacent to the parental clade of large
hydrogen-bond-forming amino acids, suggesting that the Asp/
Asn-accepting A domains evolved from a promiscuous
ancestor that accepts large polar amino acids. From the Asp/
Asn clade, an actinobacterial Asp/Glu clade further branches
out (Figure 2b), suggesting that the actinobacterial Glu-
accepting A domains emerged at a later point from Asp/Asn-
accepting A domains by cavity-enlarging mutations. Non-
proteinogenic amino acid-accepting A domains may have
evolved later, as seen for the (di)hydroxyphenyl-glycine (Dpg/
Hpg) clade branched out in the middle of the Val/Ile clade,
which was specifically distributed in Actinomycetota. However,
we also note that random point mutation is another driving
force for the functional diversification of A domains. The most
prominent example is A domains accepting Ala and Leu, which
are mixed in many other clades (Figure S3), presumably due to
the structural generality of Ala and Leu for partially resembling
other amino acid structures. Detailed analysis of such

Altogether, our bioinformatic analyses suggest that the
flavodoxin-like subdomain has a critical influence on the
substrate specificity of an A domain. It is notable that previous
phylogenetic analyses of A domains using the full-length
sequence did not lead to reliable substrate prediction.26
Indeed, the A domain regions outside the A3 to A6 motif tend
to have high sequence similarity with other A domains from
the same gene cluster, regardless of its substrate specificity.35
Such sequence homogenization, caused by concerted evolu-
tion,36 can override the original evolutionary information and
result in the difficulty for substrate prediction by the full-length
A domain. Even using the A3 to A6 motif region for
phylogenetic analysis, earlier research found it to emphasize
a genus-specific cladogram;34 however, the substrate-depend-
ent trend was indeed observable in their result of 138 A
domain sequences. Our result, leveraging a substantially larger
data set, reveals these substrate-dependent cladograms with
greater clarity.
2.3. NRPStransformer Enables Accurate Substrate
Prediction of A Domains. The observation of substrate-
dependent clades of A domains further prompted us to develop
a prediction tool for the A domain substrates by using
flavodoxin-like subdomain sequences. To this end, we sought
to apply a protein language model to develop an NRPS A
domain substrate prediction tool, NRPStransformer (Figure
3a). First, we prepared the characterized A domains as the
training data set. We selected the top 43 amino acid substrates
that have 9 and more A domains in our data set for substrate
prediction (Table S1). A domains accepting minor substrates
and stand-alone A domains that located as outliers in the
phylogenetic tree were removed, resulting in 3,806 charac-
terized A domains for algorithm development (Supporting
Information set S3).
We designed a classification layer with the 43 amino acid
output heads based on Evolutionary Scale Modeling-2 (ESM-
2), a transformer-based language model pretrained on large-
scale protein data sets.37 Next, the model was trained and fine-
tuned using the data set separated into training and validation
data with a 8:2 ratio by 5-fold cross-validation (Supporting
Information Method). The validation results for the five-folds
showed a consistent prediction accuracy of more than 91%,
with the highest of 92.9% accuracy, indicating stable
performance of our prediction model (Figure 3b). Among
different substrates, NRPStransformer showed 82% (Gln) to
100% (Thr, etc.) accuracy as shown in the confusion matrix
(Figure 3c). Overall, amino acids with relatively low prediction
score also showed a mixed cladogram in the phylogenetic
analysis (e.g., Leu and Trp), suggesting that our AI-based
model can extract biologically meaningful information from the
input sequences.
Hinted by the phylogenetic analysis result, we also designed
the phylogenetic cladogram prediction mode based on the
same ESM-2 model.37 After training and fine-tuning using the
same data set but with a classification layer with the 17
phylogenetic clades, the algorithm achieved 99% prediction
accuracy.
We then benchmarked NRPStransformer with NRPSpre-
dictor2, the current standard prediction algorithm based on
support vector machine (SVM) classification.23 Since
NRPSpredictor2 was trained with 550 A domains back in
2011, we also retrained NRPSpredictor2 using our 43 amino
acid substrate data set of 3,043 training sequences from the last
fold of the cross-validation data set and used the remaining 743
validation A domain sequences for benchmarking. The result
showed that while original NRPSpredictor2 gave 59% correct
prediction, retrained NRPSpredictor2 significantly improved
its prediction accuracy to reach 87% correctness, slightly below
the 92% accuracy of NRPStransformer (Figure 3b).
Interestingly, when we used the Stachelhaus code for
prediction, i.e., comparing the 10-residue code of a test A
domain from the 763 validation A domains against the 3,043
reference data set and outputting the most frequent substrate
from the best match A domains, this simple 10 amino acid
comparison method still gave 89% prediction accuracy. We
note that the Stachelhaus code prediction worked well for
those test samples which found highly identical codes from the
reference data set but performed worse than AI-based
prediction for those without a similar code in reference.
These results indicate that the sufficient coverage of the A
domain training data set and the quality of their substrate
labels affect the prediction accuracy most profoundly, and the
use of a protein language model can further improve the
accuracy over simple key residue comparison and traditional
machine learning models, particularly for those with lower
sequence similarity to references.
Several limitations should be noted. First, the NRPStrans-
former outputs the top three hits based on the prediction
score, but a high prediction score does not always guarantee its
correctness (Figure 3d). Second, NRPStransformer (and other
sequence-based prediction tools in general) cannot accurately
predict A domains that have an altered substrate specificity
caused by point mutations. Therefore, it should not be used to
evaluate enzyme engineering outcomes based on a small
number of mutations.
Lastly, we compared the prediction accuracy of NRPStrans-
former with recently developed algorithms PARAS38 and
DeepAden39 reported as preprints. Using the test data set of
763 sequences, NRPStransformer outperformed the other
algorithms by a margin of 4−6% (Figure 3b). To avoid training
and data set preparation biases, we also collected 155 recently
published A domain sequences (May 2024−June 2025,
including NRPSs characterized in this work, Supporting
Information set S4) to test real-world accuracy of these
tools. NRPStransformer showed a decreased but highest 82.6%
accuracy (Figure 3e), despite the data set containing other
than the 43 predictable amino acids. Notably, DeepAden
integrates contrastive learning of sequence and substrate
structure information on A domains, showing comparable
accuracy to our NRPStransformer but with less training data
set. However, the PARASECT algorithm that integrates
substrate structure information on top of the sequence-based
PARAS algorithm did not show significant improvement,38
highlighting the need for better utilization of substrate
structure information to enhance A domain prediction.

Nonribosomal Peptides. To demonstrate the application of
the NRPStransformer, we performed structure-oriented
genome mining for the targeted discovery of specific
nonribosomal peptides. Polycationic peptides are a promising
class of antibiotics, including colistin and polymyxins, which
are regarded as last-resort antibiotics. By screening NRPSs rich
in cationic residues (Lys, Orn, and Arg), we identified an
NRPS that potentially encodes a novel polycationic peptide

We next sought to obtain difficult-to-access NRPs by
genome mining. Cyanobacterial glycolipopeptides such as
hassallidin and balticidin are potent antimicrobial peptides, but
cultivation of their producer strain requires light and long
duration (∼30 days).40,41 By searching NRPSs with three
consecutive Thr, a common feature for these cyanopeptides,
we found a similar NRPS in Chromobacterium rhizoryzae JP2−
74. Metabolomics screening of its five day culture extract
revealed the production of the peptide Sch 20561 (2), Sch
20562 (3),42,43 its novel derivative Sch 20563 (4), and its
analogs (5−7) whose biosynthetic gene cluster has not been
identified before, with 7/8 correct amino acid predictions
(Figures 4b, S10, and S13−S18, Tables S4−S5). Notably, the
predicted Trp in module 9 was replaced by His in 2 and 3, but
we indeed isolated Trp-containing 4 as expected. Additionally,
by extracting the mass fragment of the decarboxylated
threonine, we detected a series of their derivatives whose
structures can be deduced based on MS/MS analyses (Figure
4b, Figures S13−S18). The glycosyltransferase Sch25 presum-
ably catalyzes the glycosylation on the threonine side chain
(Figure S8). These examples demonstrate that accurate A
domain prediction by the NRPStransformer enables precise
structure-guided genome mining of NRPs.
Lastly, we applied NRPStransformer for genome mining of
our in-house strains. An uncharacterized NRPS was identified
in Streptomyces cattleya NRRL 8057, a well-studied strain for
the production of various natural products.44−47 To obtain the
NRPS product in a higher amount, we performed coculture
with Tsukamurella pulmonis.48 The LC−MS analysis revealed a
series of peptide products, cattleyapeptin A (8) and its
derivatives (9−13), bearing an unusual dehydrogenated
cyclohexenylalanine (CHA)49 as a building block (Figures 4c
and S19−S24, Table S6). The biosynthesis of CHA is likely
catalyzed by chorismite-modifying enzymes Cat34, Cat15,
Cat14, and others, and the β-OH-Asn and 4-OMe-Trp are
estimated to be biosynthesized by oxygenases and methyl-
transferases in the cat cluster (Figure S9). The first A domain
was predicted as Glu but with a low score of 0.35. Based on the
product structure and because of the lack of an epimerization
domain, this A domain should accept a D-Asp produced by
aspartate racemase. The cyclohexene-containing amino acid in
module 3 was wrongly predicted as Leu by NRPStransformer
with a confidence score of 1.0. However, the NRPStransformer
cannot predict rare amino acids other than the 43 trained ones,
and more reference data are required for it to adjust to these
rare amino acids. Nonetheless, all of these wrong predictions
were “close” answers (i.e., Tyr vs Phe, Ala vs Gly in 1; Trp vs
His in 2; Glu vs Asp, Leu vs 3,4-dehydro-CHA in 8), and our
genome mining results overall illustrate reliable prediction of
amino acid substrates by the NRPStransformer.
3. CONCLUSION
In summary, we reveal a global sequence-to-substrate relation-
ship of A domains and provide the NRPStransformer, a highly
accurate substrate prediction algorithm for NRPS A domains.
Our result illustrates that a phylogenetic cladogram of the
flavodoxin-like subdomain of A domains has a strong
correlation with the substrate amino acid structure and
indicates potential evolutionary paths for A domains with
different substrate specificity. The NRPStransformer achieves
remarkable accuracy, covering various major nonproteinogenic
amino acids. We hope this work will deepen our understanding
of the substrate specificity of A domains and accelerate further
exploitation of bacterial NRPSs.
