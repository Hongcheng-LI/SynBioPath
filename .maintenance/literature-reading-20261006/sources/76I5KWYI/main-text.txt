Cinnamosyn, a Cinnamoylated Synthetic-Bioinformatic Natural
Product with Cytotoxic Activity

ABSTRACT: Most biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) are
functionally inaccessible by using fermentation methods. Bio-
informatic-coupled total synthesis provides an alternative approach
for accessing BGC-encoded bioactivities. To date, synthetic
bioinformatic natural product (synBNP) methods have focused
on lipopeptides containing simple lipids. Here we increase the
bioinformatic and synthetic complexity of the synBNP approach by
targeting BGCs that encode N-cinnamoyl lipids. This led to our
synthesis of cinnamosyn, a 10-mer N-cinnamoyl-containing
peptide that is cytotoxic to human cells.
M
icrobial natural products are a historically important
source of bioactive molecules to fuel therapeutic
discovery programs. In response to both the diminished
discovery of novel bioactive natural products in recent decades
and advances in tools for sequencing and analyzing the
biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) that encode their
production, sequence based natural product discovery methods
are being developed as alternatives to functional screening of
microbial culture extracts.1 Unfortunately, even with the
advent of a multitude of genetic strategies for activating
BGCs, most BGCs remain silent in the laboratory.2 The
creation of increasingly accurate bioinformatic algorithms for
predicting the chemical output of BGCs provides an
opportunity to access the molecules encoded by sequenced
BGCs through total chemical synthesis. We have called the
products of this process synthetic-bioinformatic natural products
(syn-BNPs). Many of our past syn-BNP discovery efforts
centered on synthesizing bioactive lipopeptides containing
simple straight chain lipids.3−7 Here we increase both the
bioinformatic sophistication and the synthetic complexity of
the syn-BNP approach by expanding to natural products with
more complex lipid substituents. A growing number of natural
products with cinnamic acid based lipids of varying complexity
(N-cinnamoyl containing peptides, CCPs) have been found to
have diverse bioactivities (Figure 1a).8−10 With an increasing
number of silent sequenced BGCs predicted to encode CCPs,
we sought to expand the chemical complexity of bioactive syn-
BNPs by focusing on these BGCs. This led to our
identification of the cnn BGC, which we predicted would
encode a previously unidentified CCP. We accessed the
predicted product of the cnn cluster through total chemical
synthesis and found it to be potent and specifically cytotoxic to
mammalian cells. By coupling more detailed bioinformatic
algorithms with more complex synthetic methods, the synBNP

Ortho-substituted cinnamoyl-ACP precursors to CCPs are
derived from a type II polyketide biosynthesis (Figure 1b).
The core polyketide synthase (PKS) catalytic domains first
assemble an acyl polyene chain. An isomerase (ISO) present in
all reported CCP clusters is then thought to catalyze cis−trans
isomerization of the polyene to a conformation suitable for
aromatization, through a predicted 6π-electrocyclic ring
closure and subsequent dehydrogenation.11 Using conserved
ketosynthase alpha (KSα), ketoreductase (KR) and ISO
sequences from characterized CCP BGCs as query sequences,
hundreds of putative CCP-encoding BGCs can be found in
publicly available databases.9,11 From these BGCs we sought to
identify those for which the encoded structure could be (1)
accurately predicted and (2) readily produced by chemical
synthesis (Figure 1c).
We initially used an adenylation (A) domain substrate
specificity analysis to predict the sequence of the NRP encoded
by each potential CCP BGC. In this analysis, the 10 amino
acids that make up the substrate binding pocket of an A-
domain (A-domain code) were extracted from each predicted
CCP cluster and these were compared to a database of known
A-domain codes.12 The closest database code(s) was identified
for each query A-domain, generating a peptide sequence for
each BGC. A maximum of three A domains with codes that
exhibited >3 nonidentical positions to their closest database
code(s) was allowed for a prediction to be considered strong.
Several filtering steps were then imposed on the peptide
sequences to remove duplicate sequences and those corre-
sponding to reported CCPs. BGCs were then manually
inspected for features that would complicate an accurate
total structure prediction. Namely, large numbers of post-A-
domain amino acid tailoring enzymes with unpredictable regio-
and/or stereoselectivity and miscellaneous tailoring enzymes
with often unpredictable function (e.g., cytochrome P450s)
from primary sequence data were investigated. For this study,
we focused on the bioinformatically tractable cnn gene cluster
from Streptomyces sp. col6, which was predicted to encode a
natural product that did not resemble any previously
characterized CCP structures.
The complete structure encoded by the cnn BGC was
predicted through manual analysis of A-domain substrate
specificity, a detailed comparison of cnn cinnamoyl-encoding
genes to those in known CCP clusters and a BlastP analysis of
all predicted cnn genes (Figure 2). Cnn megasynthetases (Cnn
27−29) are expected to produce a decapeptide based on the
presence of 10 complete NRPS modules (Figure 2a). A trans-
acting A-domain (Cnn27) was predicted to belong to M1,
which lacks an A-domain. A domain codes within the cnn
cluster with exact matches to a code from a functionally
characterized A-domain (A1, A2, A3, A4, A8 and A10) were
assigned the corresponding amino acid (allo-Thr1, Phe2, Leu3,
Val4, Pro8 and Leu10, respectively). The four A-domains
without perfect matches (A5, A6, A7, A9) were further analyzed
using the SANDPUMA bioinformatics tool, which combines
several prediction algorithms to determine an A-domain’s
substrate. A5 and A7 gave predictions that were consistent with
the Stachelhaus predictions (Ala and Gly, respectively).
However, in the case of both A6 and A9 the closest Stachelhaus
code predicted a Leu, while SANDPUMA predicted an Orn.
Orn was chosen for both positions based on a manual analysis
of known A domain codes corresponding to Orn and Leu
(Table S2). Orn (and also Lys) encoding A domains contain a
negatively charged residue at positions 238 and 331 to stabilize
the positively charged side chain of their substrate. In contrast,
Leu encoding A domains contain a hydrophobic residue at

The structure of the N-cinnamoyl moiety encoded by the
cnn cluster was predicted by comparison of the biosynthetic
cassette (cnn7-26) expected to encode cinnamoyl-ACP biosyn-
thesis to those of all reported CCPs with available BGC
sequences. Overall, the predicted cinnamoyl cassette in cnn7−
26 most closely resembles that of the atr cluster (encoding
atratumycin) in terms of both gene organization and sequence
homology (Figure 2b). Every predicted cinnamoyl-ACP
encoding gene in the atr cluster has a homologue (60−85%
identity) in the cnn cluster. Based on the similarity of these two
gene cassettes, we predicted that they produce the same or
similar cinnamoyl-ACP structures. The 2-methylcinnamoyl
moiety present in atratumycin was, therefore, chosen for the
predicted metabolite of cnn. This substituent is predicted to be
appended to Thr1 in accordance with reported CCPs. Our
peptide sequence and cinnamoyl analyses yielded compound 1
(Figure 2c), which we term cinnamosyn (N-cinnamoylated
syn-BNP). By analysis of cnn1−44 using antiSMASH
annotations and BlastP analysis using the NCBI and MIBIG
databases, no additional amino acid biosynthetic or tailoring
genes are found in this BGC. Cinnamosyn (1) was synthesized
using standard Fmoc-based solid-phase peptide synthesis on 2-
chlorotrityl resin (Scheme 1). To address the challenge of
constructing the ester linkage between Thr1 and Leu10,
Yamaguchi esterification conditions (BzCl, DIPEA, and
DMAP in DCM) were used. The linear peptide was cleaved
from resin, cyclized in solution, and purified by semi-
preparative RP-HPLC. Final cinnamosyn (1) was characterized
by HRMS and NMR (Figures S2−S7).
Cinnamosyn (1) was screened for antimicrobial activity
against the ESKAPE pathogens and Candida albicans and for
mammalian cytotoxicity against HeLa cells. Although no
antimicrobial activity was observed, 1 exhibited cytotoxicity
with an IC50 value of 7.0 μM against HeLa cells (Table 1).
Cinnamosyn (1) was screened against an additional 13
microbial strains and 9 mammalian cell lines. Weak activity
(MIC of 8 μg/mL) was observed against the outer membrane-
deficient E. coli BAS 849 strain, and IC50 values ranging from 4
to 21 μM were observed against the additional mammalian cell
lines. Compound 1 showed no apparent selectivity for
cancerous or healthy cell lines.
To evaluate the role of the cinnamic acid in the observed
cytotoxicity of cinnamosyn (1) we synthesized a variant in
which the cinnamic acid was replaced with hexanoic acid,
called cinnamosyn-C6 (2). Hexanoic acid represents the
straight chain length of cinnamic acid without its signature
aromatic ring. Against both healthy and mammalian cancer cell
lines 2 showed reduced cytotoxicity compared to cinnamosyn
at a maximum assay concentration of 64 μg/mL, highlighting
the value that increasing synBNP complexity will likely have on
gaining access to a broader collection of bioactivities encoded
by silent BGCs.

uncharacterized BGC which inspired our synthesis of
cinnamosyn (1), a cytotoxic synBNP that requires a cinnamoyl
moiety for its activity. The synBNP approach was founded
with the synthesis of simple linear peptides and lipopeptides
predicted from A-domain analyses of NRPS BGCs.17 We then
increased both the bioinformatic sophistication and synthetic
complexity to include cyclic (lipo)peptides and structures
containing nonproteinogenic amino acids.4,6,7,18−20 Here we
expanded the synBNP approach even further to bioinformati-
cally predict and synthetically incorporate a complex lipid. We
believe these stepwise advances just scratch the surface of the
potential a synBNP approach has for accessing bioactive small
molecules from the rapidly growing collection of silent BGCs
that are being uncovered in large scale sequencing efforts. As
predictive algorithms improve, coupling with advanced total
synthesis efforts will likewise improve the structural complexity
of the encoded products that can be accessed by using a
synBNP approach.
