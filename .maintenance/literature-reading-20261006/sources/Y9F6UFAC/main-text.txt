Dilarmycins A−C, Calcium-Dependent Lipopeptide Antibiotics with
a Non-canonical Ca2+-Binding Motif

ABSTRACT: Genome analysis of strain Streptomyces sp. CA-
278952 revealed a biosynthetic gene cluster encoding a putative
lipopeptide with a sequence containing an Asp-Gly-Glu-Ala motif.
We envisioned that this motif could mimic the canonical Asp-X-
Asp-Gly sequence found in previously reported calcium-dependent
lipopeptide antibiotics. Chemical investigation of the producing
strain led to the discovery of three novel lipodepsipeptides,
dilarmycins A−C. The calcium-dependent antibacterial activity of
the new compounds was confirmed against the Gram-positive
pathogens methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus and vanco-
mycin-resistant Enterococcus.
C
alcium-dependent antibiotics (CDAs) represent a prom-
ising reservoir of potentially innovative drugs capable of
combating the growing threat of nosocomial antibiotic-
resistant pathogens. In the era often referred to as the golden
age of antibiotic discovery (1940s−1960s), numerous potent
and structurally unique antibiotics were introduced to clinical
use.1 However, the subsequent decades (1970s−2000s) are
now recognized as the “discovery void”, during which only two
novel classes of antibiotics distinct in both structure and
mechanism were discovered. Notably, the CDA daptomycin
stands out as one of the few pioneering antibiotics to enter the
market since the year 2000.2,3 Among non-ribosomal lip-
opeptides, CDAs are a growing family of N-acylated, cyclic
peptides that require calcium to exert their antibacterial
activity. They typically contain a 9 or 10 amino acid
macrocycle and can be classified into two different groups:
compounds of group I are cyclized through an ester bond (i.e.,
depsipeptides; e.g., daptomycin,4,5 taromycin,6,7 A54145,8,9
and CDA10,11), and compounds of group II cyclize via an
amide bond (e.g., laspartomycin,10,12 friulimicin,13−15 tsush-
imycin,16 and amphomycin17). Most of the already known
CDAs have been isolated from soil bacteria and harbor a
conserved Asp-X-Asp-Gly (DXDG) structural motif in their
cyclic peptide core, which is known to promote calcium
binding. Nonetheless, and despite their common structural
features, the mechanisms by which different CDAs target
bacteria can vary significantly.18−21 Recently, two families of
CDAs containing alternative Ca2+-binding motifs, malacidins22
(group II, DDG motif) and cadasides23 (group I, DDXGEE
motif), have been discovered from soil metagenome libraries.
Genome mining efforts have additionally revealed that many
other putative CDAs remain uncharacterized.22 Herein, we
report the discovery of a new family of CDAs with a previously
undescribed alternative calcium-binding motif, which were
isolated employing a combination of genome mining and
OSMAC (One strain, Many Compounds)24 approaches from a
soil-derived Streptomyces strain. During our continuous efforts
on drug discovery from microbial sources, we identified
Streptomyces sp. CA-278952 as a producer of globomycin,
allowing us to pin down its hitherto unknown biosynthetic
gene cluster (BGC).25 Close examination of the remaining
BGCs from the genome of this strain led to the discovery of an
unknown cluster (dil BGC) putatively encoding the biosyn-
thesis of a lipopeptide, which shared some similarity to the
cluster responsible for viennamycin biosynthesis26 (Figure S29
of the Supporting Information). Viennamycins are cysteic/
aspartic-acid-containing lipodepsipeptides with a 7 amino acid
macrocycle, which lack the calcium-binding motif and have no
antibacterial activity.

showed levels of protein identity with the corresponding
homologous proteins in the viennamycin BGC of over 96%
(Table S4 of the Supporting Information), with the only

Analysis of the NRPS proteins DilJ, DilK, DilL, and DilM
indicated that the adenylation domains from modules 1−9 and
12−14 share the same Stachelhaus code as those of modules
1−12 from vienamycin NRPS proteins (Table S5 of the
Supporting Information).26 Substrate specificity analysis for
the adenylation domains of modules 10 and 11, absent in the
viennamycin NRPS system, suggested the incorporation of Asp
and Gly, respectively. Taken together, these data allowed us to
propose the peptide backbone to be Asn1-Ile2-D-Phe3-D-Cya4-
Asp5-Thr6-D-NMeVal7-Gly8-Gly9-Asp10-Gly11-D-Glu12-Ala13-
Pro14. This predicted backbone contains the motif Asp10-Gly11-
D-Glu12-Ala13, which we envisioned could mimic the canonical
Ca2+-binding Asp-X-Asp-Gly pattern found in most calcium-
dependent antibiotics (Figure 1).
To confirm this hypothesis, we set out to produce, isolate,
and characterize the encoded peptides by applying an OSMAC
approach. The producing strain was cultured in 16 different
media, and the corresponding acetone extracts were analyzed
by liquid chromatography−high-resolution electrospray ioniza-
tion mass spectrometry [LC-(+)-HRESIMS]. Among other
conditions, the chromatogram of the FRM medium (section
2.1 of the Supporting Information) culture extract showed
three significant peaks with [M + H]+ ions at m/z 1588.6811,
1602.6965, and 1552.7136 (Figures S1−S4 of the Supporting
Information). Searches in different natural product databases
failed to identify known metabolites with the observed exact
masses, suggesting that they were new molecules. In addition, a
key in-source fragment ion at m/z 151.1114 detected for two
of the compounds, also observed in the MS/MS spectra of
viennamycins, advanced the presence of an unsaturated fatty
acyl chain in their structures. Moreover, a second fragment ion
at m/z 265.1541, which differs from the previous fragment ion
in an Asn residue, strongly suggested their lipopeptide nature
(Figures S9−S14 of the Supporting Information).
Subsequently, the strain CA-278952 was cultured in 250 mL,
and dilarmycins A−C (1−3) were isolated (Figure 2)
following the procedures outlined in the Experimental
Procedures of the Supporting Information, yielding 1.7, 1.0,
and 0.7 mg of compounds 1−3, respectively.
Dilarmycin A (1) was obtained as a white, amorphous solid.

A detailed inspection of the correlation spectroscopy
(COSY), total correlation spectroscopy (TOCSY), and
heteronuclear multiple-bond correlation (HMBC) spectra
identified Ala, Asn, Asp, Gly, Glu, Ile, Phe, Pro, N-Me-Val,
and Thr residues in compound 1. In addition, the presence in
the HSQC spectrum of a methylene group (δH 2.76 and 2.81/
δC 52.3) coupled to an α-proton of amino acid (δH 4.41/δC
51.5) in the COSY spectrum strongly suggested the presence
of a cysteic acid (Cya) residue in the structure.26,28 This
identification was further supported by tandem mass
spectrometry (Figures S9−S14 of the Supporting Information)
and molecular formula requirements. For the Pro-14 residue
and other amino acids surrounding it, a double set of signals
could be observed in the HSQC spectrum, which indicated the
existence of two different conformations in an approximate 1:1
ratio (Figures S15 and S16 of the Supporting Information).
Both conformer signals were considered for the structural
elucidation of the compounds (Tables S1−S3 of the
Supporting Information). The peptide sequence was deter-
mined by the analysis of NH−Hα nuclear Overhauser effect
spectroscopy (NOESY) correlations and HMBC cross-peaks
between NH and Hα to CO (Figures S35−S37 of the
Supporting Information), resulting in the cyclic nonapeptide
core of compound 1.
Although the HMBC spectrum of compound 1 lacked a
correlation between Hβ and the carbonyl group of the amide
bond of the Thr-6 and Pro-14 residues, the depsipeptide ring
closure between these residues could be established on the
basis of the NOESY cross-peak between their Hβ signals
(Figure S17 of the Supporting Information). This assignment
was further supported by the low-field chemical shift of Hβ of
Thr-6 (δH 5.19/5.03 ppm in conformers A/B, respectively).
In addition, the fatty acid chain was determined as (2Z,4E)-
8-methylnona-2,4-dienoic acid (MNDA) from the COSY and
HMBCs. The Z and E configurations of the Δ2 and Δ4 double
bonds of the fatty acyl chain were established on the basis of
the JH,H coupling constants, with J2,3 and J4,5 values of 11.3 and
15.2 Hz, respectively. The link between the lipid chain and the
peptide backbone was confirmed by the HMBC of the NH
proton (δH = 8.23 ppm) from the Asn1 residue with the
carbonyl group of MNDA (δC = 166.0 ppm). The absolute
configuration of amino acids in compound 1 were determined
by advanced Marfey’s analysis as L-Asp (Asn-1, Asp-5, and Asp-
10), D-Phe, D-Cya, NMe-D-Val, D-Glu, L-Ala, and L-Pro; these
results are in agreement with the stereochemistry predicted
from the bioinformatic analysis, where epimerization domains
are found in the modules corresponding to the incorporation
of Phe, Cya/Asp, N-Me-Val, and Glu. The Cα configurations
for Ile-2 and Thr-6 were determined to be S by Marfey’s
analysis, while those of their Cβ were deduced by a comparison
of the HSQC spectrum of hydrolysate of compound 1 to those
of Ile, allo-Ile, Thr, and allo-Thr standard amino acids (Figures
S18−S21 of the Supporting Information).29,30
Dilarmycin B (2) was assigned the molecular formula
C70H103N15O26S based on (+)-ESI−TOF data that displayed
the adducts [M + 2H]2+ (calculated for C70H105N15O26S2+,
801.8532; found, 801.8524) and [M + H]+ (calculated for
C70H104N15O26S+, 1602.6992; found, 1602.6965). In compar-
ison to compound 1, compound 2 accounted for an extra
methylene group determined by tandem mass spectrometry
(MS/MS) fragmentation to be in the fatty acid chain (m/z
165.1270; Figure S12 of the Supporting Information). The
HSQC NMR data of the peptide backbones of compounds 1
and 2 are almost identical. Inspection of the TOCSY and
HMBC spectra of 2 allowed us to establish the correlations
between terminal carbons of the lipidic chain, identifying it as
(2Z,4E)-8-methyldeca-2,4-dienoic acid (MDDA; Figure S22 of
the Supporting Information).

The molecular formula of compound 3 differed from that of
compound 1 by accounting for an extra carbon atom while
lacking sulfur and one oxygen atoms. The (+)-ESI−TOF
spectrum of compound 3 compared to that of compound 1
confirmed that they share the same fatty acid side chain (m/z
151.111), and therefore a different peptide core (Figure S14 of
the Supporting Information). These observations concurred
with the substitution of Cya-4 with Asp-4 in compound 3. The
COSY, NOESY, and HMBC data of the compound confirmed
the aforementioned difference, which was further supported by
the results from MS/MS (Figure S14 of the Supporting
Information). For both compounds 2 and 3, the absolute
configurations of their constituent amino acids were assumed
to be the same or analogous (D-Asp instead
D-Cya in
compound 3) to those of compound 1, as they share a
common biosynthetic origin.31

In summary, the genome analysis of strain Streptomyces sp.
CA-278952 led to the discovery of dilarmycins A−C, three
novel calcium-dependent lipopeptide antibiotics (CDAs). The
new compounds harbor an alternative Asp-Gly-Glu-Ala
(DGEA) calcium-binding motif, akin to the canonical Asp-X-
Asp-Gly (DXDG) sequence found in previously reported
CDAs. The calcium-dependent antibacterial activity of the new
compounds was confirmed against multidrug-resistant Gram-
positive pathogens. These results expand the structural
diversity and knowledge about the relevant CDA family of
specialized metabolites.
Beyond the antibacterial activity, the ecological role of some
CDAs as ion chelators has been postulated on the basis of the
environmental conditions from which they were isolated.23
From an evolutionary perspective, the structural differences
between the characterized CDAs regarding the size of the
macrocycle and the binding motifs might represent adaptative
changes that would be translated into differential ion affinities
or selectivities depending upon the environmental conditions.
Further global systematic studies of this interesting family of
metabolites may shed light on these questions.
