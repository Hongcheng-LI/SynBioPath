on Their Enzymatic Degradation Characteristics for Mycotoxins

ABSTRACT: Mycotoxin contamination poses substantial health risks to humans and animals. In this study, the two laccases
PpLac1 and AoLac2 from Pleurotus pulmonarius and Aspergillus oryzae were selected and heterologously expressed in Pichia pastoris
in a food-grade manner to detoxify aflatoxin B1 (AFB1), zearalenone (ZEN), and deoxynivalenol (DON). Both laccases exhibited
degradation activity toward these three mycotoxins, while the efficiency of these for DON was relatively low. Therefore, molecular
docking between these laccases and DON was conducted to analyze their potential interaction mechanisms. Furthermore, the
degradation conditions of AFB1 and ZEN by the two laccases were optimized, and the optimal degradation rates for AFB1 and ZEN
by PpLac1 reached 78.51 and 78.90%, while those for AFB1 and ZEN by AoLac2 reached 72.27 and 80.60%, respectively. The
laccases PpLac1 and AoLac2 successfully transformed AFB1 and ZEN into the compounds AFQ1 and 15-OH-ZEN, which were 90
and 98% less toxic than the original compounds, respectively. Moreover, the culture supernatants demonstrated effective mycotoxin
degradation results for AFB1 and ZEN in contaminated feed samples. The residual levels of AFB1 and ZEN in all samples ranged
from 6.61 to 8.72 μg/kg and 3.44 to 98.15 μg/kg, respectively, and these levels were below the limit set by the European Union
standards. All of the results in this study indicated that the two laccases have excellent application potential in the feed industry.
KEYWORDS: mycotoxins, degradation, laccases, Pichia pastoris, molecular docking
■INTRODUCTION
Food and feed contamination by mycotoxins has been causing
significant issues in recent decades.1,2 According to the Food
and Agriculture Organization (FAO) of the United Nations,
more than a quarter of the world’s crops and agricultural
products are contaminated with mycotoxins,3 in which
aflatoxin B1 (AFB1), zearalenone (ZEN), and deoxynivalenol
(DON) are the most prevalent ones.4 These mycotoxins are
associated with harmful effects, including neurotoxicity,
immunotoxicity, genotoxicity, extrahepatic toxicity, and hep-
atotoxicity.5−8 The International Agency for Research on
Cancer (IARC) has classified AFB1 as a class I carcinogen,
while ZEN and DON were regarded as class III carcino-
gens.9,10 Therefore, the development of an efficient mycotoxin
detoxification technology is urgently needed.
Conventional detoxification methods for mycotoxins involve
physical, chemical, and microbial approaches.11,12 However,
these methods possess drawbacks such as nutrient loss, reagent
residues, and secondary pollution to the environment.13,14 In
contrast, enzymatic degradation offers advantages, including
mild reaction conditions, high efficiency, low cost, and
convenient operations.15,16 Fungal laccases (EC 1.10.32)
have attracted considerable attention due to their environ-
mentally friendly characteristics and catalytic properties, such
as high oxidation−reduction potentials, wide temperature
ranges, and maximum activity under acidic conditions.17,18
These characteristics make them suitable for use in food and
feed environments.18 To date, several studies have reported the
efficiency of laccases from fungi and bacteria in degrading
AFB1 and ZEN. Zhao et al. reported that Bacillus licheniformis
laccase CotA was able to degrade 39% of the AFB1 and 70% of
the ZEN.19,20 Loi et al. reported that Pleurotus pulmonarius
laccase Lac2 and Pleurotus eryngii laccase Ery4 were able to
degrade 86% of the AFB1 and 100% of the ZEN.21 Thus, it is
of great interest to explore more laccases for their application
in the detoxification of mycotoxins in the food and feed
industries.
Currently, Aspergillus niger and Aspergillus oryzae are usually
the expression hosts to produce laccases in the industry, while
productivity of laccases in these species was relatively low.22,23
Compared to Aspergillus species, Pichia pastoris has a stronger
protein secretion capability.24 Usually, with high-density
fermentation, P. pastoris could produce considerable pro-
teins.25,26 For example, by using the high-density fermentation
method, the yield of human serum albumin reached 8.86 g/L
and the recombinant laccase CueO reached 41,000 U/L in P.
pastoris.27,28 Therefore, in our study, two fungal laccases,
namely, PpLac1 (GenBank: KAF4592641.1) and AoLac2
(GenBank: 6H5Y), were selected and expressed in P. pastoris
GS115 in a food-grade manner. These two laccases were
assayed for their degradation capacity toward AFB1, ZEN, and
DON. Molecular docking was conducted to analyze the
potential interaction mechanisms between laccases and DON.

■MATERIALS AND METHODS
Plasmids, Strains, and Materials. The amino acid sequences of
laccases PpLac1 from P. pulmonarius and AoLac2 from A. oryzae29 are
shown in Table S1. Genes Pplac1 and Aolac2 encoding two laccases
were synthesized in GenScript (Nanjing, China) and stored in
Escherichia coli Top 10 hosts. P. pastoris GS115 and plasmid pPIC9K
were kept in our laboratory. Recombinant strains P. pastoris
GS115(pPIC9K-Pplac1) and P. pastoris GS115(pPIC9K-Aolac2)
were constructed in this work.
The growth and induction media used were BMGY and BMMY
(Table S2). All reagents used in the above culture medium were
purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent (Shanghai, China).
The mycotoxins AFB1, ZEN, and DON were purchased from Pribolab
(Shandong, China). The restriction endonucleases SacI and the
plasmid extraction kit were purchased from Beyotime (Shanghai,
China). The chemicals ABTS (2,2′-azino-bis-3-ethylbenzothiazoline-
6-sulfonic acid), HBT (1-hydroxybenzotriazole), TEMPO (2,2,6,6-
tetramethyl-1-piperidinyloxy), guaiacol, and syringaldehyde were
purchased from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). Acetonitrile,
methanol, and others were analytically pure.
Construction and Recombinant Expression of the Laccases
PpLac1 and AoLac2 in Yeast. The genes and amino acid sequences
of PpLac1 and AoLac2 were analyzed by using the NCBI database.
The recombinant plasmids pPIC9K-Pplac1 and pPIC9K-Aolac2 were
extracted and successfully confirmed by double enzyme digestion
(Figure S1). Two recombinant plasmids were linearized using the
SacI enzyme, followed by transformation into P. pastoris GS115
through electrotransformation to generate the recombinant yeast
strains GS115(pPIC9K-Pplac1) and GS115(pPIC9K-Aolac2). The
specific construction procedures for recombinant strains are shown in
Figure 1.
The positive recombinant strains GS115(pPIC9K-Pplac1) and
GS115(pPIC9K-Aolac2) were cultivated in the BMGY medium at 28
°C and 200 rpm for 24 h. Then, cells were harvested and cultivated in
BMMY containing 0.75 mmol/L CuSO4 at 28 °C and 200 rpm. The
inductive expression cultures were inducted by the addition of 1.0%
methanol for a duration of 96 h. The proteins in the BMMY medium
culture supernatants were collected by centrifugation at 16113g for 10
min and verified by sodium dodecyl-sulfate polyacrylamide gel
electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) (SDS-PAGE) analysis.
Enzymatic Activity Assays of PpLac1 and AoLac2. Enzymatic
activity of laccases was evaluated using the ABTS method.30 The
enzyme reaction volume contained 100.0 mmol/L citrate buffer (pH
3.5), 1.0 mmol/L ABTS, and 20.0 μg/mL enzymes. The reaction was
performed at 37 °C for 10 min and then terminated by an ice bath for
1 min. The optical absorbance of the reaction mixture was measured
at 420 nm with an ultraviolet spectrophotometer. One unit (U) of
enzymatic activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to
oxidize 1 μmol of ABTS per minute at 37 °C, pH 3.5. The specific
relationship between absorbance and enzymatic activity is shown in

Analysis of Enzymatic Properties of PpLac1 and AoLac2.
Enzymatic properties such as optimal reaction temperature, thermal
stability, optimal reaction pH, pH stability, influences of metal ions,
and enzyme kinetics were investigated in this work. The enzymatic
properties of laccases PpLac1 and AoLac2 were determined under
different conditions (temperature: 25−75 °C; pH: 1.0−6.0; metal
ions: Na+, K+, Cu2+, Mg2+, Zn2+, Ca2+, Mn2+, Co2+, and Fe2+). The
measurement methods of kinetic parameters Km and Vmax were based
on a previous study.31 The maximum enzymatic activity was set at
100%.
Degradation and Detection of Mycotoxins. Mycotoxin
degradation tests were performed using the supernatants obtained
from recombinant strains GS115(pPIC9K-Pplac1) and GS115-
(pPIC9K-Aolac2). The reaction system contained 100.0 mmol/L
citrate buffer (pH 3.5) with 1.0 mmol/L ABTS, 20.0 μg/mL culture
supernatants, and 2.0 μg/mL mycotoxins (AFB1, ZEN or DON). The
reaction system was incubated at 37 °C for 12 h, and then, an equal
volume of acetonitrile was added to terminate the reaction.
Subsequently, the reaction system was filtered through a 0.22 μm
filter and the residual content of mycotoxins was detected by an
ultraperformance liquid chromatography-triple quadrupole analyzer
(UPLC-TQD). The residual mycotoxin content detected in the test
samples referred to the amount after subtraction of background
consumption and instrument error. The degradation rate of
mycotoxins can be calculated using Formula 2.

Molecular Docking and Simulation for Laccases and DON.
The structural models of PpLac1 and AoLac2 were constructed by
using the crystal structure of laccase from Cerrena sp. (PDB: CPX-
112101, 1.76 Å) as a template. The DON structure was obtained from
the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). The
three-dimensional (3D) structures of laccases PpLac1 and AoLac2
were predicted by the Swiss-Model (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/).
The docking studies between laccases and DON were carried out
using Autodock Tools 1.5.6. Initially, the laccases and DON were
processed through dehydration and hydrogenation. According to the
molecular docking method of MCO laccase,32 the box size in this
study was set at 126 Å × 126 Å × 126 Å in the x, y, z dimension,
centered at x = 2.08 Å, y = 25.35 Å, z = 22.09 Å. Docking analysis was
performed, and the parameters searched consisted of the Lamarckian
genetic algorithm followed by 50 runs in a semiflexible manner.32,33
The molecular docking model of laccases and DON was displayed by
PyMol 2.5 software.
Optimization of Degradation Parameters for Mycotoxins
by Laccases. In order to study the optimal degradation parameters
for PpLac1 and AoLac2, a series of conditions were further optimized.
Different reaction times from 2 to 12 h were tested according to the
above reaction system. The reaction temperatures for the degradation
of AFB1 and ZEN by PpLac1 and AoLac2 were as follows: 20, 30, 40,
50, and 60 °C. The effects of pH on the degradation of AFB1 and
ZEN by two laccases were assessed with a 100.0 mmol/L buffer (pH
2.0−6.0) at a constant temperature of 37 °C. A series of protein
concentrations were employed to determine the optimal concen-
tration for the reaction, including 10.0, 20.0, 30.0, 40.0, and 50.0 μg/
mL. The present study examined the influence of various mediators
such as ABTS, guaiacol, syringaldehyde, HBT, and TEMPO.
Mediators were added to the buffer to a final concentration of 1.0
mmol/L.
Analysis of Degradation Products for AFB1 and ZEN by
Laccases. Degradation products of AFB1 and ZEN were analyzed by
ultraperformance liquid chromatography-time-of-flight (UPLC-TOF).
The reaction involved 5.0 μg/mL AFB1 or ZEN with the enzyme
solution incubating at 37 °C for 3, 6, 12, and 24 h. The liquid
chromatography detector was WATERS ACQUITY PDA, with a
detection wavelength of 200−400 nm, and the analytical column was
C18 (2.1 mm × 150 mm, 1.7 μm) with a column temperature of 45
°C. Mobile phase A was acetonitrile, and mobile phase B was 0.1%
formic acid. The injection volume was 5.0 μL, and the flow rate was
set at 0.30 mL/min. The ion source temperature was maintained at
100 °C, while the desolvent gas temperature was set to 400 °C.
Degradation Tests of AFB1 and ZEN in Contaminated Feed
Samples. Contaminated feed samples, including cotton meal, corn
steep liquor, and distillers dried grains (DDGS), were separately
weighed at 1.0 g each and placed in a 50 mL centrifuge tube. The
culture supernatants (containing 1.0 mg/mL of protein) and ABTS
(1.0 mmol/L) were added to the samples, and the volume was
adjusted to 5.0 mL with citrate buffer (pH 3.5). The reaction samples
were then incubated at 37 °C for 10 h and extracted with
acetonitrile−water (8:2, v/v) for 20 min. After centrifugation for 5
min, the supernatants were evaporated and then dissolved with
acetonitrile. The samples were filtered through a 0.22 μm organic
filter membrane, and the residual mycotoxin content was analyzed by
UPLC-TQD. The standard curves of AFB1 and ZEN for the
quantitative assay are shown in Figure S3. The detailed operating
procedures for these two mycotoxins are shown in Figure S4.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Selection of Laccases PpLac1 and AoLac2 for
Mycotoxin Degradation. As reported by Silva et al., laccases
from different origins can degrade multiple mycotoxins.34
Previous studies have demonstrated that the laccase Lac2 from
P. pulmonarius had a degradative capacity to mycotoxins.35 In
order to explore new laccases that still have never been
reported for mycotoxin degradation, bioinformatic investiga-
tions were performed through NCBI BLAST. According to the
results of NCBI BLAST analysis, laccase PpLac1 and Lac2 had
the same origin and exhibited 99.4% homology. Thus, we
inferred that the enzyme PpLac1 could also possess the ability
to degrade mycotoxins. Meanwhile, Salas et al. reported that
AoLac2 from A. oryzae exhibited noticeable stability at 65 °C
and pH 3.0, which suggested a promising application potential
for mycotoxin degradation.29
Since the laccases PpLac1 and AoLac2 have not been
reported for mycotoxin degradation, these two enzymes were
selected and heterologously expressed in P. pastoris, which
possessed a good secretion capability, to investigate their
mycotoxin degradation ability.
Inducible Expression and Enzymatic Properties of
PpLac1 and AoLac2. Genes Pplac1 and Aolac2 were cloned
into the pPIC9K vector to construct the recombinant plasmids
pPIC9K-Pplac1 and pPIC9K-Aolac2. These two recombinant
plasmids were then transformed into P. pastoris GS115 to
obtain recombinant strains P. pastoris GS115(pPIC9K-Pplac1)
and P. pastoris GS115(pPIC9K-Aolac2). Furthermore, these
two recombinant strains were induced for 96 h to secrete
laccases PpLac1 and AoLac2. The theoretical molecular masses
of PpLac1 and AoLac2 were 55 and 64 kDa, respectively.29,35
However, the results of SDS-PAGE showed that the molecular
masses of laccases PpLac1 and AoLac2 were greater than the
theoretical size (Figure 2). Although the molecular masses of
these two laccases were relatively greater than the theoretical
size, we could still detect the enzymatic activity using the
ABTS method (Figure S5). According to previous studies, N-
glycosylation of recombinant proteins during secretion usually
happens in the yeast expression system for heterologous
expression, which may increase the molecular mass of the

Furthermore, after fermentation for 96 h, the protein

Previous studies have reported the expression of laccases in
P. pastoris X3335 and Saccharomyces cerevisiae.29 However,
considering the variation in properties resulting from
heterologous expression in different hosts, it was necessary to
explore the enzymatic properties of PpLac1 and AoLac2 when
they were heterologously expressed in GS115.
The results revealed that the optimal temperatures of
PpLac1 and AoLac2 were 40 and 55 °C, respectively.
Meanwhile, both laccases maintained a relative enzymatic
activity of more than 75% within the temperature range of 30−
65 °C (Figure 3A). Furthermore, the thermal stabilities of
these two laccases were also investigated. The relative
enzymatic activity of both laccases remained above 60% at
60 °C, while a significant decline in the activity was observed at
70 °C (Figure 3B).
The results showed that the optimal pH for PpLac1 was 2.0
and that for AoLac2 was 3.0 (Figure 3C). When treated at pH
3.0 for 1 h, the enzyme PpLac1 retained 100% enzymatic
activity, while AoLac2 retained 70% (Figure 3D). Both laccases
have excellent stability under acidic conditions, and PpLac1
demonstrated superior acid resistance compared to AoLac2.

temperatures of the laccases in this work were still lower

The enzymatic activities of PpLac1 and AoLac2 were
determined in the presence of different metal ions. The results
showed that Fe2+ inhibited the activity of both laccases,
whereas the other metal ions had no significant effect on the
enzymatic activity (Table 1). Low concentrations of Mg2+,
Mn2+, Co2+, and Cu2+ promoted the enzymatic activities of
PpLac1 and AoLac2, which were all 1.11 times higher than the
initial values. However, high concentrations of other metal
ions, with the exception of Cu2+, Mg2+, Ca2+, and Mn2+,
reduced the enzymatic activities of these two laccases.
The kinetic parameters of PpLac1 and AoLac2 were
determined at different concentrations of ABTS (Figure 4).
The Km value for PpLac1 was found to be 306.80 μmol/L, with
a Vmax of 5.92 μmol/(L·min). On the other hand, AoLac2
exhibited a lower Km value of 24.76 μmol/L, accompanied by a
higher Vmax of 16.75 μmol/(L·min). These results indicated
that the enzyme AoLac2 possesses a better affinity to the
substrate ABTS compared to that of PpLac1. A comparison of
the enzyme kinetic parameters of the laccases derived from
various reports is listed in Table 2, in which the enzyme
AoLac2 obtained in this work exhibited superior enzymatic
properties.

Optimization of Mycotoxin Degradation Parameters.
In this study, the degradation conditions for AFB1 and ZEN by
recombinant laccases were optimized with the following
factors: reaction time, temperature, pH, protein concentration,
and mediators. The degradation efficiency of the two laccases
in relation to reaction time was examined for AFB1 and ZEN.
Both laccases showed the highest degradation efficiency for
AFB1 within 24 h. PpLac1 demonstrated a degradation rate of
66.03%, while AoLac2 demonstrated a degradation rate of
62.93% (Table S7). The highest degradation efficiency for
ZEN was achieved within 2 h. The degradation rate of PpLac1
reached 70.87%, while that of AoLac2 reached 74.09% (Table
S8). Importantly, the degradation efficiency of ZEN by both
PpLac1 and AoLac2 was higher than that of AFB1.
Moreover, the optimal degradation temperature for
mycotoxins differed between those of PpLac1 and AoLac2.
PpLac1 displayed degradation rates of 66.0% for AFB1 and
70.84% for ZEN at 40 °C (Figure 7A). The degradation
efficiency for two mycotoxins by PpLac1 did not exceed 50%
when the temperature was below 40 °C, while the degradation
efficiency for ZEN reduced to only 20% when the temperature
was 60 °C. In contrast, the degradation efficiency for AFB1 by
PpLac1 remained 61.26% when the temperature was 60 °C
(Figure 7A). Conversely, the degradation efficiency of
mycotoxins by AoLac2 improved as the reaction temperature
increased. AoLac2 exhibited the highest degradation efficiency
of 63.69% for AFB1 and 70.52% for ZEN at 50 °C (Figure 7E).
In addition, among these different pH conditions, the
degradation efficiency of ZEN by PpLac1 and AoLac2 was
found to be higher than that of AFB1. Specifically, the

Analysis of Mycotoxin Degradation Products by
PpLac1 and AoLac2. The mycotoxin transformation path-
ways include hydroxylation, oxidation, hydrogenation, de-
epoxidation, methylation, hydrolysis, and others.42 To further
demonstrate the detoxification mechanisms of PpLac1 and
AoLac2 on AFB1 and ZEN, UPLC-TOF was used to
investigate the degradation products of AFB1 and ZEN after
treatment with the above laccases.
The liquid chromatogram clearly indicated the presence of
an AFB1 degradation product peak at 4.46 min, and the peak
area demonstrated an increase with reaction time (Figure 8A).
Further examination of the degradation reaction solution of
AFB1 by laccase showed that the major fragment ion [M−H]−
exhibited an m/z of 327, which is consistent with the
molecular weight of AFQ1. Therefore, the degradation product
by laccases of AFB1 was AFQ1 (Figure 8B,C). Compared to
the structure of AFB1 (C17H12O6), AFQ1 (C17H12O7) contains
an additional oxygen atom attached to the third carbon atom.
The modification disrupted the cyclopentenone ring of AFB1
by undergoing a 3-α-hydroxylation reaction, which represents
an important detoxification form for AFB1, similar to the
results by Li et al.42 It is important to note that AFQ1 has no
genetic toxicity and is 90% less toxic than AFB1.43
Meanwhile, a peak corresponding to the degradation
product of ZEN appeared at 3.13 min, and its peak area
increased with reaction time (Figure 8D). Analysis of the
degradation reaction solution of ZEN revealed that the major
fragment ion [M−H]−displays an m/z of 333, which is
consistent with the molecular weight of 15-OH-ZEN.
Simultaneously, the parent fragment ion produced a character-
istic daughter ion of m/z = 191.0. Qin et al.44 also reported the
observation of similar fragment daughter ions. Therefore, the
results showed that the degradation product by laccases of
ZEN was 15-OH-ZEN (Figure 8E,F). Notably, 15-OH-ZEN
(C18H22O6) possesses an additional hydrogen atom on the
15th carbon compared to ZEN (C18H22O5) as a result of the
hydroxylation reaction. It has been reported that 15-OH-ZEN
exhibits a 98% reduction in estrogenic activity compared to
ZEN, making it a relatively safe degradation product.45
Evaluation of Mycotoxin Degradation Efficiency in
Contaminated Feed Samples. Degradation experiments
were carried out on mycotoxins in contaminated feed samples,
including cotton meal, corn steep liquor, and distillers dried
grains (DDGS), using the culture supernatants containing the
enzymes from the recombinant strains.
The results showed that AoLac2 exhibited superior
efficiency in degrading mycotoxins in contaminated feed
samples compared with PpLac1 (Figure 9). PpLac1 achieved
degradation rates of 43.37% for AFB1 in cotton meal, 50.93%
for ZEN in corn steep liquor, and 48.83 and 40.55% for AFB1
and ZEN in DDGS, respectively. AoLac2 achieved degradation
rates of 55.97% for AFB1 in cotton meal, 82.54% for ZEN in
corn steep liquor, and 52.47 and 44.70% for AFB1 and ZEN in
DDGS, respectively. The enzyme AoLac2 exhibited a much
more effective degradation efficiency for ZEN in corn steep
liquor. The varying degradation efficiencies of laccases in
different feeds can likely be attributed to the diverse matrices
of the feeds, which may further interfere with the reaction
between laccases and mycotoxins.46 Furthermore, after
degradation, the residual levels of AFB1 and ZEN in the
contaminated feed samples ranged from 6.61 to 8.72 μg/kg
and 3.44 to 98.15 μg/kg, respectively. Importantly, all of these
levels were below the EU limit standards (No 165/2010, No
1126/2007).47,48 The residual levels for the two mycotoxins
are shown in Table S11.
