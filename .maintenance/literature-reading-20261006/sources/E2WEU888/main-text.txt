Transcription Factor Engineering in Aspergillus nidulans Leads to the
Discovery of an Orsellinaldehyde Derivative Produced via an
Unlinked Polyketide Synthase Gene

ABSTRACT: Secondary metabolites are generally produced by enzymes encoded by genes within a biosynthetic gene cluster.
Transcription factor genes are frequently located within these gene clusters. These transcription factors often drive expression of the
other genes of the biosynthetic gene cluster, and overexpression of the transcription factor provides a facile approach to express all
genes within a gene cluster, resulting in production of downstream metabolite(s). Unfortunately this approach is not always
successful, leading us to engineer more effective hybrid transcription factors. Herein, we attempted to activate a putative cryptic
biosynthetic gene cluster in Aspergillus nidulans using a combination of transcription factor engineering and overexpression
approaches. This resulted in the discovery of a novel secondary metabolite we term triorsellinaldehyde. Surprisingly, deletion of the
polyketide synthase gene within the gene cluster did not prevent triorsellinaldehyde production. However, targeted deletion of a
polyketide synthase gene elsewhere in the genome revealed its role in triorsellinaldehyde biosynthesis.
A
hallmark of fungal genomics is that the genes that encode
proteins involved in biosynthesis of specific secondary
metabolites (SMs) are usually, but not always, clustered within
the genome.1 Genome sequencing studies have revealed that
the number of SM biosynthetic gene clusters vastly exceeds the
number of known SMs,2−4 suggesting that many more SMs
remain to be discovered. Unfortunately, most putative SM
biosynthetic gene clusters are silent under standard laboratory
growth conditions.5 While a number of strategies have shown
moderate success in expressing these gene clusters and
determining their products, no one approach has been
universally successful.
The expression, and therefore regulation, of about half of
biosynthetic gene clusters is driven by one or more
transcription factors encoded within the cluster. Elucidating
the regulation of gene clusters lacking transcription factor
genes and determination of their SM products can be
challenging. However, upregulation of cluster-associated tran-
scription factors can, in some cases, result in high levels of
expression of other genes in the cluster and consequent
compound production.6−10 Often, however, simple over-
expression of the transcription factor(s) does not result in
production of compounds at sufficiently high levels for
isolation and analysis. Among 18 silent SM biosynthetic gene
clusters containing transcription factor genes in Aspergillus
nidulans, overexpression of the transcription factor genes using
the inducible alcA promoter [alcA(p)] resulted in the
production of adequate levels of SMs to permit isolation and
characterization in only three cases.11 For six of the clusters,
new compounds were detected upon induction, but the levels
of SM production were too low to permit characterization. For

approach may have been only partly successful for several
reasons. One problem is that the compounds produced may be
difficult to discern from background peaks in metabolite
profiles. Production of these compounds also is likely to
deplete precursor pools, leading to lower titers of target
molecules. We have subsequently addressed this problem by
deleting many of the most highly expressed gene clusters,
lowering the background substantially.12,13
Another problem is that simple upregulation of transcription
factor genes may not result in strong overexpression of the
target metabolic genes due to post-transcriptional regulation of
transcription factor activity. Multiple mechanisms controlling
transcription factor activity are known, including negative post-
translational regulation.14 We have developed an approach to
overcome this problem, at least in some instances, by
exploiting the modular structure of transcription factors15 to
construct synthetic hybrid transcription factors.16 These
modular transcription factors retain the native DNA binding
domain, which directs binding of the transcription factor to
DNA binding sites in promoters of metabolic gene targets. The
DNA binding domain is fused to a highly potent activation
domain that is active during late stationary phase,16 when SM,
and particularly polyketide, biosynthesis can be driven to high
levels.17,18 We have previously utilized this approach, fusing the
DNA binding domain of the AlnR transcription factor to the
activation domain of the AfoA transcription factor to activate
the silent gene cluster responsible for (+)-asperlin production
in A. nidulans.16
We have focused here on a putative SM biosynthetic gene
cluster, located on chromosome I, identified by Inglis et al.,19
that contains two Zn(II)2Cys6 transcription factor genes
(AN6788 and AN6790) adjacent to a highly reducing
polyketide synthase gene (AN6791). Simple upregulation of
AN6788 and AN6790 did not result in detectable new
compounds.11 However, it is important to note that this
putative biosynthetic gene cluster may not be a biosynthetic
gene cluster at all. A hallmark of biosynthetic gene clusters is
that the genes within the cluster are coordinately regulated.
Andersen et al.20 have used transcriptional profiling under 44
growth conditions to identify coregulated gene clusters and
predict SM biosynthetic gene clusters in A. nidulans. This
approach indicated that AN6788 through AN6791 are not
coregulated, raising the possibility that these adjacent genes do
not form a biosynthetic gene cluster. To address the question
of whether AN6788, AN6790, and AN6791 are part of a
biosynthetic gene cluster, we have used a multifaceted
approach by constructing an inducible AN6788 hybrid
transcription factor, and an inducible AN6790 transcription
factor, while implementing hierarchical boosting of inducible
expression though a high-level constitutive promoter and using
a strain with a reduced background SM profile. This approach
permitted the production of two known polyketide SMs,
orsellinaldehyde (1) and 3-(2,4-dihydroxy-6-methylbenzyl)-
orsellinaldehyde (2) (hereafter termed diorsellinaldehyde), as
well as a novel polyketide that we term triorsellinaldehyde (3)
(Figure 1). Surprisingly, targeted gene deletions revealed that a
polyketide synthase gene located on a different chromosome is
required for the biosynthesis of these polyketide SMs. Taken
together, these results suggest that the hybrid AN6788
transcription factor can, alone or with AN6790, activate
expression of the NR-PKS gene pkfA (AN3230).
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Hybrid Transcription Factor Rationale. Previously, our
group attempted to activate the A. nidulans AN6791
biosynthetic gene cluster, encoding a putative highly reducing
polyketide synthase, by placing the putative transcription factor
gene AN6788 under control of alcA(p).11 Details of the genes
in the putative AN6791 biosynthetic gene cluster are listed in

putative Zn(II)2Cys6 transcription factor genes (AN6788 and AN6790). We fused the native DNA binding domain (DBD) of AN6788 with the
AfoA activation domain (AD) to create a hybrid transcription factor. We created a strain in which both the hybrid AN6788-AfoA transcription

cluster and characterize its product. AfoA drives expression of

that upregulation of afoA drives the production of very high
levels (>2 g L−1) of asperbenzaldehyde, an intermediate in
asperfuranone biosynthesis.22 Therefore, the AfoA activation
domain is able to drive expression to high levels in late
stationary phase growth.
Generation of a Strain Expressing a Hybrid AN6788
Transcription Factor. To construct a hybrid AN6788
transcription factor, we first used InterProScan to identify
the AN6788 Zn(II)2Cys6 DNA binding domain at residues
15−65, and PCOILS to predict a putative dimerization motif
at residues 60−91.23,24 PSORTII analysis revealed two putative
nuclear localization signals of the SV40 Large T-antigen-type at
residues 29−32 and 58−61.25,26 We fused sequences
containing the predicted DNA binding domain of AN6788
[amino acids (aa) 1−200, as annotated at FungiDB (FungiDB.
org)] to sequences containing the AfoA aa 4−358,
(nucleotides 10−1199) as annotated at FungiDB (Figure 2).
Note, however, that afoA (AN1029) is likely incorrectly
annotated at FungiDB.16 The FungiDB annotation is based on
AspGD annotation five. The protein predicted from this
annotation lacks exon 1 and the DNA binding domain, and
bioinformatic analysis and functional data demonstrate that
AfoA is 86 amino acids longer at the N-terminus than the
FungiDB annotation indicates.16 (See Figure S1 in the SI of ref
16 for the corrected amino acid sequence for AfoA.) Amino
acids 4−358 in the FungiDB annotation is, thus, aa 90−444
according to our analysis. Note additionally that when the
fusion PCR fragment integrates by homologous recombination
(Figure 2), it creates a fusion gene that extends to the afoA
stop codon (aa 580 as per FungiDB and aa 666 according to
our analysis) and is followed by the afoA 3′ untranslated
region.
The hybrid transcription factor sequence was placed under
control of alcA(p), which is strongly induced by ethanol and a
variety of alcohols, aldehydes, ketones, and other compounds
(Figure 2).27 A yA 5′ flanking sequence and the Aspergillus
terreus pyrG gene (AtpyrG) were incorporated upstream of
alcA(p). The resulting construct was transformed into strain
LO9577. LO9577 carries a replacement of the yA gene with a
fragment containing the Aspergillus fumigatus pyroA gene
(AfpyroA) and the afoA gene (including its 3′ untranslated
region) driven by the aldA promoter [aldA(p)]. Integration of
the transforming fragment results in the AN6788 hybrid
transcription factor driven by alcA(p) at the yA locus.
Integration also replaces AfpyroA with AtpyrG. The resulting
transformants thus contained a hybrid transcription factor
gene, consisting of sequences encoding the DNA binding
domain of AN6788 and the activation domain of AfoA, under
control of alcA(p) (Figure 2). We analyzed two genetically
identical sibling strains with this genotype, LO10141 and
LO10145.

LO9577 is derived from the parent strain LO8030, which
contains deletions of eight of the most highly expressed SM
biosynthetic gene clusters.12 This serves two purposes; first, it
minimizes the SM background, facilitating detection of new
SMs. Second, it theoretically should free up SM precursor
molecules that can then feed into other biosynthetic pathways.
Finally, we placed the alcR gene under the control of the strong
constitutive gpdA promoter [gpdA(p)].28 The alcR gene
encodes the transcriptional activator AlcR, which, in the
presence of an inducer, binds to alcA(p) to strongly drive its
expression.29,30 To do this, a transforming fragment was
generated which contained the A. fumigatus riboB selectable
marker (AfriboB) fused to alcR, which was placed under
control of gpdA(p). This construct was transformed into
recipient strain LO10141 to generate LO10236 and into
LO10145 to generate LO10240. LO10236 and LO10240 have
the same genotype and they are expected to robustly express
the hybrid AN6788 transcription factor.
Engineering Strains for Expression of AN6790, or
both AN6788-AfoA and AN6790. We generated a set of
strains that express either native or hybrid forms of the
AN6790 transcription factor by the methodology described
above. LO10193 is similar to LO10141 and LO10145 except
that it carries an AN6790 hybrid transcription factor gene
under alcA(p) control at the yA locus instead of an AN6788
hybrid transcription factor gene. LO11974 carries a replace-
ment of the promoter of AN6790 with alcA(p). We also
constructed a strain that places both a hybrid AN6788 and
native AN6790 under control of alcA(p). To do this, a
transforming fragment was constructed that contained AfpyroA
as well as AN6790 placed under control of alcA(p). This
construct was integrated at AN6790 in the recipient strain
LO10240, replacing the endogenous promoter of AN6790
with alcA(p), thereby generating strain LO11940. A full list of
strains and corresponding genotypes used in this study is
shown in Table 1.
AN6788 Hybrid Transcription Factor Activation
Confers Production of Orsellinaldehyde and Diorselli-
naldehyde. We first attempted to activate the putative
AN6791 biosynthetic gene cluster by placing individual
transcription factors within the cluster under control of
alcA(p). Upon induction of strain LO10236, which expresses
a hybrid AN6788 under control of alcA(p) along with alcR
under control of gpdA(p), we detected two major induced
compounds via HPLC-DAD-MS (Figure 3). Following
purification and characterization, we identified these com-
pounds as 1 and the related 2 via 1D NMR, MS, and reference
to an in-house database of A. nidulans metabolites previously
isolated in our lab (Figures S1−S4, S13−14, Tables 2 and 3). 1
is an important aromatic polyketide precursor that functions as
an anti-inflammatory agent that also induces apoptosis.31,32 2
resembles a dimer of 1 that was previously isolated by placing
the A. nidulans nonreducing polyketide synthase AN3230
under control of alcA(p).11 In contrast, no compounds
detectable by HPLC-DAD-MS were induced with strain
LO11974, which expresses a native AN6790 coding sequence
under control of alcA(p) and also carries gpdA(p)alcR (Figure
3). We also note that we could not detect 1 or 2 upon
induction of LO10193, which expresses a hybrid AN6790
(data not shown).
Dual Transcription Factor Activation Confers Pro-
duction of Triorsellinaldehyde. We next overexpressed
both the hybrid AN6788 and native AN6790 simultaneously
using strain LO11940 in which both transcription factors are
driven by alcA(p). Upon alcA(p) induction, we again detected
both 1 and 2 as major products (Figure 3). Interestingly, the
yields of both compounds were higher upon dual transcription
factor activation; areas under the curve in negative-mode
extracted ion chromatograms revealed that the titers of 1 and 2
were increased ca. 5.4- and 12.0-fold, respectively, in strain
LO11940 relative to LO10236. We also detected in LO11940
a third induced product 3 with m/z 424. Importantly, we
observed that this compound was exclusively produced when
both transcription factors were overexpressed (Figure 3).
Detailed 1D and 2D NMR analysis revealed 3 as 3-(3-(2,6-
dihydroxy-4-methylbenzyl)-2,4-dihydroxy-6-methylbenzyl)-or-
sellinaldehyde (which we term triorsellinaldehyde), which
resembles a trimer of 1 and is new to science. For NMR
spectra (Figures S5−S12) and mass data (Figure S15), see SI.
For NMR chemical shift data, see Tables 2−4.
Structural Elucidation. On the basis of HRESIMS data, 3
had a molecular formula of C24H24O7 with 13 double bond
equivalents. Careful examination of the 1D and 2D NMR
spectra, coupled with the previous induction of 1 and 2,
suggested that 3 was an orsellinaldehyde derivative. Further,
the addition of ca. 136 Da, as determined by the mass
spectrum of 3, suggested that 3 resembles 2 with the addition
of a methylene group and a trisubstituted benzene (the
difference in mass between 1 and 2 is 136 Da; the addition of
136 Da to 2 is 424, the mass of 3).

methyl protons [δH 2.43 (3H, s), 2.08 (3H, s), 1.97 (3H, s)]

Production of the SMs Arising from Dual Tran-
scription Factor Activation Requires the Unlinked
Nonreducing Polyketide Synthase Gene AN3230. To
determine whether the AN6791 highly reducing polyketide
synthase gene adjacent to the two engineered transcription
factor genes was responsible for production of 1, 2, and 3, we
deleted AN6791 by gene replacement with the Aspergillus
oryzae pyrithiamine resistance gene (ptrA)34,35 in the dual
transcription factor overexpression strain LO11940 (alcA(p)-
AN6788 (aa 1−200)-afoA (aa 130−666) alcA(p)-AN6790).
The resultant AN6791 deletion strain (LO12110) showed no
effect on the production of these three SMs (Figure 3). 1 and 2
are known products arising from expression of the nonreducing
polyketide synthase encoded by the AN3230 (pkfA) gene
within the pkf gene cluster on linkage group VI.11 As 3 appears
to be structurally related to 1 and 2, we hypothesized that its
biosynthesis, in addition to production of 1 and 2, is
attributable to activation of pkfA by the overexpressed
AN6788 hybrid and AN6790 transcription factors. In order
to determine if these three induced SMs are produced via
PkfA, we deleted pkfA by gene replacement with A. oryzae ptrA
in LO11940 [alcA(p)-AN6788(aa 1−200)-afoA(aa 130−666),
alcA(p)-AN6790]. Deletion of pkfA (strain LO12139)
abolished the production of 1, 2, and 3 (Figure 3). Taken
together, these results indicate that the nonreducing polyketide
synthase encoded by pkfA is required for the production of 1,
2, and 3, and the highly reducing polyketide synthase encoded
by AN6791 is not required for the production of these
compounds.

We did not detect production of the aspernidines, the final
products of the pkf gene cluster,36 which indicates that the
aspernidine biosynthetic pathway is not functioning, although
the backbone polyketide is produced. The simplest explanation
is that the combination of transcription factors we have used
fails to drive expression of at least some critical genes of the pkf
cluster, although it clearly drives expression of pkfA. In this
regard, deletion of pkfB, pkf C or pkf D results in aspernidines
not being produced.36 If expression of any of these genes is
inadequate, then aspernidines are unlikely to be produced.
Finally, it is important to point out that 2 and 3 may not be
direct products of pkfA. Upregulation of pkfA alone results in
production of 1 and 2,11 so expression of other pkf genes is not
required for conversion of 1 to 2. An excess of 1 could be
converted to 2 and 3 spontaneously or via as yet undetermined
enzymes possibly unrelated to the pkf biosynthetic gene
cluster.
Our attempts herein to activate expression of AN6791 have
not resulted in AN6791-dependent production of compounds.
One obvious explanation is that expression of AN6791 is not
driven by AN6788-AfoA or AN6790 individually or in
combination. Another possibility, however, is that the PKS
encoded by AN6791 is not functional. To determine if
AN6791 is capable of producing compounds, we replaced the
promoters of AN6791 as well as AN6793 with alcA(p).
AN6793 is a putative hydrolase that is predicted to be required
for product release. We also replaced the promoter of alcR with
the gpdA promoter to enhance expression from the alcA
promoter, creating strain LO11902. Metabolite profiling of
LO11902 revealed an induced metabolite with m/z = 258
(Figure S17). These results reveal that AN6791 is functional
and suggest strongly that AN6788 and AN6790 do not drive
expression of AN6791 and AN6793.
We note that additional unknown regulatory mechanisms
may impede the full potential of the AN6788-AfoA hybrid
transcription factor to activate its target genes. The current
annotation of AN6788 is simplistic, as unpublished RNA-seq
data suggests that additional multiple alternative splice variants
of the native transcript likely encode truncated AN6788
protein isoforms containing the DNA binding domain, in
addition to full-length protein isoforms. In the hybrid
transcription factor strains, truncated isoforms lacking the
AfoA activation domain may conceivably be produced in
addition to the AN6788-AfoA hybrid transcription factor and
could compete with AN6788-AfoA activity.

Future Prospects Regarding Activation of Cryptic A.
nidulans Biosynthetic Gene Clusters. This study has
revealed that the combination of hybrid transcription factor
engineering with simultaneous overexpression of clustered
transcription factor genes may promote the production of
novel, potentially unexpected SMs. We also note that the
overexpression of alcR can markedly increase SM titers in
instances where biosynthetic genes are placed under control of
alcA(p). Previously,11 in 5 of the 18 attempts we made to
activate biosynthetic gene clusters by placing cluster-associated
transcription factor genes under control of alcA(p), novel
compounds were observed, but low titers prevented
purification and characterization of these compounds. The
use of gpdA(p)alcR may result in production of compounds in
amounts adequate for characterization. The approaches
described above should prove useful in future attempts to
identify these elusive SMs as well as those from other species.
■CONCLUSIONS
We have identified a new polyketide, triorsellinaldehyde (3).
Our data indicate that AN6788 and AN6790 are transcription
factors that in tandem can, at least in an engineered context,
drive expression of a nonreducing polyketide synthase gene
that is not clustered with the genes encoding these two
transcription factors. Our data are more consistent with those
of Andersen et al.,20 which suggest that the highly reducing
polyketide synthase gene AN6791 is not part of a biosynthetic
gene cluster with AN6788 and AN6790, than the analysis of
Inglis et al.19 that predicted that AN6787 through AN6791
form a biosynthetic gene cluster.
■EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
General Experimental Procedures. HPLC-DAD-MS spectra
were obtained using a ThermoFinnigan LCQ Advantage ion trap
mass spectrometer with a reverse phase C18 column (Alltech Prevail
C18; particle size, 3 μm; column, 2.1 by 100 mm) at a flow rate of 125
μL min−1. Conditions for MS included a capillary voltage of 5.0 kV, a
sheath gas flow rate at 60 arbitrary units, an auxiliary gas flow rate at
10 arbitrary units, and an ion transfer capillary temperature of 350 °C.
High-resolution mass spectra were acquired on a ThermoFisher Q-
Exactive Orbitrap mass spectrometer. MS conditions were as follows:
3.8 kV capillary voltage, sheath gas flow rate of 6 arbitrary units
(AUs), auxiliary gas flow rate of 2 AUs, and ion transfer capillary
temperature at 320 °C.
Compounds were fully purified using an Agilent 1200 HPLC-DAD
equipped with an RP-18 column (Phenomenex Luna 5 μm C18, 250 ×
10 mm) at a flow rate of 4.0 mL min−1. Normal-phase preparative thin
layer chromatography plates were purchased from Merck.
NMR spectra were recorded on an Oxford NMR AS400
spectrometer (400 MHz for 1H, 100 MHz for 13C) and processed
using Mestrelab MNova. All chemical shifts are listed in ppm and are
referenced to the residual 1H solvent peak. NMR solvents acetone-d6
and methanol-d4 were purchased from Cambridge Isotopes
Laboratories.
Molecular Genetic Manipulations. Transformation constructs
were generated by fusion PCR as previously described.37,38 All
constructs were verified by multiple diagnostic PCR reactions. The
selectable markers used were the A. fumigatus riboB gene (AfriboB),
the A. fumigatus pyroA gene (AfpyroA), the A. terreus pyrG gene
(AtpyrG), and the A. oryzae pyrithiamine resistance gene (ptrA).
Transformation procedures were conducted as previously described.37
Fermentation and HPLC-DAD-MS Analysis. For initial screen-
ing of strains, 3.0 × 107 spores of each strain were inoculated into 125
mL Erlenmeyer flasks containing 30 mL lactose minimal medium
(15.0 g L−1 lactose, 0.31 g L−1 KOH, 6.0 g L−1 NaNO3, 0.52 g L−1
KCl, 0.52 g L−1 MgSO4·7 H2O, 1.52 g L−1 KH2PO4, and 1.0 mL L−1
Hutner’s trace element solution).39 When needed, media were
supplemented with 0.5 mg L−1 pyridoxine, 2.5 mg L−1 riboflavin,
and/or uracil (1 g L−1) and uridine (10 mM). Cultures were
incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 180 rpm. For alcA(p) induction,
50 mM methyl ethyl ketone was added after 42 h. Following
induction, cultures were incubated for another 72 h.
Following incubation, mycelia were filtered from the culture
medium by vacuum filtration. The culture medium was extracted with
20 mL ethyl acetate. Organic extracts were isolated, dried (TurboVap
LV) and resuspended in 1 mL 4:1 methanol/dimethyl sulfoxide. Ten
μL of the extract was analyzed via HPLC-DAD-MS. The solvent
gradient was 95% MeCN−H2O (solvent B) in 5% MeCN−H2O
(solvent A), both of which contained 0.05% formic acid, as follows:
0% solvent B from 0 to 5 min, 0 to 100% solvent B from 5 to 35 min,
100% solvent B from 35 to 40 min, 100 to 0% solvent B from 40 to 45
min, and re-equilibration with 0% solvent B from 45 to 50 min.
Compound Purification. For isolation of 2, 5.0 × 108 spores of
LO10236 were inoculated into seven 2 L Erlenmeyer flasks containing
500 mL lactose minimal medium supplemented with 0.5 mg L−1
pyridoxine. Note that the titer of 1 was too low to permit purification
from this strain; its later identification was facilitated by the higher
titers of LO11940. Cultures were incubated at 37 °C with shaking at
180 rpm. For alcA(p) induction, 50 mM methyl ethyl ketone was
added after 42 h. Following induction, cultures were incubated for
another 72 h.
Following incubation, mycelia were filtered from the culture
medium by vacuum filtration. The culture medium was extracted
three times with a volume of ethyl acetate equal to the volume of the
medium. The ethyl acetate extract was evaporated in vacuo (660 mg)
and fractionated via isocratic normal-phase column chromatography
using 49:1 dichloromethane-methanol as a mobile phase. Fractions
containing the compound of interest as indicated by thin-layer
chromatography were combined and dried in vacuo (35 mg). These
combined subfractions were fully purified via HPLC-DAD. The
solvents used were 100% acetonitrile (solvent B) and 100% H2O
(solvent A), both containing 0.05% trifluoracetic acid. The solvent
gradient used was: 0 to 100% solvent B from 0 to 19 min, 100%
solvent B from 19 to 24 min, 100 to 0% solvent B from 24 to 25 min,
and re-equilibration with 0% solvent B from 25 to 30 min. 4.0 mg of 2
was isolated and analyzed by 1H and 13C NMR.
For isolation of 1 and 3, 5.0 × 108 spores of LO11940 were
inoculated into eight 2 L Erlenmeyer flasks, each containing 500 mL
lactose minimal medium. Culture conditions and induction
parameters were the same as above. The ethyl acetate extract (395
mg) was fractionated via normal-phase column chromatography using
ethyl acetate:hexanes as a mobile phase in the following ratios: 1:9,
3:7, 1:1, 7:3, 1:0. Fractions containing both 1 and 3 as indicated by
thin-layer chromatography were combined and dried in vacuo (155.5
mg). This subfraction was further purified via normal-phase
preparative thin-layer chromatography using 1:19 methanol/dichloro-
methane. Bands were excised from the preparative thin-layer
chromatography plate, flushed with organic solvent, and dried in
vacuo. 1 was analyzed with no additional purification. 3 was further
purified using HPLC-DAD using isocratic elution. The solvents used
for purification of 3 were 100% acetonitrile (solvent B) and 100%
H2O (solvent A), both containing 0.05% trifluoracetic acid. The ratio
of solvent A to solvent B was 1:1. 25.7 mg of 1 and 10.5 mg of 3 were
isolated.
