ABSTRACT: We report on the use of nitric oxide-mediated transcrip-
tional activation (NOMETA) as an innovative means to detect and access
new classes of microbial natural products encoded within silent
biosynthetic gene clusters. A small library of termite nest- and
mangrove-derived fungi and actinomyces was subjected to cultivation
profiling using a miniaturized 24-well format approach (MATRIX) in the
presence and absence of nitric oxide, with the resulting metabolomes
subjected to comparative chemical analysis using UPLC-DAD and GNPS
molecular networking. This strategy prompted study of Talaromyces sp.
CMB-TN6F and Coccidiodes sp. CMB-TN39F, leading to discovery of the
triterpene glycoside pullenvalenes A−D (1−4), featuring an unprece-
dented triterpene carbon skeleton and rare 6-O-methyl-N-acetyl-D-
glucosaminyl glycoside residues. Structure elucidation of 1−4 was
achieved by a combination of detailed spectroscopic analysis, chemical
degradation, derivatization and synthesis, and biosynthetic considerations.
T
he last two decades have witnessed a resurgence of
interest in microbial natural products, in part due to the
widespread adoption of an array of technological innovations
in the fields of natural product detection, isolation, character-
ization, and structure elucidation, but also due to a growing
appreciation that microbial genomes are far richer in natural
product biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) than previously
imagined. Significantly, many of these newly detected BGCs
are under tight transcriptional regulation, effectively denying
access to encoded natural products until activated by some
form of stimuli. In the natural world transcriptional activation
can, for example, be stimulated by changes in the composition
and abundance of carbon and nitrogen sources including the
physical state of cultivations (i.e., solid vs liquid, levels of
salinity, light, pH, temperature, oxygenation, and metal ions)
and/or by exposure to chemical cues produced by other
microbes. In a laboratory setting the varying of some of these
parameters by a program of cultivation profiling can, in
principle, reveal optimal conditions for the transcription/
production of new classes of microbial natural product.
However, cultivation profiling is not without its challenges,
with traditional approaches typically relying on shake flasks
and/or solid agar Petri dish cultivations that can be costly in
consumables and in demands on equipment, space, labor, and
time. Likewise with the use of natural chemical cues, which can
suffer from high cost and poor availability, or concerns over
their relevance to stimulating different genera/species, similarly
with co-culture, where the very nature of the defensive
response can make successful scale up cultivation problematic.
In an effort to overcome some of the challenges faced by
cultivation profiling we implemented a miniaturized 24-well
plate methodology (MATRIX)1 that facilitates the efficient,
reliable, and simultaneous small-scale profiling of bacteria and
fungi in multiple media, under shaken and static broth as well
as solid agar conditions. The MATRIX approach is highly
flexible and can readily accommodate a wide range of
parameters, including different media and physical conditions,
and the addition of natural or unnatural chemical cues. In situ
solvent extraction of MATRIX cultures followed by UPLC-
DAD and/or UPLC-QTOF-MS/MS global natural products
social (GNPS)2 molecular networking analysis provides a rapid
and cost-effective means to differentiate new from known and
rare from common natural products, while simultaneously
optimizing production of target chemistry.

Furthermore, in an effort to develop a natural chemical cue
that could be operationalized as a practical productivity tool, in
2014 we reported that a significant number of taxonomically
diverse fungi respond to very low doses (0.6 ng/mL) of
lipopolysaccharide (LPS).3 It was demonstrated that LPS
stimulated the endogenous production of nitric oxide (NO) as
a transcriptional activator “chemical cue” that could enhance,
suppress, activate, and/or accelerate the production of
biosynthetically and chemically diverse natural products across
multiple genera of fungi. Expanding on this discovery, in 2019
we reported on the natural chemical communication between
Aspergillus sp. CMB-AsM0423 and Streptomyces sp. CMB-
StM0423, co-isolated from a sample of Australian beach sand.4
This study revealed that during opportunistic co-cultivation a
weakly bacteriostatic diketopiperazine produced by the
Aspergillus stimulated the Streptomyces to produce NO, which
in turn served as a transcriptional activator of a Streptomyces
silent BGC encoding for a new class of fungistatic natural
products, the heronapyrroles. These twin observations inspired
the concept of nitric oxide-mediated transcriptional activation
(NOMETA), where we posited that addition of NO to either
fungal or bacterial monocultures could activate selected silent
BGCs and provide access to new chemistry. To render
NOMETA practicable, it was determined that commercially
available sodium nitroprusside (SNP) could serve as a suitable
NO surrogate, applicable to both analytical (MATRIX) and
scaled-up cultivations.4
This current study describes our application of MATRIX
and NOMETA methodology to detect a new class of triterpene
N-acetyl-D-glucosaminyl glycoside, pullenvalenes A−D (1−4),
from the Australian termite nest-derived fungus Talaromyces
sp. CMB-TN6F. Subsequent comparative chemical profiling of
1250 microbial extracts in our collection detected only one
other pullenvalene producer, Coccidioides sp. CMB-TN39F,
which coincidentally was co-isolated from the termite nest with
CMB-TN6F. What follows is an account of our investigation
into the chemistry of both Talaromyces sp. CMB-TN6F and
Coccidioides sp. CMB-TN39F, leading to the production,
isolation, and structure elucidation of 1−4, with structures
assigned on the basis of detailed spectroscopic analysis,
chemical degradation, partial synthesis, and biosynthetic
considerations.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
As part of our ongoing investigations into Australian microbial
natural products and the potential of MATRIX and NOMETA
protocols, we investigated a library of microbes isolated from a
termite nest collected in Pullenvale, Queensland, Australia (16
fungi and 10 actinomyces), and samples of mangrove soil and
plant material collected near Brisbane, Queensland, Australia
(28 fungi and 15 actinomyces). All 69 microbes were subjected
to MATRIX cultivation profiling using potato-dextrose agar
(PDA) for fungi and ISP2 broth for actinomyces, in both the
absence (non-NOMETA) and presence (NOMETA) of the
NO source sodium nitroprusside. Following in situ extraction
with EtOAc, each analyte was subjected to UPLC-DAD
analysis to detect NOMETA responses. To validate our
NOMETA approach, we used the positive control Streptomyces
sp. CMB-StM0423, which had previously been reported to be
NOMETA responsive, leading to transcriptional activation of
the heronapyrroles.4 This investigation detected a very
significant NOMETA response, with the termite nest-derived
fungus Talaromyces sp. CMB-TN6F (Figure S1) activating
production of a suite of natural products (1−4) possessing
molecular formulas unprecedented among known (reported)
fungal metabolites.
To determine if the NOMETA response observed in CMB-
TN6F could be replicated by simply modifying culture
conditions, we used our MATRIX strategy to profile the
CMB-TN6F secondary metabolome across 11 different media
compositions (Table S1), under solid agar as well as shaken
and static broth conditions, with and without NOMETA.
UPLC-DAD analysis of the resulting 66 MATRIX extracts
confirmed that 1−4 could not be detected under any non-
NOMETA MATRIX conditions. Furthermore, our MATRIX
analysis established the optimal conditions for CMB-TN6F
production of 1−4 as NOMETA in M1 shaken broth (Figure 2
and Figure S2).
Armed with a diagnostic GNPS cluster for 1−4, we searched
our GNPS data set assembled from (non-NOMETA) cultures
of 1250 bacteria and fungi sourced from geographically and
ecologically diverse Australian marine and terrestrial substrates
and including the 15 termite nest-derived fungi co-isolated
with CMB-TN6F. The goal of this study was to determine if
1−4 were constitutively produced by any other microbes (i.e.,
without NOMETA). Significantly, 1−4 were detected in the
PDA culture of Coccidioides sp. CMB-TN39F (Figures S3 and
S4), a termite nest-derived fungi co-isolated with CMB-TN6F,
with a subsequent MATRIX and GNPS analysis (Figures S5−
S8) confirming high yields from M2 agar (26.5 °C, 20 days).
As CMB-TN39F was a superior producer of 1−4 compared to
CMB-TN6F, we undertook separate scaled up M2 (80 plate)
and PDA (100 plate) cultivations, with solvent extraction and
partitioning followed by reversed phase chromatography
(Schemes S1 and S2), with M2 yielding 1 and 2, and PDA
2−4. What follows is an account of the structure elucidation of
1−4.

Subsequent analysis of the 2D NMR COSY and HMBC data

The relative configuration of the sugar residue in 1 was
readily assigned as 6-O-methyl-N-acetylglucosamine on the
basis of diagnostic J measurements and ROESY correlations
(Figure 3B). For example, measures for J2′,3′ (10.9 Hz), J3′,4′
(8.8 Hz), and J4′,5′ (10.0 Hz) were consistent with axial
orientations for H-2′, H-3′, H-4′, and H-5′, while J1′,2′ (3.7 Hz)
suggested a equatorial orientation for H-1′ consistent with an
α-glycoside linkage to the terpene residue. ROESY correlations
between H-2′ and both H-1′ and H-4′ were supportive of this
configurational assignment.
The relative configuration of the ring A−C tricyclic core in 1
was established by consideration of ROESY correlations
between H-3 and H-7 which necessitated they be cofacial
and pseudoaxially oriented, requiring the 3-glycosyl substituent
to be pseudoequatorially disposed. Additional ROESY
correlations between Hβ-9 and both H-7 and H3-27 required
a cofacial arrangement, while a correlation between H-10 and
H3-26 required they be cofacially oriented and opposite that of
H-3, H-7, Hβ-9, and H3-27, thereby establishing the complete
relative configuration of the ring A−C tricyclic core (Figure
3B).
The relative configuration of the ring D substructure in 1
was established through diagnostic axial−axial J22,21ax (11.7 Hz)
and axial−equatorial J22,21eq (2.3 Hz), with a ROESY
correlation between H-22 and H2-16 requiring H-18 to be
equatorially oriented. Likewise, a ROESY correlation between
H3-29 and H2-17 required the former to be equatorially
oriented, establishing the ring D relative configuration as
shown (Figure 3B).
Finally, consideration of the C-15 to C-14 Newman
projection (Figure 3C) supported a single C-14/C-15 relative
configuration capable of accommodating the observed ROESY
correlations from H-15 to both the α-facial H-10 and H-13 and
between H-10 and H2-16, and Hα-13 and H3-28. Taken
together, these considerations support assignment of the
relative configuration for rings A−C extended to C-15, and
separately for ring D in 1.
To determine the absolute configuration about the sugar
residue, an analytical scale sample of 1 was subjected to acid
hydrolysis in methanol to release the methyl glycoside 5, which
was immediately derivatized with L-Marfey’s reagent (L-FDAA,
1-fluoro-2,4-dinitrophenyl-5-L-alanine amide) to yield 6
(Figure 4), which a UPLC-DAD-MS analysis revealed to be
a resolvable ∼2:1 mixture of α:β anomers (Figure S21). To
secure authentic synthetic standards, a sample of N-acetyl-D-
glucosamine was subjected to sequential conversion to a
mixture of methyl glycoside α:β anomers (i); followed by
sequential protection first as the 6-trityl ether (ii) then 3,4-
dibenzyl ether (iii); then selective detritylation to the 6-
hydroxy (iv) followed by conversion to the 6-O-methyl ether
(v); debenzylation to the 3,4-dihydroxy (vi) and acid
hydrolysis and conversion to the L-FDAA Marfey’s derivative
6 and the D-FDAA Marfey’s derivative 7 (Figures 4 and S15−
S20). Significantly a UPLC-DAD-MS analysis confirmed 6
prepared from 1 was identical to the synthetic authentic
standard prepared from N-acetyl-D-glucosamine (Figure S21),
thereby establishing a D configuration about the sugar residue
and, together with biosynthetic considerations (see discussion
for 2 below), allowing assignment of the structure for
pullenvalene A (1) as shown (Figure 1, partial relative/
absolute configuration about rings A−C and D only).

Revisiting the need to assign absolute configurations about
the terpene substructure rings A−C in 1−4, it was reasoned
that hydrolysis of glycosides 2 and 4 would yield a common
aglycone that based on our earlier studies (see 1a above)
would undergo selective monoesterification at the 4-OH (not
the unreactive 3-OH). With the supplies of 2 and 4 from
CMB-TN39F near exhausted we were delighted when parallel
investigations of a library of >3000 microbial isolates
assembled under the auspices of the Australian citizen science
initiative, Soils for Science, disclosed a fungus (S4S-07771A07)
from a backyard soil sample collected in Emerald, in the central
Highlands region of Queensland, as an excellent producer of
pullenvalene D (4) (together with other new pullenvalenes,
work-in-progress). Hydrolysis of a sample of 4 from S4S-
07771A07 yielded the aglycone 4a (Table S9, Figures S46 and
S47), which as predicted converted to the Mosher esters 4b (S
ester) and 4c (R ester), with a Mosher analysis (Table S10,
Figures 9 and S48−S50), confirming a 4S absolute
configuration, and extended to the 3R,4S,6S,7S,10R,14R,15S
configuration about the tricyclic ring A−C terpene core in 1−4
as shown.
The GNPS molecular family associated with pullenvalenes in
CMB-TN39F under different MATRIX conditions revealed an
array of minor biosynthetically related co-metabolites (Figure
10A). Interestingly, while M2 cultivation was optimal for
production of 1−4 and the minor monoglycosides 8−11, rice
or PDA cultivations provided access to the additional minor
diglycosides 12a/b and 13a/b. Although these minor
metabolites could not be isolated and independently
characterized, it is possible to tentatively assign structures
based on molecular formulas, biosynthetic considerations, and
GNPS co-clustering with 1−4 (Figure 10A). For example, we
propose that 8 (C36H63NO9 Δmmu +2.3), 9 (C37H65NO9
Δmmu +2.2), and 10 (C38H65NO10 Δmmu +2.0) are likely the
deacetyl-demethyl, deacetyl, and demethyl analogues of 1,
respectively, while 11 (C37H65ON10 Δmmu +2.3) is a deacetyl
analogue of 2 (Figure 10B). Likewise, 12a/b (C46H80N2O14
Δmmu +2.4) is tentatively attributed to either one or both of
the monodeacetylated analogues 12a and/or 12b, and 13a/b
(C47H80N2O15 Δmmu +2.1) to either one or both of the
monodemethylated analogues 13a and/or 13b, of 2. This
MATRIX−GNPS analysis of CMB-TN39F further illustrates
the impact that cultivation conditions can have on both the
level of production and chemical diversity of pullenvalenes and
how access to the broader range of analogues can be achieved
by scale-up cultivation under multiple conditions.
Accounts of fungal natural products featuring a 6-O-methyl-
N-acetyl-D-glucosaminyl glycoside residue are rare and to the
best of our knowledge limited to twin reports in 2008 of a rice

sequence for BraB to probe the genomes of Coccidioides sp.
CMB-TN39F and Talaromyces sp. CMB-TN6F to detect
putative pullenvalene BGCs (Figure 11B and C), each of
which featured a terpene cyclase, a cytochrome P450, and an
N-acetylglucosamine transferase.
The pullenvalenes A−D (1−4) did not exhibit growth
inhibitory activity (IC50 > 30 μM) against human colon
(SW620) or lung (NCI-H460) carcinoma cells (Figure S51) or

Escherichia coli ATCC 11775, the fungus Candida albicans

methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25923

isolated from the termite nest (Figure S53).
In summary, this study illustrates how an integrated program
of cultivation profiling (MATRIX), with and without nitric
oxide-mediated transcriptional activation (NOMETA), and
coupled with UPLC-DAD and molecular network (GNPS)-
guided chemical profiling, enabled discovery of a new class of
transcriptionally silent natural product, the pullenvalenes.
Detailed spectroscopic analysis, supported by chemical
degradation, derivatization, and synthesis, and biosynthetic
considerations permitted assignment of structures for
pullenvalenes A−D (1−4), featuring a new triterpene carbon
skeleton and the rare 6-O-methyl-N-acetyl-D-glucosaminyl
glycoside residue. Minor co-metabolites co-clustering in the
GNPS pullenvalene molecular family were tentatively identi-
fied as demethylation and deacetylation analogues of 1−4. In
addition to proposing a plausible biosynthetic path from the
precursor squalene to the pullenvalenes through a sequence of
epoxide-mediated cyclizations, 1,3-hydride/methyl shifts, and
glycosylations, we used knowledge of the brasilane N-
acetylglucosamine transferase BraB to probe the genomes of
Coccidioides sp. CMB-TN39F and Talaromyces sp. CMB-TN6F
to detect putative pullenvalene BGCs.

Genomic Analysis of Coccidioides sp. CMB-TN39F and
Talaromyces sp. CMB-TN6F. The extracted DNA was sequenced
using the Illumina Nextseq 2X150 bp platform. The sequencing runs
yield 3.6 gigabases of sequence reads for Coccidioides S5 and 2.2 GB
for Talaromyces S6. These reads were trimmed using TrimmomaticPE
v0.3916 to eliminate bases at the 3 and 5 prime of the read with a
minimum PHRED cutoff of 30; only reads longer than 120 bases were
kept for assembly, which was performed with SPADES v3.13.1.28 The
resulting assemblies were processed with AUGUSTUS 3.4.029 to
predict coding sequences (CDS) and protein sequences. Annotation
of biosynthetic gene clusters was then performed with AntiSMASH
v6.1.1.30 The AntiSMASH-annotated genomes were then analyzed
with BiG-SCAPE to identify shared BGC families. Only one shared
BGC family was found to be brasilane N-acetylglucosamine
transferase. Orthologous BGCs for this family were mined and
compared using the CORASON pipeline, adapted for fungal genomes
(Figure S55).
