Double Bond Geometry Controls Diterpene Synthase Catalysis

ABSTRACT: Terpene synthases (TSs) generate extraordinary struc-
tural complexity through carbocation cascades, but the mechanistic role
of the substrate’s alkene geometry in directing enzyme catalysis remains
unexplored. Here, we interrogate diterpene synthase (DTS) mechanisms
using a 6Z-conﬁgured geranylgeranyl diphosphate analogue ((6Z)-
GGPP) as a probe. Across a diverse panel of class I DTSs, substitution
of the natural substrate GGPP with (6Z)-GGPP led to profound shifts
in product proﬁles, yielding a wide array of diterpenes that exhibit
multiple previously unreported carbon skeletons. A mechanistic analy-
sis combining isotopic labeling experiments and density functional the-
ory (DFT) calculations reveals that the 6Z double bond reshapes
carbocationic landscapes, explaining the enzymatic formation of the
isolated products.
KEYWORDS: terpene synthases, substrate analogues, enzyme catalysis, isotopic labeling, DFT calculations
■INTRODUCTION
Terpenoids constitute the largest class of natural products, and
their remarkable structural diversity arises from the ability of
terpene synthases (TSs) to convert acyclic isoprenoid diphos-
phates into complex hydrocarbon skeletons through carboca-
tion cascade reactions.1−3 Prenyltransferases (PTs) catalyze
the head to tail condensation of dimethylallyl pyrophosphate
(DMAPP) and isopentenyl pyrophosphate (IPP) to produce
the acyclic oligoprenyl pyrophosphate precursor. Classical
trans-PTs generate the all-E-conﬁgured intermediates geranyl
(GPP),4 farnesyl (FPP),5 geranylgeranyl (GGPP),6 geranylfar-
nesyl
(GFPP)7
and
even
farnesylfarnesyl
pyrophosphate
(FFPP),8 which serve as the primary substrates of TSs ranging
from mono- to triterpene synthases. These enzymes catalyze
multistep cationic cascade reactions that may be initiated by
diphosphate abstraction (type I TS) or protonation of the sub-
strate (type II TS). The resulting cationic intermediates can
react through intramolecular additions of double bonds to cat-
ionic centres, leading to ring closures, Wagner−Meerwein rear-
rangements, hydride and proton transfers, which translocate the
cation before another cyclization reaction may proceed. The
catalytic process is terminated by the attack of water to yield a
terpene alcohol, or by deprotonation resulting in a terpene
hydrocarbon. Which cyclization reactions occur inside the
hydrophobic cavity of TSs is a question of the substrate confor-
mation that is determined through interaction of the substrate
with the hydrophobic active site.
Besides E-selective PTs, diﬀerent enzymes are known that
produce Z-conﬁgured isoprenoid pyrophosphates (Figure 1A),
including PTs that generate neryl diphosphate (NPP, C10),9
(2Z,6Z)-FPP,10,11 and (2Z,6Z,10Z)-GGPP.12 Furthermore, a
recently characterized PT from Streptomyces clavuligerus pro-
duces stereoisomeric mixtures of GGPP and GFPP, including
(2Z,6Z,10Z)-GGPP (also named nerylneryl pyrophosphate,
NNPP) and (2Z,6Z,10Z,14Z)-GFPP.13
Only a limited number of terpenes are known to be formed
by
TSs
from
Z-conﬁgured
oligoprenyl
pyrophosphates
(Figure 1B). For instance, PsTPS1 from the ﬂea beetle
Phyllotreta striolata converts (2Z,6E)-FPP into the aggregation
pheromone (6R,7S)-himachala-9,11-diene (1),11 Cop6 from
Coprinus cinereus catalyzes the conversion of the same substrate
into α-acoradiene (2),14 and recently characterized FPP syn-
thase homologs from African frogs turn (2Z,6E)-FPP into bisa-
bolane sesquiterpenes including anymol (3).15 In addition,
ShZIS from Solanum habrochaites converts (2Z,6Z)-FPP into
7-epi-zingiberene (4).16 Among diterpenes, SlTPS21 from
Solanum lycopersicum mediates the biosynthesis of lycosantalene
(5) from NNPP.17,18 LfTPS1 from Leucophyllum frutescens19 as
well as EdTPS22 and EdtTPS4 from Eremophila drummondii
and E. denticulata, respectively,20 generate dihydroserrulatene
(6) from NNPP, while ElTPS31 and ElTPS3 from E. lucida
convert this precursor into (3Z,7Z,11Z)-cembratrien-15-ol (7)

from Streptomyces paromomycinus was shown to transform
NNPP into (1S,2Z,6Z,10Z,14S)-casbene (9).21
These examples reveal that double bond geometry is a driver
of terpene diversiﬁcation, but the general principles through
which double bond conﬁgurations govern TS-catalyzed reactiv-
ity remain poorly understood. Canonical diterpene biosynthesis
from GGPP typically progresses through initial 1,10, 1,11 or
1,14-cyclization, as exempliﬁed by the cases of benditerpe-
2,6,15-triene (10),22 cyclooctat-9-en-7-ol (11),23 phomopsene

bond usually gets involved at later stages of the terpene cycliza-
tion cascade, and therefore we assumed that a modiﬁcation of
its conﬁguration may provide a strategic entry point to probe
how geometric changes of the substrate structure may reshape
carbocation landscapes and consequently product diversity.
Here we report on the discovery of 14 new diterpenes obtained
with 14 class I diterpene synthases (DTS) from (2E,6Z,10E)-
GGPP, named for simplicity henceforth in this article (6Z)-
GGPP. An investigation of the cyclization mechanisms toward
the most interesting compounds through isotopic labeling exper-
iments and DFT calculations is included.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Synthesis of (6Z)-GGPP
The substrate analogue (6Z)-GGPP was synthesized as shown
in Figure 2. Starting from geraniol (14), a bromination and sub-
sequent alkylation of ethyl acetoactetate yielded the β-ketoester
15 that upon saponiﬁcation and spontaneous decarboxylation
resulted in geranylacetone (16). A Z-selective Peterson oleﬁnation
with ethyl methyldiphenylsilylacetate26,27 aﬀorded (2Z)- and (2E)-
17 (dr = 2:1) that were separated by column chromatography. A
DIBAIH reduction to 18 was followed by bromination and alkyl-
ation of ethyl acetoacetate yielding 19. Subsequent saponiﬁcation
with decarboxylation to 20 and Horner−Wadsworth−Emmons
reaction resulted in (2E)- and (2Z)-21 that were again separable
by column chromatography. Reduction to the alcohol 22, bromi-
nation and nucleophilic substitution with diphosphate gave access
to the target compound (6Z)-GGPP with an overall yield of
5% over 12 steps.

assigned based on biosynthetic considerations, assuming the
same stereochemical course for the initial cyclization to A4 as

More pronounced eﬀects were observed in the reaction cata-
lyzed by the cyclooctat-9-en-ol synthase from S. iakyrus

The cyclization mechanism from GGPP to cyclooctat-9-en-7-
ol (30) proceeds through initial 1,11-10,14-cyclization to A5,
and then involves a third cyclization, several hydride shifts and
an interesting rearrangement of a cyclopropylmethyl cation
(E5) (Figure 5A).38 Several compounds obtained from (6Z)-
GGPP
can
be
explained
through
similar
initial
steps:
Ionization to A6 and 1,11-10,14-cyclization (with B6 that is
not a minimum structure skipped) lead to C6 that can undergo
a 1,2-hydride shift to D6 and deprotonation to 31 and 32.
Alternatively, a slight change in the cyclization mode to a
1,11-2,10-cyclization and deprotonation give access to 33. The
biosynthesis of 34 requires a complex continuation of the cas-
cade from D6 through several hydride and proton shifts,
another cyclization and a skeletal rearrangement with formation
of the spirocyclic skeleton.
The reaction mechanism was computationally analyzed by DFT,
using the wB97M-V/Def2-TZVPPD//B97D3/6-31G(d,p) level of
theory.39−42 These calculations revealed the energetic feasibility
of the proposed mechanism with elementary steps that exhibit
low activation barriers or are even barrierless (Figure 5B and
Figure S72 and Table S9). The highest activation barrier
(8.2 kcal/mol) was determined for the transannular 1,5-proton
shift from E6 to F6.

The main natural product of MgMS from GGPP is myrothec-

and 39 can be explained analogously, allowing assignment of

Compound 40 is also formed from (6Z)-GGPP by the clitopi-

Several DTSs known to produce a series of structurally related
tetracyclic compounds were investigated for their potential to
convert (6Z)-GGPP, including N. testacea phomopsene (41) syn-
thase (NtPS),45 spiroviolene (42) synthase from Streptomyces
violens (SvS),48,49 cattleyene (43) synthase from Streptomyces
cattleya (CyS),45 and phomopsene/allokutznerene (41/44) syn-
thase from Allokutzneria albata (PmS)50 (Figure 7A). Despite
their structural complexity, the products formed by these
enzymes share a common structural motif composed of a cis-
fused ﬁve-membered ring bearing two geminal methyl substitu-
ents in addition to a third Me group at a quaternary carbon
shared with the adjacent ring; these enzymes belong to the
cyclopentane-forming DTSs.51 Interestingly, incubation of these
enzymes with (6Z)-GGPP revealed the appearance of several
shared chromatographic peaks, suggesting convergence toward
a common carbocation cascade.
The incubation of (6Z)-GGPP with NtPS resulted in a mark-
edly increased product complexity compared to reactions with
native GGPP (Figure S10). Five compounds were isolated and
structurally characterized as (3E,7Z)-dolabella-3,7,18-triene (39),
(1(10)Z,4E)-prenylgermacrene A (40), (6Z)-cis-xeniaphyllene
(45), and the neophomopsenes A (46) and B (50) (Figures 7
and 8 and Figures S73−S112 and Tables S10−S14). SvS also pro-
duced 40 and 46, besides the main products neophomopsene C
(47) and neoviolene (49) (Figures S11 and S113−S128 and
Tables S15 and S16). Conversion of (6Z)-GGPP with CyS
yielded the main product 45, besides 40, 46 and 47, and PmS gave
the main product 45 and traces of 46 and 47. Due to signal over-
laps, for compound 49 the interpretation of the NOESY correla-
tions did not allow an unambiguous structural assignment and
the alternative structure of 48 was taken into consideration. This
issue was resolved by experiments discussed below.
The formation of the compounds isolated from the
cyclopentane-forming DTSs can be rationalized through the fol-
lowing mechanism (Figure 7B): After ionization and 1,11-
10,14-cyclization to C8, ring expansion to D8, ring opening to
E8 and reverse ring closure lead to F8. This mechanism closely
resembles a model proposed by Tantillo for variediene biosyn-
thesis.52 Additional ring closures to G8 and H8 may be
followed by ring expansion to I8, ring contraction to L8 and
1,3-hydride shift to M8 that is the precursor of 46. Another
1,2-hydride shift leads to N8 and 47. Compound 45 can be
explained by 2,10-cyclisation of B8 to O8 and deprotonation.
Continuing from O8 (Figure 8A), a ring closure to P8, 1,2-
methyl shift to Q8 and a ring contraction lead to R8, that fur-
ther reacts by ring expansion to S8. This intermediate is a
branching point from which the two possible structures 48
and 49 for neoviolene can both be explained. S8 may either
react through a 1,2-hydride shift, ring opening and deproton-
ation to 48, or through a sequence of a 1,2- and a 1,3-hydride
shift, ring opening and deprotonation to 49. From G8 a 1,2-
hydride shift, ring closure, another 1,2-hydride transfer and
deprotonation explain the biosynthesis of 50 (Figure 8B).
Notably, these two biosynthetic pathways from S8 to 48 and
49, respectively, lead to diﬀerent positions of the migrating
hydrogens, which allows to distinguish between the alternatives
through an isotopic labeling experiment. For this purpose,
(6Z)-(2-2H)GGPP was synthesized (Scheme S2, green H =
2H in Figures 7 and 8) and incubated with SvS. Deuterium
labeled 49 was isolated and analyzed by 13C- and 1H-NMR
spectroscopy,
revealing
deuterium
migration
to
C6
(Figure S129), which is in line with the structure of 49, but
not of 48 for the isolated compound neoviolene.
A similar NMR analysis of deuterated 46 obtained from
(6Z)-(2-2H)GGPP conﬁrmed the terminal hydride migration
sequence L8-M8-N8 in its biosynthesis by NtPS (Figure S130).
Furthermore, GC/MS analysis of the SvS products 46 and 47
returend molecular ion peaks at m/z 273 (retainment of 2H for
46) and m/z 272 (loss of 2H for 47), respectively, which is in line
with the mechanistic proposal regarding the hydride shift
sequence L8-M8-N8 (Figure S131).

unknown diterpene skeletons. Isotope labeling experiments in
combination with DFT calculations were employed to elucidate
the corresponding cyclization mechanisms, highlighting the role
of substrate conformational constraints in directing terpene
cyclization. The results obtained through this study also have
an interesting evolutionary aspect: Recent research has demon-
strated that some TSs naturally convert substrates with Z-
conﬁgured double bonds, but why is this the case? It seems
possible that it was in ﬁrst instance the prenyltransferase making
the oligoprenyl pyrophosphate precursor that has evolved,
changing its stereoselectivity from an E-selective to a Z-selective
enzyme. The existing TSs in an organism in which such an evo-
lutionary event may have happened may still have accepted the
changed substrate, and only later evolutionary processes may
have adapted a TS for an optimal substrate turnover. Such adap-
tive processes may also have resulted in a higher product selec-
tivity. Not surprisingly, many enzyme-substrate combinations
using (6Z)-GGPP investigated in this study gave a lower prod-
uct selectivity than observed with normal GGPP, which seems
to support such a view. From a broader perspective, our ﬁnd-
ings open new avenues for terpene scaﬀold diversiﬁcation and
mechanistic interrogation. Substrate geometry modulation
oﬀers a complementary strategy to enzyme engineering, enables
access to otherwise inaccessible carbocation landscapes to dis-
sect and manipulate the fundamental logic of TS catalysis.
