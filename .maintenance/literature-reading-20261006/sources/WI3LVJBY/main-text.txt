ABSTRACT: Polyketides, nonribosomal peptides, and their hybrids constitute a major class of clinically important antibiotics. To
avoid self-toxicity, producing organisms must employ mechanisms of self-resistance. Here, we describe the structures, biosynthesis,
and self-resistance strategy of the aridomycins, new linear polyketide antibiotics. Using NMR spectroscopy, whole genome
sequencing, and bioinformatics, we determined the absolute configurations of all 28 stereocenters and identified a previously
unrecognized acyltransferase specificity motif (GHSQ···FVAH) associated with hydroxymalonyl-ACP incorporation. We also
assigned the biosynthetic gene cluster (BGC) for aridomycin, as well as for blasticidin A, a polyketide structurally related to the well-
characterized aflastatin A, both of which support the new AT motif assignment. Interestingly, aridomycin A, the glycosylated
congener, lacked antimicrobial activity against multidrug-resistant pathogens and the producing Amycolatopsis strains. Its temporal
production preceding the aglycone aridomycin B, together with substrate specificity studies of the glycosyltransferase AriGT,
indicate that reversible glucosylation acts as a self-resistance mechanism. To our knowledge, this represents the first example of such
a strategy outside the macrolide class.
■INTRODUCTION
Antimicrobial resistance is a growing public health problem
that calls for the development of new antibiotics and
antifungals. Historically, Actinomycetes, GC-rich Gram-pos-
itive bacteria, have been a bountiful source of clinically
approved antibiotics. Together with the biosynthetic gene
cluster (BGC) encoding for antibiotic biosynthesis, a
resistance gene must also be present in the BGC or the
genome at large to protect the producing organism from
death.1 Recently, massive amounts of whole genome
sequencing data have become available, and bioinformatic
searches for microbial and fungal BGCs has emphasized the
enormous potential for discovery of new chemistry from these
systems.2,3 Further, DNA sequencing and bioinformatics have
revealed that arid desert soil is rich in actinomycetes and
genetic diversity corresponding to two secondary metabolite
biosynthetic classes, the nonribosomal peptide synthetases
(NRPS) and polyketide synthases (PKS).4
Here, we describe the structures, biosynthesis, antimicrobial
activity and self-resistance mechanism of the aridomycins,
novel polyketides serendipitously discovered in two geo-
graphically distant Amycolatopsis sp. isolated from North
American desert soil. Aridomycin B is a linear, long carbon-
chain, polyhydroxylated tetramic acid and aridomycin A is its
glucosylated analog. Whole genome sequencing revealed a
large hybrid NRPS/PKS pathway for the molecules that was
validated by biochemical characterization of AriGT, a
glycosyltransferase in the cluster that converts aridomycin B
into aridomycin A. A new acyltransferase (AT) specificity

■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
As part of our antimicrobial discovery efforts, we identified two
desert soil-derived actinomycete strains that inhibited the
growth of methicilin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus and the
emerging fungal pathogen Candida auris. 16S rRNA sequences
placed both strains, GA6-03 and F10, in the genus
Amycolatopsis spp. (Supporting Information). Fractionation
of liquid cultures and analysis by liquid chromatography−mass
spectrometry (LC-MS) revealed that the same major
compounds were present in both strains (Figure 1A). High
resolution electrospray ionization MS (HR-ESI-MS) of the
compounds showed [M-H]−ions of 1354.7384 and 1192.6842
(Figure S1), neither of which had matches in SciFinder or the
natural products databases Antibase and Natural Products
Atlas.5,6 Fragmentation patterns in LC-HR-MS/MS spectra
indicated the two compounds had similar structures differing
by a hexose unit (C6H10O5, calcd neutral molecular mass
162.053, Figure 1B,C).

Aridomycin Biosynthesis. To date, no biosynthetic
pathways have been assigned to the aridomycins, blasticidin
A or the aflastatins. Given the structural novelty of these
compounds, we sought to identify the BGC responsible for
aridomycin production, which would also enable bioinformatic
prediction of the absolute configurations of individual
stereocenters.20,21 High quality, contiguous genomes for strains
GA6-03 and F10 were obtained using Pac-Bio long-read
sequencing. The genomes are 98.7% identical at the nucleotide
level22 (Figure S6). AntiSMASH v723 analysis identified 32
biosynthetic regions in GA6−03 and 33 in F10 (Tables S7, S8
and Figure S7). Based on their structural features, the
aridomycins are predicted to be assembled by a Type I cis-
AT polyketide synthase (PKS) pathway.20 Among the PKS-
containing regions, only region 5 in GA6−03 and region 19 in
F10 contained enough PKS modules to account for aridomycin
biosynthesis. These two clusters share 98.8% nucleotide
identity and have identical gene content and organization,
allowing us to analyze them as a single BGC using antiSMASH,
the MiBIG repository,24 and manual annotation.
The aridomycin BGC, designated ari, spans 139 kb and
encodes a hybrid PKS-NRPS assembly line (Figure 4A). It
contains 31 open reading frames whose functions were
predicted based on sequence homology (Table S9). The ari
BGC is organized into four major regions corresponding to
precursor biosynthesis, PKS-catalyzed polyketide synthesis,
NRPS-catalyzed tetramic acid formation, and glycosylation. In
addition, it includes hypothetical proteins, putative tran-
scription factors, membrane proteins, and enzymes such as
thioesterases whose precise functions remain unassigned but
are likely involved in gene regulation, transport, and editing,
respectively.
Domain organization within each module, combined with
AT domain analysis (Figure 4B) is consistent with the NMR-
derived planar structures of 1−3. Biosynthesis initiates with the
loading of a methylmalonyl-CoA starter unit and its
subsequent decarboxylation by a loading KSQ domain (Figure
5).20 Modules 1 and 2 of ariA generate the triene moiety.
Although they include two double bond-forming domains,
three double bonds are present in the final product, suggesting
that either module 1 or 2 is used iteratively.25 The remaining
portion of the hydrophobic tail is constructed by ariB, which
contains DH and ER domains that are inactive (Figures S11
and 12).
Modules 4−6 of ariD install three ketide units. Notably, the
KR domain in module 4 is inactive (Figure 5, module 4),
leaving a ketone that can undergo nucleophilic attack by the C-
15 hydroxyl group introduced in module 6 to form a
tetrahydropyran ring. Because the ari BGC lacks genes
associated with tetrahydropyran formation,26,27 we propose
that this cyclization occurs nonenzymatically and note that an
aridomycin analog with opposite stereochemistry at C-15 was
never observed.28,29 Interestingly, both blasticidin A and
aridomycins possess identical tetrahydropyran moieties, as
unambiguously assigned by NMR. To further support this
hypothesis, we examined the genome of Streptomyces
griseochromogenes ATCC 14511 (also known as IFO 13413;
GenBank accession no. NZ_CP016279.1), a producer of
blasticidin A. AntiSMASH analysis identified a single
biosynthetic region with sufficient PKS modules to account
for blasticidin A production, which also includes genes
involved in hydroxymalonyl-ACP biosynthesis (Figure S13
and Table S10).12,30,31 Modules 6−8 of the blasticidin A BGC
are predicted to generate the same carbonyl- and tetraol-
containing polyketide intermediate as seen in aridomycin, and
this cluster similarly lacks genes for known enzymatic routes to
tetrahydropyran formation (Figure S13 and Table S10). It
remains unclear whether the tetrahydropyran ring closure
occurs while the intermediates are tethered to the PKS or after
product release. We also cannot exclude the possibility that a
domain in module 4, showing homology to dehydratase-like
(DHt) domains found in trans-AT PKS systems, plays a role in
this transformation.
The protein products of ariE through ariI (modules 7−21)
are responsible for constructing the extended polyol chain of
aridomycins A and B, spanning carbons C-17 to C-47. Three
dehydratase (DH) domains, found in modules 9, 14, and 16
(open circles in Figure 5), appear to be inactive despite
containing the conserved catalytic His and Asp residues
(Figure S11). The final PKS module encoded by ariI is missing
an acyltransferase (AT) domain, suggesting that another AT
domain in the pathway performs trans-loading of the required
malonyl extender unit. The tetramic acid moiety is derived
from alanine, which is incorporated by a nonribosomal peptide
synthetase (NRPS) and cyclized by a TrdC-type Dieckmann
cyclase, ariJ.32 The BGC contains two NRPS genes, ariC and
ariK, either or both of which may participate in this step
(module 22). Although ariK is colinear with the PKS genes, it
lacks a functional adenylation (A) domain required for amino
acid loading. In contrast, ariC, encoded upstream in the cluster,
contains an A domain but lacks a condensation (C) domain,
and its A domain specificity could not be confidently predicted.
Tetramic acid formation and release of the full-length
polyketide chain from the terminal peptidyl carrier protein
(PCP) domain are catalyzed by ariJ. N-methylation, required
to yield aridomycin B, is carried out by the methyltransferase
ariMT. This pathway also explains the biosynthetic origin of
aridomycin A: ariGT, a member of the macrolide glycosyl-
transferase (MGT) family, is encoded in the cluster (Table
S7), along with ariGH, a glycoside hydrolase. Together, the

Bioinformatic Predictions of the Absolute Configuration.
We next applied bioinformatic analyses to assign the absolute
configurations of ketide fragments whose relative stereo-
chemistry had been determined by NMR, and for which the
absolute configurations of the corresponding PKS extender
units are known. Stereochemical predictions based on the
specificity of KR,21,33,34 DH,35 and ER36 domains, along with
the known absolute configurations of the PKS extender units
selected by AT domains, have been well established and
validated in prior studies.37,38 For example, (2S)-methylma-
lonyl-CoA is commonly incorporated by AT domains
containing the Q/YASH motif. Similarly, recent studies on
ansamitocin biosynthesis have shown that the AT domains are
specific for (2S)-methoxymalonyl-ACP.39 In the case of
aridomycins, the absolute configuration of 2-hydroxymalonyl-
ACPincorporated in ari modules 4, 5, 7, 8, and 17has not
been experimentally determined.40 However, the presence of
the genes zmaN, zmaE, zmaD, and zmaG, which produce (2S)-
methoxymalonyl-ACP in other pathways (Figure S8) strongly
suggests that the analogous product here is (2S)-hydrox-
ymalonyl-ACP.
Of the 19 catalytically active KR domains (Figure S10),
those in modules 6−8 are A1-type, producing L-configured
hydroxyl groups at C-15, C-17, and C-19 (corresponding to 3S
within the ketide extender unit). The remaining 16 KRs all
contain the LDD motif and were assigned as B1-type. Notably,
the KR in module 4 lacks the catalytic Tyr residue and is
inactive, resulting in a preserved ketone that facilitates
tetrahydropyran formation, as discussed earlier. Interestingly,
modules 9, 14, and 16 each incorporate (2S)-methylmalonyl-
CoA and contain DH domains. However, NMR data and
molecular formulas clearly show the presence of 2-methyl-3-
hydroxy substituents at the corresponding positions (C21/
C22, C31/C32 and C35/C36). The relative configuration at
these positions was unambiguously assigned using Kishi’s
Universal NMR Database 4 (Figure 3A), revealing epimeriza-
tion at C22, C32 and C36. This observation suggests that in
addition to being catalytically inactive, the DH domains in
modules 9, 14, and 16 may epimerize the KS-derived (2R)-
methyl ketone intermediates to their (2S) epimers. Such
epimerizing DH domains have been described in other BGCs
and typically co-occur with B1-type KRs.41
Bioinformatic analysis of the KR and DH domains predicted
that all alkenes in the aridomycins adopt the E configuration,
consistent with the NMR data. The two active enoylreductase
(ER) domains in the pathwaylocated in modules 18 and
19exhibit sequence features characteristic of L-type ERs
(Figure S12), suggesting that they reduce a (2S)-methyl
intermediate and produce the stereochemistry depicted in the
structure of aridomycin B (Figure 5, bottom). Only one
stereocenter in the NMR-determined structure, namely C-14,
did not align with the bioinformatic predictions. This
discrepancy is observed in both the aridomycin and blasticidin
A pathways. Whether this results from unexpected stereo-
chemistry of the hydroxymalonyl-ACP extender unit, a
stereochemical inversion during tetrahydropyran formation,
or another mechanism remains unclear and warrants future
investigation.
Multiple sequence alignments of the ari AT domains
revealed that domains that incorporate hydroxymalonyl-ACP
(modules 4, 5, 7, 8 and 17) contain a previously unreported
specificity motif, Q/FVAH (Figures 4B and S18).42,43 Analysis
of the AT domains of the blasticidin A BGC showed the
presence of the same FVAH motif in modules that install 1,2-
diol-containing ketide units (modules 6, 7, 9, 10 and 19;
Figures S14 and S15). To further probe this observation, we
searched the BIGFAM database for AT domain sequences
within a PKS that contained a GHSQ···FVAH motif. We
limited the search to 500 sequences from single PKS modules
and tabulated the top 30 (Table S11). Multiple sequence
alignments depicted in a Seqlogo plot show high conservation
(Figure S19). Moreover, this motif was present in PKS-AT
domains of many other BGCs, including those from other
actinobacterial genera such as Sciscionella, Kitasatospora and
Kibdelosporangium. Conservation of the FVAH motif in other
PKS BGCs suggest that the motif could be important for AT-
substrate specificity and not only specific to biosynthesis of
aridomycins and blasticidin A. Notably, this motif is distinct
from those identified in the BGCs of other 1,2-diol containing
compounds such as zwittermycin A, which features a GAAH
sequence in motif 2.40,44

■GLUCOSYLATION-MEDIATED RESISTANCE IN
ARIDOMYCIN BIOSYNTHESIS
Aridomycin B inhibited a range of bacterial and fungal
pathogens with MICs from 2 to 16 μg/mL, including Candida
auris which is classified as an urgent threat by the Centers for
Disease Control48 (Table 1). Interestingly, the glucosylated
analog aridomycin A (1), was inactive against all tested
organisms. Aridomycin B was equally effective against drug-
sensitive and drug-resistant strains, including methicillin-
resistant S. aureus, vancomycin-resistant E. faecalis (VRE),
and amphotericin B-resistant C. albicans. Time-kill assays
showed rapid bactericidal and fungicidal activity, with
aridomycin B reducing S. aureus and C. albicans inocula by 3
log units within 2 h at concentrations slightly above the MIC
(Figure S16), outperforming the control drugs oxacillin and
amphotericin B. Aridomycin B showed moderate cytotoxicity
against a human cell line (HEK293s), with an IC50 value of
99.1 ± 7 μM while aridomycin A was inactive.
The potent activity of the aglycone aridomycin B, complete
inactivity of aridomycin A, and the presence of glycosyl-
transferase (ariGT) and glycoside hydrolase (ariGH) genes in
the ari BGC suggest a self-resistance mechanism similar to
those used by macrolide-producing actinomycetes, such as
Streptomyces antibioticus, an oleandomycin producer.1,49,50 In
these systems, antibiotics are inactivated by glycosylation and
reactivated after export via secreted glycoside hydrolases.
Consistent with this model, AriGH carries an N-terminal
secretion signal.51 Growth curve experiments with GA6−03
and F10 showed that aridomycin A levels peaked early and
declined as aridomycin B levels rose (Figure 6A), indicating in
situ conversion of the inactive glycoside to the active aglycone.
Supporting this, aridomycin B (2) inhibited both producer
strains with an MIC of 8 μg/mL, whereas aridomycin A (1)
showed no activity (Table 1).

erythromycin and tylosin (Figure 6C and Figure S17). Each
compound was incubated with AriGT and UDP-Glc, and

while functionally similar to macrolide glycosyltransferases, is

both aridomycin B and its glycosylated derivative, aridomycin
A.
Intrigued by the role of glycosylation as a self-resistance
mechanism in natural product biosynthesis, we used
bioinformatics to query the MIBiG database for BGCs that
encode both a glycosyltransferase and a glycoside hydrolase.
Surprisingly, the search returned only four distinct secondary
metabolites (eight MiBiG entries): erythromycin, methymy-
cin/pikromycin, chalcomycin/aldgamycin, and lankamycin, all
of which are macrolide antibiotics (Table S12). Notably, each
of these macrolides contains two sugar units on the
macrocyclic core, and in each case, self-resistance involves
glycosylation of one sugar to form a transient disaccharide. In
two of these pathways, the number of glycosyltransferases
corresponds to the number of sugars present in the final
compound. For example, erythromycin is diglycosylated and its
BGC encodes two glycosyltransferases, EryBV and EryCIII.
Methymycin/pikromycin, which is monoglycosylated with
desosamine, contains a single glycosyltransferase specific for
that sugar. Lankamycin on the other hand is a diglycosylated
macrolide whose BGC encodes three glycosyltransferasesan
arrangement reminiscent of oleandomycin biosynthesisand
may represent an unexplored example of glycosylation-
mediated self-resistance. This analysis suggests that antibiotic
inactivation via glycosylation is relatively rare. To our
knowledge, the aridomycins represent the first example of
self-resistance involving glycosylation in a nonmacrolide
polyketide.
■DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS
The actinomycete-derived aridomycins reported here are
among the most structurally complex polyketides characterized
to date. Their strong antibacterial and antifungal activity,
combined with a temporally controlled self-resistance mech-
anism involving glycosylation, motivated a detailed inves-
tigation into their biosynthesis, structural assignment and self-
resistance strategy. Through a combination of extensive NMR
analysis, whole genome sequencing and bioinformatic
annotation, we determined the complete structure and
absolute configuration of the aridomycins. Of 28 total
stereocenters, only the C-14 center located in the tetrahy-
dropyran ring differs between experimental and bioinformatic
predictions of KR and AT domain specificity. Interestingly, the
experimental determination agrees with the aflastatin A and
blasticidin A structures (Figure S5), both of which also contain
tetrahydropyran rings and 1,2-diol motifs derived from
hydroxymalonyl-ACP. In this study we also identified the
previously uncharacterized BGC for blasticidin A in the
genome of S. griseochromogenes ATCC 14511. Comparative
analysis of this cluster with the ari BGC revealed key
biosynthetic parallels, including modules that support for-
mation of the tetrahydropyran ring and shared AT domain
motifs, notably the FVAH sequence associated with hydrox-
ymalonyl-ACP incorporation (Figure S18). These insights not
only validate our structural assignments but also expand the
toolbox for predicting polyketide stereochemistry from
biosynthetic gene sequences.
All antibiotic-producing microbes must avoid poisoning
themselves, and they have evolved diverse strategies to do so
including efflux pumps, target modification, and the production
of inactive prodrugs. In this work, we uncovered a rare and
elegant self-resistance mechanism used by aridomycin-
producing strains: reversible glucosylation of the antibiotic
scaffold. The glycosyltransferase AriGT inactivates aridomycin
by adding a glucose moiety, while the glycoside hydrolase
AriGH, predicted to be secreted, removes the sugar to
regenerate the active compound, likely during or after export
(Figure 7). This form of chemical “masking” has been reported
previously only in a few macrolide antibiotics. Here, we show it
also occurs in a linear polyketide, expanding the scope of
known self-resistance strategies. Interestingly, AriGT is highly
specific for aridomycin and does not act on known macrolides,
such as tylosin or erythromycin. The exact target of aridomycin
remains unknown, but glucosylation could prevent binding by
altering the molecule’s conformation or physical properties.
