ABSTRACT: Bispyrrolidinoindoline-diketopiperazines (BPI-DKP) alkaloids, characterized by
a complex 3a,3a′-bispyrrolidino[2,3-b]indoline scaffold, constitute a large class of synthetically
challenging bioactive pyrroloindoline natural products. Their biosynthesis, mediated by P450
enzymes, involves oxidative dimerization of diketopiperazines (DKPs), yet the underlying
mechanistic and stereochemical details have remained unclear. Here, we elucidate the
molecular mechanism of TtpB1, a P450 dimerase that stereoselectively couples two DKP units
to form C2-symmetric BPI scaffolds through an unexpected cascade. Contrary to the prevailing
double-radical coupling hypothesis, integrated structural, biochemical, and computational
studies reveal a stepwise process: (i) Compound I-mediated generation of a nitrogen-centered
DKP radical, triggering cyclization to a C3-radical pyrroloindoline intermediate; (ii) radical
addition to the C3′ position of a second DKP; and (iii) single-electron transfer (SET)-driven
cyclization to construct the second pyrroloindoline unit. This dual N-centered radical- and
SET-driven cyclization showcases unprecedented versatility in natural product dimer
biosynthesis. Molecular dynamics and QM/MM calculations further demonstrate how substrate conformational dynamics enforce
stereochemical fidelity, yielding the C2-symmetric BPI-DKP core with vicinal quaternary stereocenters. These findings provide the
first structure-guided mechanistic elucidation of BPI-DKP synthase, laying the groundwork for rational enzyme engineering and
biomimetic synthesis of these pharmacologically important alkaloids.
■INTRODUCTION
Bispyrrolidino[2,3-b]indoline (BPI) alkaloids, a pharmacolog-
ically significant class of pyrroloindoline natural products, are
widely distributed in bacteria, fungi, and plants. They have
attracted extensive pharmacological interest due to their
notable biological activities, including cytotoxic, antimicrobial,
and antitumor effects.1−8 Structurally, these compounds
feature a sterically congested 3a,3a′-bispyrrolidino[2,3-b]-
indoline core, formed via C3a-C3a′ head-to-head dimerization
of cyclotryptamine units (Figures 1a and S1). The BPI-
diketopiperazine (DKP) subclass is structurally distinguished
by two tryptophan-containing DKP-derived moieties and
predominantly contains an intriguing C2- symmetric BPI core
(e.g., tetratryptomycin A (1),9 (−)-ditryptophenaline,10
(−)-dibrevianamide F,11 and (+)-win 6482112 (Figure 1a).1
This framework poses significant synthetic challenges due to its
vicinal all-carbon quaternary stereocenters, driving the
development of innovative synthetic strategies.1,13−17
Emerging biosynthetic research reveals nature′s efficient
strategies for assembling complex BPI scaffolds with precise
regio- and stereochemical control. Notably, among diverse BPI
alkaloids, only BPI-DKPs have been confirmed to be
biosynthesized via P450-catalyzed oxidative DKP dimerization,
with only a few of such enzymes characterized to date.9,11,18−20
Fungal P450 enzymes, notably DtpC and its homologues,
represent the first characterized systems for C3−C3′ coupling
and pyrroloindoline formation in (−)-ditryptophenaline
(Figure 1a) biosynthesis.11 However, the inherent mem-
brane-bound nature of these fungal proteins has impeded
detailed mechanistic studies. The recent discovery of TtpB1,9
the first bacterial P450 mediating regio- and stereospecific
cyclo-L-tryptophanyl-L-tryptophanyl DKP (cWW, 2) dimeriza-
tion to 1 (Figure 1b), expands both the enzymatic repertoire
for BPI biosynthesis and opportunities for mechanistic
investigations. Furthermore, additional bacterial asymmetric
DKP dimerases, including NzeB (synonym NasF5053),21−23
NascB,24 NasbB (synonym NasB),22,25 and AspB (synonym
NasS1868),22,25 catalyze the formation of various asymmetric
dimers structurally related to BPI alkaloids, including
pyrroloindoline-containing DKP dimers such as naseseazines

and other dimers (Figure S2). Despite these advances, the
precise molecular mechanisms governing the formation of the
intriguing BPI scaffold remain poorly understood.
The prevailing model of BPI-DKP biosynthesis proposes
that the process proceeds through the coupling of two
pyrroloindoline C3-radicals via a P450-catalyzed diradical
mechanism (Figure 1b).9,18,20 The formation of the first
pyrroloindoline may initiate through alternative hydrogen
abstraction pathwayseither at the indole N−H or the DKP
N−H position. The second pyrroloindoline radical intermedi-
ate is thought to form similarly, possibly via Compound II,
followed by radical coupling to complete the assembly of the
BPI scaffold. Although chemical synthesis can be achieved
through diradical coupling,14,15,17 fundamental gaps remain in
understanding the enzymatic process: (i) how the two
pyrroloindoline units are formed, and (ii) what governs the
stereochemistry at the adjacent quaternary centers. Addressing
these unresolved issues is critical for understanding the
mechanism of BPI formation and the catalytic principles of
P450 dimerases.
In this study, we present the first molecular insights into
BPI-forming P450 catalysis. Our findings reveal an unprece-
dented radical-initiated cascade for BPI scaffold assembly,
challenging the prevailing diradical coupling model. Through
structural, biochemical, and computational analyses, we
identify key active-site residues and delineate a multistep
pathway in which dynamic substrate conformations guide the
stereoselective formation of the BPI scaffold. Remarkably, a
single P450 enzyme employs two distinct cyclization modes to
generate both pyrroloindoline moieties. These findings
redefine the catalytic principles of P450 dimerases and
significantly expand our understanding of the enzymatic
strategies for assembling complex dimeric natural products.

■RESULTS
Functional Characterization of TtpB1. In the course of
our independent investigation into diketopiperazine (DKP)
dimerases derived from bacterial genomes,24 we also function-
ally characterized the TtpB1 gene cluster.9 Heterologous
expression of the cyclodipeptide synthase (CDPS) TtpA1 in
Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) confirmed that it mediated the
production of cWW (2) (Figure S3). Subsequent feeding
experiments involving 2 inMycobacterium smegmatis MC2 155
strain26 expressing TtpB1 led to the isolation and NMR-based
structural determination of the enzymatic product 1 (Figure
S4), thereby confirming the functional activity of TtpB1.
For biochemical characterization, recombinant TtpB1 was
purified (Figure S5), and its activity was successfully
reconstituted in vitro using a spinach-derived redox system
consisting of ferredoxin, ferredoxin reductase (Fd/FdR), and
NADPH (Figure 2a). The enzyme’s kinetic parameters were
determined to be Km = 0.178 ± 0.05 mM and kcat = 0.308 ±
0.33 min−1 (Figure S6), which are similar to the values
reported by Li et al.9 To assess substrate specificity, we tested
an additional 23 tryptophan-containing DKPs, including four
halogenated cWW analogs (Figure S7). TtpB1 exhibited
activity exclusively toward four halogenated cWW derivatives
(5-F-cWW; 7-F-cWW; 5,5′-F-cWW; 6,6′-Cl-cWW), distin-
guishing them from other tested DKPs (Figure S7). Structural
analysis confirmed that the dimerization product of 6,6′-Cl-
cWW (3), compound 4, maintains the same BPI framework as
the native product 1 (Figure 2a). These findings suggest that
TtpB1 has a strict preference for the cWW core structure but
can accommodate specific halogen modifications.
TtpB1 Utilizes Cpd I as a Putative Oxidant. Recent
studies suggested that certain P450 enzymes involved in the
formation of asymmetric DKP dimers, such as AspB (NasS1868)
and NzeB (NasF5053) (Figure S2), may utilize ferric-superoxide
as their primary oxidizing species.27 This represents a
departure from the conventional P450 catalytic mechanism
that employs Compound I (Cpd I) as the primary oxidant.28,29
Interestingly, these enzymes lack the conserved acidic residue
(Asp/Glu) in the distal pocket of the I-helix (Figure 2b),
which is essential for proton transfer to the ferric peroxo
intermediate during the canonical formation of Cpd I in
canonical P450 catalysis (Figures 2c and S8).30−33 However, it
remains unclear whether this alternative mechanism applies to
other DKP P450 dimerases.
A comparative sequence analysis revealed that, unlike
asymmetric DKP dimerases of AspB, NzeB (NasF5053) and
NascB, the TtpB1 retains the conserved P450 “acid-alcohol
pair” motif, with glutamate at position 241 and serine at
position 242 (E241 and S242) (Figure 2b). Site-directed
mutagenesis of S242 to alanine, or E241 to alanine or
asparagine resulted in a significant decrease in catalytic activity
(Figure 2d), confirming the importance of this motif for

Asymmetric Substrate-Binding Modes in the TtpB1-
Substrate Complex. To investigate the molecular basis of
substrate recognition and catalysis by TtpB1, we conducted
crystallographic analyses of its complex with the native cWW
(2) substrate. Despite extensive efforts, only a monoliganded
complex was obtained at 2.50 Å resolution (PDB ID: 9W5T)
(Figure 3a), which did not fully capture the authentic
bisubstrate-binding conformation. This limitation was ad-
dressed by using halogenated cWW analogs, with compound
3 enabling successful cocrystallization at 2.48 Å resolution
(PDB ID: 9W0A) (Figure 3b). The resulting structure
revealed a well-defined dual-substrate complex, with both
substrate molecules clearly resolved in the electron density
maps (Figure 3b,c). A comparative structural analysis between
the TtpB1-2 (one substrate) and TtpB1-3 (two substrates)
complexes showed nearly identical substrate-binding con-
formations (Figure S9, RMSD: 0.327 Å), suggesting that
halogenation at the C6 does not significantly alter the native
binding mode of cWW (2).
Contrary to the initial hypothesis that both substrates would
adopt identical conformations to form symmetric pyrroloindo-
line units, the structural data revealed distinct binding modes
for the two 3 molecules within the TtpB1-substrate complex.
Specifically, the TtpB1-3 complex displayed asymmetric
binding of the two molecules of compound 3: Sub1 adopts a
U-shaped conformation, interacting with residues G87, A237,
L175, and W189 (Figure 3d), a conformation likely critical for
the cyclization step. In contrast, Sub2 assumes an extended
conformation stabilized by hydrophobic interactions with
residues including P11, F80, G177, G287, G288, P290, and
I390 (Figure 3d). This asymmetric binding model suggests
that the two pyrroloindoline units are unlikely formed via the
speculated C3-diradical coupling pathway.
Furthermore, TtpB1 shares approximately 40% sequence
identity with the asymmetric DKP dimerases NzeB (NasF5053)
and AspB (NasS1868),21,22 which maintain a similar substrate-
binding geometry (Figure S10). Both of these asymmetric
DKP dimerases exhibit dual functionality, allowing precise
control over radical coupling reactions while preserving strict
stereospecificity. This structural conservation suggests that
TtpB1 may employ a similar radical cascade mechanism to
construct at least one pyrroloindoline unit, with the ability to
control radical coupling and stereospecific catalysis to form the
C3−C3′ connection.

Cpd I Species Initiates DKP N12−H Abstraction for
the First Pyrroloindoline Formation. To elucidate the
mechanism underlying the TtpB1-catalyzed dimerization
reaction, a combination of molecular dynamics (MD)
simulations and quantum mechanics/molecular mechanics
(QM/MM) calculations was performed under the Cpd I
state. Initially, 200 ns MD simulations were carried out (Figure
S11) to characterize the binding modes of the two substrates.
Consistent with prior investigations of other DKP dimerases,
two predominant binding conformations of Sub1 were
identified during the MD simulations (Figure S12). In
conformation a (Conf-a), the DKP N12−H forms a hydrogen
bond with Cpd I (Figure S12a). Additionally, in Conf-a, Sub1
engages in hydrogen-bonding interactions with residues
Ala237 and Phe80, as well as hydrophobic contacts with
Leu175 and Trp189. Site-directed mutagenesis of A237G,
F80A, L175A, W189F, and W189A, along with combinatorial
double mutants, resulted in a substantial reduction in catalytic
activity (Figure 3e), indicating the critical role of these residues
in stabilizing Sub1 in the Conf-a conformation. Notably, the
W189F variant retained relatively high catalytic efficiency,
suggesting that optimal substrate stabilization necessitates a
balance between steric effects and aromatic π−π interactions.
In an alternative conformation b (Conf-b), Sub1 adopts a
configuration wherein its indole N1−H forms a hydrogen
bond with the Cpd I species, resembling the conformation
observed in the crystal structure (Figure S12b). Given that
both binding modes were sampled during the MD simulations,
both were incorporated into subsequent mechanistic analyses.
Recent structural and computational studies of related
asymmetric DKP dimerases, like NzeB (NasF5053), NascB and
PtmB, have suggested that the Cpd I species preferentially
targets the DKP N12−H position rather than the indole N1−
H to initiate the hydrogen atom abstraction (HAA) in
asymmetric DKP dimerases.36,38,39 To evaluate whether this
mechanistic preference extends to BPI-DKP formation, we first
explored the feasibility of an N1-initiated pathway, wherein
Cpd I abstracts the hydrogen from the indole N1−H bond to
generate a pyrroloindoline intermediate.
Our QM/MM study suggests that HAA from the indole
N1−H group by Cpd I proceeds with a modest energy barrier
of 12.5 kcal/mol (Figure S13), yielding the intermediate Int1c,
which is 1.8 kcal/mol higher in energy relative to the reactant
complex (RCc). Population analysis reveals that the radical
generated in Int1c is delocalized over the indole ring of Sub1,
with significant spin population localized at C3 (−0.46) and
N1 (−0.24). Emerging from the Int1c intermediate, four
potential reaction pathways were explored (Figure S14);
however, all were found to be inaccessible kinetically for
further cyclization (Figure S15). Additionally, the N1-initiated
pathway requires multiple proton transfers (Figure 1b), yet no
suitable proton-shuttling residues are positioned near Sub1 to
facilitate these steps (Figure 3d). Even assuming indole
nitrogen (N1•) protonation, precedent from asymmetric
DKP dimerases indicates that the resulting protonated indole
radical remains inert toward both cyclization and intermo-
lecular coupling.21,39 Consequently, HAA from the substrate
indole N1−H group leads to a dead-end intermediate (Int1c)
incapable of further transformation, consistent with prior
theoretical studies toward asymmetric DKP dimerases.21,39
Concurrently, building upon previously proposed catalytic
mechanisms observed in asymmetric DKP dimerases,36,38,39 we

Intermolecular C3−C3′ Bond Formation Occurs
before the Second Pyrroloindoline Cyclization. Regard-
ing subsequent catalysis, prior studies widely proposed that
independent cyclization of Sub2 by Cpd II could generate a
second pyrroloindoline radical intermediate.9,18,20 To test this
hypothesis, we first evaluated the Cpd II-mediated HAA from
DKP-NH within Int2a. However, the calculated energy barriers
derived from the scanned energy profile exceed 37.4 kcal/mol,
indicating that this pathway is highly unfavorable kinetically
(Figure S16a). The extended conformation of Sub2 positions
both N1−H′ and N12−H′ ≥8.0 Å from the heme center
(Figure S16b), making direct cyclization at these sites
improbable. Notably, the shorter distance (5.0 Å) between
C3 and C3′ suggests their bond formation occurs prior to
cyclization, challenging the proposed two independent
cyclization mechanism.
Beginning with intermediate Int2a, the intermolecular C3−
C3′ coupling between the pyrroloindoline radical and Sub2
was investigated. As illustrated in Figures 4 and S17, this
process (Int2a →TS3a) encounters a substantial energy barrier
of 33.7 kcal/mol relative to RCa, indicating that direct
intramolecular N12−C2 coupling within Int1a is unlikely to
facilitate the dimerization reaction.
Inspired by previous studies on asymmetric DKP dimerases
and other DKP-related P450 enzymes,22,39−41 we assumed the
DKP radical (Int1a) may undergo the conformational change
to facilitate the subsequent dimerization. To explore this
possibility, the rotation of the dihedral angle C2−C3−C10−
C11 was examined using enhanced umbrella sampling. As
depicted in Figure S18a, such a rotation process encounters an
energy barrier of 8.9 kcal/mol, resulting in an alternative
conformation that is 6.2 kcal/mol higher in energy than the
initial state. In this new conformation, the Sub1 radical is
stabilized through hydrogen bonding interactions with
surrounding residues, including Ala237 and Phe80 (Figure
S18b). Additionally, the dihedral angle increases to 61.0°,
facilitating N12-mediated radical addition to the C2 position
(indole Si-face), ultimately generating the S-configured
pyrroloindoline radical intermediate. To further assess the
stability of the Sub1 radical in this “Si” conformation, fully
relaxed MD simulations were conducted over 200 ns,
demonstrating system stability with a backbone atom root-
mean-square deviation (RMSD) below 1.5 Å (Figure S19a).
Figure 5a illustrates the QM/MM-calculated relative energy
profile for the dimerization reaction initiated from the “Si”
conformation (Int1b) of the Sub1 radical. In the Int1b
intermediate, the spin density is primarily localized on the
N12 atom (−0.75). From this state, the N12 atom attacks the
Si-face of the C2C3 double bond, overcoming an energy
barrier of 21.5 kcal/mol to yield Int2b. This step is exothermic,
with an energy release of 8.4 kcal/mol (Int1b →Int2b). The
C2 atom retains the S stereochemistry in Int2b, consistent with
the stereochemical configuration observed in the final product
at this position. Within the newly formed Int2b intermediate,
the C3 site exhibits the highest spin density (−0.62), which
facilitates the subsequent intermolecular C3−C3′ bond
formation. However, the direct formation of the C3−C3′
bond from Int2b is highly unfavorable kinetically, requiring an
exceptionally high activation barrier exceeding 77.0 kcal/mol
(Figure S20), primarily due to steric constraints arising from
the extended C3−C3′ distance of 8.58 Å.
To address this, additional MD simulations (Figure S21a)
were performed to relax the active site conformation of Int2b,
resulting in a new intermediate, Int2b′. In Int2b′, the
pyrroloindoline radical intermediate engages in π−π stacking
interactions with the indole ring of Sub2, reducing the C3−C3′
bond distance to 4.02 Å (Figure S21b). Starting from Int2b′,
QM/MM calculations show that the C3−C3′ coupling
proceeds with a significantly lower energy barrier of 20.6
kcal/mol (Int2b′ →TS3b). This step is endothermic by 5.3
kcal/mol (Int2b′ →Int3b). Our findings suggest that the 2S-
pyrroloindoline intermediate (Int2b′) is competent for
subsequent coupling, unlike its 2R-configured counterpart
(Int2a, Figure 4). Both thermodynamic and kinetic analyses
reveal a strong preference for Int2b′ in facilitating the C3−C3′
coupling step. MD simulations further demonstrate that after
Sub1 forms the initial pyrroloindoline radical intermediate
(Int2b), Sub2 adopts a conformation prohibitive to independ-
ent cyclization. Notably, the O1 atom of Cpd II maintains
average distances of 7.72 Å (from N1′−H) and 10.24 Å (from
N12′−H), while the C3−C3′ distance remains significantly
shorter (4.30 Å) (Figure S21). Together, these structural and
energetic insights unequivocally support C3−C3′ radical
addition as the dominant pathway for DKP−DKP linkage
formation after initial cyclization, being both thermodynami-
cally favorable and kinetically accessible.
SET-Triggered Cyclization for the Second Pyrroloin-
doline Formation. Thereafter, we found that Int3b can
undergo a spontaneous electron transfer, during which the
electron shifts from the substrate radical to the Fe(IV)−OH
moiety, affording the substrate cation and Fe(III)−OH species
within Int4b. This process is thermodynamically favorable, with
an exothermicity of 9.2 kcal/mol (Int3b→Int4b). To evaluate
alternative pathways, we examined an OH-rebound mechanism
for hemiaminal formation but found it energetically unfavor-
able (>30.7 kcal/mol barrier) due to the excessive O1−C2′
distance (6.60 Å) in Int3b (Figure S22). These results
collectively support the electron transfer pathway. In Int3b,
the spin population on Fe and C2 is 1.71 and −0.50,
respectively (Figure 5a). In Int4b, the spin population on Fe
and C2 changes to 1.00 and 0.00 (Table S1), respectively.
These support the electron transfer from the substrate radical
to the iron center (Figures S23 and S24).
Starting from Int4b, we assume that the substrate cation may
lose the proton from the NH+ group. Thus, MD simulations
were conducted on Int4b to identify possible proton receptor
near the indole N1′−H′ cation group (Figure S25a). Notably,
these MD simulations reveal that the indole N1′−H moiety of
Sub2 can form a proton channel with a water molecule and
Glu315 (Figure S25), suggesting that Glu315 can act as a base
to mediate the deprotonation process. Starting from the MD-
derived structure Int4b′ (Figure 5a), QM/MM calculations
indicate that the water-assisted proton transfer from N1′−H is
favorable both kinetically and thermodynamically. This process
involves an activation barrier of 10.9 kcal/mol (Int4b′ →TS4b)
and an exothermicity of 5.6 kcal/mol (Int4b′ →Int5b). Indeed,
site-directed mutagenesis of the putative general base Glu315
to alanine or glutamine nearly abolishes catalytic activity
(Figure 5b), underscoring the critical role of Glu315 in proton

examined whether Glu315 could abstract a proton from the
DKP NH group during nucleophilic attack on CNH+.
However, energy profile scans revealed an insurmountable
barrier (>79.4 kcal/mol), ruling out this mechanism (Figure

energetically prohibitive, with a barrier exceeding 50.3 kcal/

neither pathway is viable, reinforcing Glu315 as the exclusive
proton acceptor in this catalytic step.
The final cyclization step of Int5b can be facilitated by the
general acid−base catalyst from pH buffer via a nonenzymatic
pathway, leading to the final product 1 with correct
stereoselectivity (Figure S27). We also evaluated the
subsequent cyclization starting directly from Int4b. However,
the process exhibits a barrier exceeding 35.0 kcal/mol,
rendering this pathway kinetically prohibitive (Figure S28).
pH-dependent kinetic assays, performed to detect potential
intermediates, revealed none observable but indicated optimal
reactivity under near-neutral conditions (pH ∼7.0, Figure
S29). Notably, this cyclization strategy aligns well with
established synthetic approach for C3-substituted pyrroloindo-
lines,42,43 thereby reinforcing the validity of our computational
model.
■DISCUSSION
Inspection of Figures 4 and 5a reveals that the intermolecular
C3−C3′ bond formation from the “Si” conformation (Int2b →
TS3b, Figures 5a and S30) is significantly more favorable than
that from the “Re” conformation (Int2a →TS3a, Figure 4).
This preference is primarily attributed to the substantially
lower energy of Int2b (7.9 kcal/mol) relative to Int2a (15.6
kcal/mol). In Int2a, the dihedral angle C3−C10−C11−C16 is
measured at −119.3°, resulting in considerable ring strain
within the newly formed five-membered ring. Conversely, Int2b
exhibits a dihedral angle of −168.6°, which effectively alleviates
ring strain. Consequently, rotation around the C2−C3−C4−
C11 dihedral angle not only reduces the activation barrier for
the intermolecular C3−C3′ bond coupling between the
pyrroloindoline radical intermediate and Sub2 but also dictates
the stereoselectivity of the dimerization reaction.
Our computational study indicates that the TtpB1-catalyzed
dimerization is initiated by HAA from the diketopiperazine
N12−H atom of Sub1, corroborating previous findings.36,38,39
Notably, N12−H abstraction by Cpd I proves essential for
initial cyclization, mirroring mechanisms in asymmetric DKP
dimerases.38,39 Substrate conformational dynamics emerge as
critical for coupling efficiency, evidenced by thiotryptophan-
derived DKPs that disrupt the requisite hydrogen-bonding
network for N12-proximal alignment (Figures S31 and S32).
The intramolecular N12−C2 bond formation constitutes the
rate-limiting step (transition from Int1b to Int2b), with an

Based on our integrated structural and computational
analyses, Scheme 1 summarizes the molecular mechanism by
which P450 TtpB1 catalyzes the formation of the C2-
symmetric BPI skeleton. The transformation proceeds through
a sequential radical cascade. Initially, an N12-radical-mediated
cyclization by Cpd I forms the first pyrroloindoline radical
intermediate. This is followed by a C3−C3′ radical addition
and SET-driven cyclization, leading to the formation of the
second pyrroloindoline moiety. TtpB1 catalyzes BPI-DKP
formation through an unprecedent P450-mediated cyclization-
coupling-cyclization cascade, challenging the prevailing hy-
pothesis of diradical-mediated coupling between two in-
dependently cyclized intermediates. This reaction exemplifies
rare P450-catalyzed N-centered radical chemistry in indole
alkaloid biosynthesis.44 Unlike typical P450 mechanisms
involving indole N−H abstraction followed by radical
coupling, TtpB1 employs direct N12-radical-initiated addition
for C−C bond formation. This unconventional reactivity not
only drives DKP dimerization but also expands the scope of
P450-mediated indole alkaloid biosynthesis.44,45 These mech-
anistic insights could enable the development of novel cascade
strategies for constructing BPI-DKP frameworks.
TtpB1 represents the first structurally characterized BPI
synthase, and our study provides the first structure-guided
mechanistic insight into BPI-DKP synthase. Notably, while
P450 enzymes have been confirmed to play a role in BPI-DKP
biosynthesis among the diverse BPI alkaloids, biosynthetic
logic and gene cluster analyses strongly suggest that P450s are
likely involved in the assembly of other BPI frameworks.46−48
Our computational analyses further indicate that the TtpB1
catalytic mechanism is likely conserved in P450-mediated BPI-
DKP biosynthesis. Stereochemical diversification of the core
scaffold may result from P450-dependent conformational
control of the DKP substrate.
Furthermore, comparative structural and mechanistic
analyses of other asymmetric DKP dimerasesincluding
NzeB (NasF5053),21−23 NasbB (NasB),22,25,39 and AspB
(NasS1868)22,25,38underscore the distinctive regioselectivity
exhibited by TtpB1 in DKP dimerization. While TtpB1 shares
substrate binding conformations and catalytic mechanisms for
the formation of the first pyrroloindoline unit with these
asymmetric DKP dimerases, the primary divergence lies in the
C3−C3′ regioselectivity of the C3-pyrroloindoline radical
addition. This regioselective difference critically influences the
subsequent SET-triggered cyclization of the second pyrroloin-
doline unit. Phylogenetic analyses further corroborate evolu-
tionary relationships between BPI-DKP synthases and
asymmetric dimerases (Figure S33). Collectively, these
findings elucidate the fundamental mechanistic principles
underlying TtpB1-catalyzed assembly of symmetric BPI-DKP
scaffolds. Beyond establishing a comprehensive reaction
blueprint for this transformation, our work provides broader
insights into biosynthetic strategies employed in both
symmetric and asymmetric DKP dimer alkaloid formation.
■CONCLUSION
In summary, this study elucidates the molecular mechanism of
TtpB1-catalyzed BPI-DKP formation, revealing an unprece-
dented biosynthetic cascade that challenges prevailing
hypotheses in BPI-DKP scaffold biosynthesis. Through
structural, biochemical and computational analyses, we have
(i) clarified the previously debated catalytic mechanism,
demonstrating how the TtpB1 processes identical pyrroloindo-
line units via distinct cyclization pathways; (ii) identified a
HAA-initiated radical cascade sequence (N12−H →N12• →
C2−N12 cyclization →C3−C3′ coupling →C2′−N12′
cyclization) characterized by precise spatiotemporal regulation;
and (iii) elucidated the role of TtpB1-mediated substrate
repositioning into a Si-face attack conformation, which resolves
both kinetic and stereochemical constraints. These findings
unveil the long-standing mystery of how the symmetrical
pyrroloindoline moiety in BPI-DKP alkaloids is assembled
through TtpB1-catalyzed biosynthesis, deepen the mechanistic
understanding of BPI-DKP skeleton formation and DKP
dimerization, and provide a structural framework for the
rational engineering of BPI-DKP synthases. This could enable
the production of structurally diverse BPI-DKP scaffolds with
potential pharmaceutical applications.
