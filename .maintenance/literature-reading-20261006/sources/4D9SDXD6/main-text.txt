Uncovering the Parallel Biosynthetic Pathways of the
Cyclohexanone and Phenol Rings in Cycloheximide and Actiphenol

ABSTRACT: The eukaryotic translation inhibitor cycloheximide
(CHX) and its analogue actiphenol (APN) feature a glutarimide
moiety and a six-membered carbocyclic ring system. The
biosynthesis of CHX and APN is not yet fully understood,
particularly with respect to the mechanism of formation of the fully
reduced cyclohexanone ring in CHX and the aromatic phenol ring
in APN. In this work, a combination of gene inactivation, chemical
synthesis, and in vitro biochemical experiments highlighted an
ensemble of three tailoring redox enzymes as being responsible for
the biosynthesis of the six-membered carbocyclic ring systems.
Specifically, two redox enzymes (ChxJ and ChxI) alone can
generate an active intermediate that undergoes a cascade of non-enzymatic transformations to create APN, while a reductive enzyme
(ChxG) acts as a gatekeeper, directing the same intermediate down a different pathway toward CHX. Finally, the full nature of each
biosynthetic pathway was established in detail, including the formation mechanisms of six-membered carbocyclic rings.
KEYWORDS: cycloheximide, actiphenol, biosynthesis, enoyl reductase, six-membered carbocyclic ring
■INTRODUCTION
Cycloheximide (CHX, 1), featuring a glutarimide moiety and a
cyclohexanone group, was first discovered in 1947 from
Streptomyces griseus,1 and it is now commonly used in
biomedical research as an inhibitor of eukaryotic protein
synthesis.2 It is known that 1 can bind to the E-site of the 60S
large subunit of eukaryotic ribosomes, thereby blocking the
translocation step in polypeptide chain elongation.3−5 Based
on this mechanism, a global translation initiation sequencing
(GTI-seq) approach was developed to provide a view of
alternative translation initiation in mammalian cells.6 Actiphe-
nol (APN, 2) has the same carbon backbone as 1 but consists
of an aromatic phenol ring instead of a cyclohexanone group. It
was first isolated in 1959 from an actinomycete strain, which
also produces 1.7 The phenomenon of coproduction of 1 and 2
has also existed in other strains, such as Streptomyces sp.
YIM56141,8 Streptomyces albulus,9 and Streptomyces sp.
YINM00001.10 In a previous study, Heine et al. demonstrated
that the glutarimide moiety of 1 and 2 was produced through
Michael addition of a malonyl extender to an α,β-unsaturated
intermediate unit, followed by cyclization.11 In addition, Yin et
al. postulated that the production of 1 and 2 is governed by a
single biosynthetic pathway and suggested postmodification
benzene ring dearomatization8 as a possible mechanism.
However, this interpretation is supported only by chxG or
chxH gene knockout experiments in vivo, and there is a lack of
experimental evidence to support it in vitro (Figure 1).
Furthermore, the dearomatization of the benzene ring is
difficult to achieve under normal temperature and pressure
(NTP), leaving uncertainty over the mechanism of six-
membered carbocyclic ring formation in these compounds.
In this study, the biosynthetic gene cluster (BGC) of 1 and 2
from a new producer Streptomyces sp. YINM00100 was
elucidated. A combination of gene deletion, chemical synthesis,
and enzyme characterization techniques was then used to
identify a tailoring redox-enzyme ensemble responsible for the
biosynthesis of the six-membered carbocyclic ring system in 1
and 2. The results show a new cooperative strategy between
these redox enzymes that results in two parallel pathways
capable of generating both benzene and cyclohexanone rings
simultaneously.

■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
In Vivo Identification of chx BGC Tailoring Redox
Enzymes in Streptomyces sp. YINM00100. In our ongoing
efforts to discover new microbial natural products, we
discovered a CHX (1)- and APN (2)-producing strain of
Streptomyces sp. YINM00100, which was isolated from the
Chinese medicinal herb Peperomia dindygulensis Miq. To
identify the BGC responsible for the production of 1 and 2
in Streptomyces sp. YINM00100, the genome of the strain was
sequenced, and the reported chx BGC (GenBank accession
number JX014302) from Streptomyces sp. YIM56141 was used
as a query to search for similar genes using local BLAST
analyses. It was found that the chx BGC that was in
Streptomyces sp. YINM00100 (GenBank accession number
OQ916340) shares a similar gene architecture with chx BGC
in Streptomyces sp. YIM56141 (Figure 1A). Both BGCs include
an acyl carrier protein (ACP, ChxC), an amidotransferase
(AMT, ChxD), an acyltransferase (AT, ChxB), a type I
polyketide synthase (PKS, ChxE), and four tailoring enzymes:
an enoyl reductase (ChxG), a ketoreductase (ChxH), a
cytochrome P450 (ChxI), and a three-domain carboxylic
acid reductase (ChxJ). The first four enzymes are proposed to
form the glutarimide-containing polyketide backbone, and the
four tailoring enzymes may participate in the postmodification
of the nascent polyketide intermediate 3 (Figure 1B). In our
prior work, the chxE/J/I/G/H genes have been inactivated in
vivo to verify their in vivo function, and besides ChxH, the
function of the other three tailoring enzymes has not been
determined in vitro.8 To confirm their performance, a genomic
library of Streptomyces sp. YINM00100 was constructed based
on cosmid vector SuperCos 1, from which the positive cosmid
p19D8 containing tailoring genes was screened by polymerase
chain reaction (PCR). Following this, the chxJ, chxI, and chxG
genes were inactivated using λ RED-mediated PCR targeting
mutagenesis,12 and the genotypes of the resultant mutant
strains Streptomyces sp. YINM00100-ΔchxJ, Streptomyces sp.
YINM00100-ΔchxI, and Streptomyces sp. YINM00100-ΔchxG
were confirmed by PCR analysis (Figures S1−S3). HPLC
analysis of the fermentations from Streptomyces sp.
YINM00100-ΔchxJ and Streptomyces sp. YINM00100-ΔchxI
showed elimination of the production of both 1 and 2 (Figure
2A, traces ii and iii), indicating the essential roles of both chxJ
and chxI in the biosynthesis of 1 and 2. However, mutant strain
Streptomyces sp. YINM00100-ΔchxG still produced 1 and 2
(Figure 2A, trace iv), which is inconsistent with a previous
report that the ΔchxG mutant only abolished the production of
1 but not 2.8 This suggests that there may be a similar gene in
the genome of Streptomyces sp. YINM00100 that can
compensate for the function of chxG.
ChxJ and ChxI Act Together to Produce Aromatic
Actiphenol from a Linear PKS Intermediate. We propose
that ChxE produces a nascent glutarimide-containing polyke-
tide intermediate (3), which is presumed to be the substrate of
ChxJ or ChxI.8,13−15 Unfortunately, the lack of accumulation of
any discrete intermediate in both mutant strains Streptomyces
sp. YINM00100-ΔchxJ and Streptomyces YINM00100-ΔchxI
prevented the study of the tailoring enzymes in vitro. This
problem was circumnavigated using Streptomyces himastatinicus
ATCC 53653, a strain that is capable of producing 9-
methylstreptimidone (7).15 Compound 7 was obtained by
large-scale fermentation and then converted to compound 3 on
the bench using a sequence of standard chemical trans-
formations (Figure 1C, Figures S4−S12, S23, and S24, and
Tables S3 and S4). The presence of the carboxyl and aldehyde
groups in 3 and 8 renders the C-10 carbonyl prone to enol
tautomerization, thus resulting in the epimerization of the
methyl group at C-11. This causes 3 and 8 to always appear as
a diastereomeric mixture during HPLC analysis (Figure S28).

ChxJ is a three-domain carboxylic acid reductase, which
includes an adenylation (A) domain that recognizes and
activates substrate, an ACP domain responsible for forming a
thioester with the substrate, and a reductase (R) domain that
reduces the thioester to an aldehyde.16 The ChxJ reductase was
expressed (Figure S29A), and assays were carried out with 3 as
a substrate using optimized conditions with ATP and NADPH
as cofactors. After the reaction, HPLC analysis showed that the
substrate 3 decreased, and a new peak pair was present at a
retention time of 24 min (Figure 2B, trace iv). The new peaks
were confirmed to be 8 by comparison to a chemically
synthesized standard. This confirms that ChxJ can recognize
carboxylic acid 3 and is capable of reducing it to the
corresponding aldehyde.
Based on our previously proposed biosynthetic pathway,
oxidation of the C-8 hydroxyl in intermediate 8 (i.e., to give a
reactive intermediate resembling 4) would trigger an intra-
molecular aldol condensation between C-9 and C-14 to yield a
cyclized intermediate 5 (Figure 1B). To verify this hypothesis,
the aldehyde intermediate 8 was treated with the oxidant
Dess−Martin periodinane (DMP). HPLC analysis showed that
a large part of the substrate was transformed to APN (2) but
not intermediate 5 (Figure S30), thus demonstrating that
oxidation of the C-8 hydroxyl in 8 results in spontaneous
cyclization and then aromatization to produce 2. This result
encouraged us to seek out a protein within the chx BGC that is
capable of performing this oxidation enzymatically. ChxI, a
cytochrome P450, was identified from gene annotation studies
as the only enzyme with oxidative activity. The recombinant
ChxI was purified as an N-His6-tagged fusion protein from
Escherichia coli (Figure S29B), which exhibited the unique red
color of P450 oxidase and showed an ultraviolet absorption
shift from 420 to 450 nm when treated with CO and Na2S2O4
(Figure S31). Enzyme assays were performed with 8 using
FDRse and FDXse as redox partners (ferredoxin (Fdx) and
ferredoxin reductase (FdR) from Synechococcus elongatus PCC
7942),17 but the expected product 2 was not observed (Figure
S32A). The same assays were attempted by using 3 as a
substrate instead to no avail (Figure S32B). Further research
revealed that some P450 enzymes require the substrate to be
bound to an ACP or peptidyl carrier protein (PCP) (e.g.,
P450BioI,18 OxyD,19 and NovI,20 which are involved in the
biosynthesis of biotin, vancomycin, and novobiocin, respec-
tively). With this in mind, the hypothesis that ChxI may react
with the substrate bound to the ACP domain of ChxJ was
proposed. To test this, ChxI and its cofactors were added to
the ChxJ reaction using compound 3 as a substrate. HPLC
analysis showed the presence of compound 2, thereby
confirming the speculation (Figure 2C, trace ii).
Overall, these results suggest that (1) the A domain of ChxJ
activates and loads 3 to the ACP domain, (2) the resultant
acyl-S-ACP intermediate is oxidized at C-8 by ChxI to form a
β-dicarbonyl group containing an acyl-S-ACP intermediate, (3)
this is reduced at the thioester and released by the R domain of
ChxJ to give reactive aldehyde 4, and (4) aldehyde 4
undergoes spontaneous (i.e., non-enzymatic) intramolecular
aldol condensation between C-9 and C-14, dehydration, and
aromatization to yield compound 2 (Figure 1B).
ChxG Acts as an Enoyl Reductase and Suggests an
Alternative Biosynthetic Pathway. The combination of
ChxJ and ChxI alone are sufficient to produce actiphenol (2)
without the need for ChxG and ChxH. This is consistent with
our previous in vivo knockout experiments,8 which showed that
both chxG and chxH mutants were unable to produce CHX
(1) but were still able to produce APN (2). This suggests that
reductases ChxH and ChxG are essential for the biosynthesis
of CHX (1). Previously, it has been shown that ChxH is
responsible for the final step in the biosynthetic pathway: the
reduction of the C-8 carbonyl group in dehydrocycloheximide
(6)8 (Figure 1B). It was thought to be possible, therefore, that
the last remaining enzyme, ChxG, is responsible for the
reductive dearomatization of APN (2) to give intermediate 6
(Figure 1B). ChxG was expressed and purified to validate this
hypothesis in vitro. The purified recombinant ChxG (Figure
S29C) exhibited a yellow color and displayed a UV−vis
absorption spectrum consistent with those of typical
flavoproteins. After the denaturation of ChxG with methanol,
HPLC analysis indicated that FMN was noncovalently bound
to ChxG (Figure S33). However, after investigating the
reaction of ChxG with compound 2 as the substrate and
NADPH as the cofactor, the dearomatization of 2 was not
observed under any of the conditions screened (Figure S34A).
Amino acid sequence alignment showed that ChxG was an
enoyl reductase belonging to the old yellow enzyme family
(Figure S35). This family typically uses FMN as a coenzyme
and is known to reduce carbon−carbon double bonds that are
conjugated with electron-withdrawing groups such as ketones,
aldehydes, nitriles, and nitro groups.21−23 It is therefore
plausible that ChxG may act to reduce the C12−C13 E-double
bond in proposed intermediates 3, 4, or 8 to a saturated carbon
bond. This would prevent the spontaneous aromatization of
the resulting C9−C14 cyclized intermediate (i.e., intermediate
5 without a double bond). This idea was subsequently assessed
in vitro.
In the first instance, acid compound 3 was used as a
substrate with ChxG, and no double bond reduction was
observed (Figure S34B). However, in the actual biosynthetic
pathway, compound 3 was loaded to the ACP domain of ChxJ
to form a 3-S-ChxJ complex, not as a free carboxylic acid. We
decided to use substrate analogs to run the mimetic reaction
because the thioester form of 3 was hard to synthesize. Three
structurally different aldehyde intermediates 2-methylcinna-
maldehyde, α-methylbutenal, and α-methylpentenal were
selected as the substrates of enoyl-reductase ChxG and all
the α,β-double bonds of these substrates can be reduced by
ChxG (Figures S34D, S36, and S37). Therefore, methyl-
cinnamoyl-SNAC (Figures S4, S13−S16, and S25 and Table
S5) was used as a model system for 3-S-ChxJ instead. In this
event, ChxG was not able to reduce the double bond of
methylcinnamoyl-SNAC (Figure S34C), implying that 3-S-
ChxJ is not a suitable substrate either.
Next, focus was shifted to aldehyde 4. Since the propensity
of biosynthetic aldehyde 4 to spontaneously undergo
cyclization and aromatization under laboratory conditions
had already been established (Figure S30), it was not pursued
as a substrate in this series of control reactions. The synthetic
aldehyde 8 was pursued instead. Enzyme assays of ChxG with
8 were carried out under optimized conditions using NADPH
as a cofactor. HPLC analysis revealed the disappearance of
substrate 8 and the presence of one new peak, designated as 9
(Figure 3A, trace iii). Compound 9 was subjected to HR-ESI-
MS, whereby a strong signal at m/z 320.1466 [M + Na]+ was
observed (Figure 3A). This corresponds to the molecular
formula ([C15H23NO5] + Na)+, which is in good agreement
with the reduction product of 8 (calcd., 320.1468).

Above experimental results indicated that ChxG should
mediate the C12−C13 double bond reduction of unstable
aldehyde 4 in CHX biosynthesis and yield a new intermediate
10, which could then undergo spontaneous aldol condensation
and dehydration to generate 11 (Figure 4). To determine how
the pathway flowed to 10 or 11, ChxJ, ChxI, and ChxG were
added together to perform a one-pot reaction using 3 as a
substrate. HPLC analysis showed that in addition to actiphenol
(2, Figure S38), a new peak was visible. The identity of this
newly observed peak was investigated through the synthesis of
authentic samples of intermediates 11 and 6. This was
achieved using 1 as a starting substrate (Figures S4, S17−
S22, S26, and S27 and Table S6) through DMP oxidation and
DDQ dehydrogenation (Figure 3B). Comparison of these
synthetically produced samples to the HPLC trace of the ChxJ,
ChxI, and ChxG one-pot reaction quickly confirmed the new
identity of the new peak as compound 11 (Figure 3C, trace ii).
Also, the molar ratio of enzyme reaction products antiphenol
(2) and compound 11 is approximately 11:1 (Figure S40),
showing a significant difference from the roughly 1:1 molar
ratio of these compounds under the fermentation condition of
the wild-type strain Streptomyces sp. YINM00100. This
difference may stem from the varying catalytic activities of
the enzymes in vivo and in vitro. This result also indicated that
aldehyde 4 was a real substrate of ChxG. After the pathway
flows to compound 11, an outstanding question in the pathway
was the nature of the transformation of 11 into 6. In a series of
reactions, compound 11 was observed to convert to 6 both in
the presence of ChxG but also in a control reaction in the
absence of any enzyme (Figure 3D, traces ii and iii), thereby
indicating that this reaction may be non-enzymatic and can be
mediated by NADPH alone. The lability of this double bond
toward reduction by NADPH can be explained by the large
partial positive charge at the β-position as a result of its cross-
conjugation with two separate carbonyl groups (Figure S41).
In addition, the conversion of 11 to 6 could also be mediated
by other redox sources such as NADH and NMNH (Figure
S42). This type of NADPH-mediated non-enzymatic reduction
has been reported in the literature.24 However, due to the slow
transformation of 11 into 6 in vitro by these redox cofactors,
we cannot rule out the possibility that reductases outside the
gene cluster are involved in this reaction.
Since previous experiments showed that 1 was still produced
in the ΔchxG mutant strain, this led us to suspect that there
may be a homologous gene in the Streptomyces sp. YINM00100
genome that complements the function of chxG. Genome
alignments showed that gene YINM00100-3952 is homologous
to ChxG (61% identity) outside of the chx BGC (Figure S35).
The gene YINM00100-3952 was amplified, and the protein
was expressed and purified (Figure S29D). HPLC-MS analysis
revealed that YINM00100-3952 can also catalyze the reduction
of the carbon−carbon double bond of 8 to 9 (Figure 3A, trace
iv). This result illustrates that YINM00100-3952 has the same
activity as ChxG and explains why the Streptomyces sp.
YINM00100-ΔchxG strain still produced compounds 1 and 2.
Stereoselectivity of the FMN-Dependent Enoyl
Reductase ChxG. Old yellow enzyme-catalyzed hydro-
genations are generally stereoselective,25,26 but it was difficult
to determine the configuration of α-methyl in ChxG-catalyzed
product 9. Several model substrates were employed to verify
the stereoselectivity of ChxG-catalyzed hydrogenation. 2-
Methylcinnamaldehyde was chosen as a substrate, and the
corresponding (2S)-2-methylphenylpropionaldehyde and (R/
S)-2-methylphenylpropionaldehyde isomers were chemically
synthesized as authentic standards. The retention times of both
S- and R/S-isomers were determined using a chiral HPLC
column (Figure 5A, traces i and ii) and compared to the ChxG
reaction mixture, leading to the conclusion that enzymatic
reduction resulted in the stereoselective reduction to the (S)
isomer (Figure 5A, trace iii). This result is consistent with the
stereoselectivity in reducing the double bonds at C12 and C13
during native cycloheximide biosynthesis.

mechanism of ChxG, attempts were made to obtain its crystal
structure but were unfortunately unsuccessful. Alternatively,
the 3D structure of ChxG was generated through a protein
structure prediction service Robetta27 and a docking engine
AutoDockVina28 was used to probe the structural details of

(Figure S34E), indicating that amino acids H162, H165, and
Y167 are crucial for the reductive activity of ChxG.
■CONCLUSIONS
In our previous study, we proposed that CHX was derived
from the enzymatic dearomatization of APN (2) and that APN
biosynthesis involved the non-enzymatic cyclization, dehy-
dration, and aromatization of intermediate 4. While evidence
for this hypothesis was provided in this study, through the
DMP oxidation of 8 (Figure 1C) to give APN (Figure S30),
subsequent findings suggested that APN is not in fact an
intermediate in the biosynthetic pathway of CHX (1). This
was demonstrated through in vitro characterization of ChxJ,
ChxI, and ChxG (Figure 4). It was shown that polyketide
intermediate 3 (obtained by chemical synthesis) is activated by
the A domain of ChxJ loaded into the ACP domain. The C-8
hydroxyl group of the acyl-S-ACP intermediate is then oxidized
to a carbonyl group by P450 enzyme ChxI. The oxidized acyl-
S-ACP intermediate is subsequently reduced by the R domain
of ChxJ and then dissociates to give 4. ChxG then acts as a
gatekeeper, directing the pathway into two directions: (i) 4
undergo spontaneous cyclization, dehydration, and aromatiza-
tion to form actiphenol (2); (ii) the double bond of 4 is
reduced by ChxG, and then the product undergoes intra-
molecular aldol condensation to form dihydroactiphenol (11),
which is reduced by NAD(P)H to generate dehydrocyclohex-
imide (6). Finally, the keto of 6 is reduced to a hydroxy group
by ChxH to generate the final product cycloheximide (1)
(Figure 4).
Six-membered carbocyclic rings are a common group in
natural products, such as lovastatin29 and spinosyn A,30
menadione,31 and aromatic polyketides and terpenoids. The
biosynthesis of six-membered carbocyclic rings can be grouped
into three main classes: pericyclic reactions,29,32 free radical
reactions,31,33 and addition reactions34−37 (Figures S43−S45),
with addition reactions typically being the most common. In
general, electrophilic addition is used to form six-membered
rings in terpenoid biosynthesis36 and nucleophilic addition is
commonly used in polyketides.37 Nucleophilic addition
reactions in PKS assembly lines, including Claisen condensa-
tions, aldol condensation, and Michael additions, are primarily
responsible for the synthesis of six-membered aromatic rings
(Figure S45). The post-PKS six-membered carbocyclic ring
formation triggered by hydroxyl oxidation and subsequent
intramolecular aldol condensation, however, are relatively rare
in such reactions.38−40 Herein, we have deciphered a rare and
