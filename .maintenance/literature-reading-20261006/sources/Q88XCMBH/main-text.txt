ABSTRACT: Trichothecenes are a widespread family of sesqui-
terpenoid toxins that can pose significant risks to food and feed
safety as well as environmental health. A defining feature of all
trichothecenes is their central tricyclic 12,13-epoxytrichothec-9-ene
(EPT) motif. Although the formation of the EPT central skeleton
has long been presumed to be a spontaneous process, the
nonenzymatic cyclization reaction forming the tetrahydropyran
ring in EPT requires acid catalysis; otherwise, it occurs too slowly
to sustain efficient trichothecene biosynthesis under physiological
conditions. Here, we resolved this decades-old problem by
identifying the missing enzymes for EPT biosynthesis. We
demonstrate that the C11 hydroxyl group of universal trichothe-
cene precursors, isotrichodiol and isotrichotriol, must be acetylated by a strictly conserved O-acetyltransferase Tri3 to furnish a
better leaving group. These acetylated intermediates preferentially undergo spontaneous allylic rearrangement with water to give
shunt products, trichodiol and trichotriol. Therefore, a novel cyclase, Tri14, which was previously annotated as a hypothetical
protein, is required to overcome the kinetically unfavored oxide bridge closure and meanwhile suppress the spontaneous formation
of any shunt products.
■INTRODUCTION
Trichothecenes are a family of sesquiterpenoids produced by
various fungal species within the order Hypocreales.1,2 To date,
over 200 structurally distinct trichothecene natural products
have been isolated, all sharing a key tricyclic structure motif
known as 12,13-epoxytrichothec-9-ene (EPT, Figure 1a).3
These terpenoids exhibit a wide range of biological activities,
depending on the respective functional groups present on the
characteristic EPT skeleton. Many trichothecenes are known
for their acute cytotoxic effects, primarily due to their potent
inhibition of eukaryotic protein synthesis.4 Specifically, they
target the peptidyl transferase center of the 60S ribosomal
subunit, interfering with peptide bond formation.5,6 The
cellular toxicity makes trichothecenes critical virulence factors
for plant pathogenic fungi.7 Furthermore, widespread
trichothecene contamination in grains and forage crops poses
a global threat to food and feed safety.8 As a result,
trichothecenes rank among the most closely monitored
mycotoxins by regulatory agencies worldwide to safeguard
human and animal health.
Trichothecenes are broadly classified into two main types
based on their structural features (Figure 1a). Macrocyclic
trichothecenes, such as verrucarin A and roritoxin B, possess a
macrodiolide ring that bridges C4 and C15 of the EPT core. In
contrast, simple trichothecenes, such as T2-toxin and
deoxynivalenol, lack this additional macrocycle.9,10 Despite
the structural diversity, all trichothecenes are believed to share
a conserved biosynthetic logic (Figure 1b).11 The biosynthesis
begins with the terpene cyclase Tri5, which catalyzes the
cyclization of farnesyl pyrophosphate to produce trichodiene
(1), the first committed intermediate.12 This is followed by the
action of a cytochrome P450 monooxygenase, Tri4, which
catalyzes three consecutive oxygenations to generate iso-
trichodiol (2).13 In Fusarium species, Tri4 orthologues can
catalyze an additional hydroxylation step, yielding isotrichotriol
(3).14 These highly oxygenated bicyclic intermediates, 2 and 3,
have been assumed to undergo spontaneous cyclization to
form EPT (4) and 3-hydroxy-EPT (5), respectively (the latter
is also known as isotrichodermol). At this stage, biosynthesis of
trichothecenes enters the tailoring phase, where decorations of
the EPT core lead to the diverse array of trichothecene natural
products (Figure S1).11,15

While the formation of the tetrahydropyran ring (B ring) in
EPT has long been considered a nonenzymatic process, this
proposal remains open to question. First, the spontaneous
cyclization of intermediates 2 and 3 occurs only under acidic
conditions,16,17 whereas trichothecene biosynthesis typically
proceeds under physiological conditions. Second, the in vitro
acid-catalyzed cyclization of 2 is highly inefficient compared
with the rapid and specific biosynthesis observed in vivo. For
instance, incubating 2 at pH 4.5 for over 3 days produced only
35% of the desired product, 4, at the expense of forming three
other byproducts (e.g., 6−8, Figure 1c).16,18 The low
transformation efficiency, combined with the lack of chemo-,
site-, and stereoselectivity in the spontaneous cyclization of 2,
contrasts sharply with the rapid and clean conversion of 2 to 4
(and 3 to 5) observed in vivo.16 These discrepancies suggest
that the cyclization of 2 to 4 (and 3 to 5) may involve an as-
yet-unidentified enzymatic process. However, the correspond-
ing enzyme(s) have yet to be identified.
In this work, we challenge the long-standing and dogmatic
hypothesis that the formation of the EPT skeleton occurs
spontaneously during trichothecene biosynthesis. We identi-
fied the missing enzymes for EPT biosynthesis, including an O-
acetyltransferase Tri3 and a novel tetrahydropyran-forming
cyclase Tri14, and unraveled the molecular mechanism. These
exciting discoveries enabled us, for the first time, to achieve
efficient biosynthetic formation of EPT under physiological
conditions.
■RESULTS
We hypothesize that if an enzyme is indeed needed for the
cyclization of 2/3, it must be conserved across all
trichothecene biosynthetic pathways, given that the formation
of 4/5 is a requisite step toward all trichothecenes. Upon
analyzing different trichothecene biosynthetic gene clusters,
three genes (tri3/5/14) are unveiled as strictly conserved
across all trichothecene-producing species (Figure 2a).19
Among them, tri3 encodes an O-acetyltransferase whose
function has been characterized in simple trichothecene
biosynthesis as a late-stage tailoring enzyme (Figure 2b).
Tri3 from Fusarium sporotrichioides has been established as the
15-O-acetyltransferase that converts 15-decalonectrin to
calonectrin, en route to T2-toxin,20 whereas Tri3 in

macrocyclic trichothecene biosynthetic pathways remain to be
characterized.
One plausible explanation for the necessity of Tri3 in all
trichothecene pathways is that it may function in the early
phase instead of the late-stage modification, such as the
formation of the EPT core skeleton. This proposal predicts
that abolishing Tri3 activity should either block trichothecene
biosynthesis at a very early (i.e., pre-EPT forming) stage or
significantly lower the yield of all trichothecene intermediates
derived from EPT, given that they would rely on slow and
inefficient spontaneous EPT formation. In line with this
prediction, recent genetic studies showed that disruption of the
tri3 gene in Fusarium graminearum resulted in the accumu-
lation of isotrichotriol and trichotriol as the major products,
rather than the immediate Tri3 substrate, 15-decalonectrin.23
Similarly, disruption of the tri3 gene in Trichoderma
arundinaceum also accumulates the bicyclic intermediate
isotrichodiol, in addition to the expected substrate, tricho-
dermol.11 We reason that if Tri3 is merely involved in the
tailoring phase, disruption of tri3 should not affect early-stage
trichothecene biosynthesis, particularly not accumulating pre-
EPT intermediates. Therefore, we hypothesize that Tri3 is
likely the missing link in EPT biosynthesis.
To test our hypothesis and elucidate the unassigned function
of Tri3 in macrocyclic trichothecene biosynthetic pathways, we
set out to characterize the function of Tri3 orthologues from
macrocyclic trichothecene producers (Figure 3a). We first
coexpressed tri4 and tri5 from Paramyrothecium roridum in the
previously reported heterologous host, Aspergillus nidulans
LO7890.24 The expected product, isotrichodiol 2, was isolated
with a titer of 15 mg/L. Under our fermentation conditions, we
did not observe any EPT product (i.e., 4), further supporting
the notion that spontaneous cyclization of 2 to 4 is a difficult
process under physiological conditions. When tri3 from either
P. roridum or Stachybotrys chartarum was introduced into the
coexpression system, 2 disappeared, and a new compound was
produced, accompanied by trace amounts of 4. NMR and GC-
MS characterization identified the new compound as β-
trichodiol 7, one of the three byproducts observed during the
acid-catalyzed cyclization of 2 (Figure 1c). Despite that only
trace amounts of 4 were produced in vivo after introducing
Tri3, the complete disappearance of 2 supports our hypothesis
that Tri3 plays a role in the early-stage biosynthesis of
trichothecenes, and 2 is likely its substrate.
To investigate the enzymatic function of Tri3, we expressed
P. roridum Tri3 (PrTri3) in E. coli and purified it to
homogeneity for in vitro characterization (Figure S3).
Complete transformation of 2 into 4 and 7 (in a ratio of
approximately 1:4) was observed in the presence of Tri3 and
acetyl-coenzyme A (AcCoA), whereas no reaction of 2
occurred with either Tri3+CoA or boiled Tri3+AcCoA (Figure
3b). Because AcCoA was also stoichiometrically converted to
CoA (Figure S4), we then performed a reaction time-course
analysis to capture the direct O-acetylated product. A reaction
intermediate, 9, was trapped at early reaction time points
(Figure 3b). LC-MS analysis revealed that 9 has a molecular
weight corresponding to the acetylated isotrichodiol (Figure
3c). Repetition of the experiment with 13C-labeled AcCoA,
which was enzymatically synthesized from [2-13C] acetate
using acetyl-CoA synthetase (ACS),25 resulted in the expected
+1 Da mass shift of 9. No reverse reaction was observed when
9 and CoA were incubated with Tri3. Isolation and 2D-NMR
structural analysis confirmed the identity of 9 as 11-O-acetyl-
isotrichodiol (Table S9).

(e.g., CH2Cl2) but is highly labile in aqueous solutions, even at
pH 7.5. In buffered solution, 9 undergoes spontaneous

Although Tri3-catalyzed acetylation of 2 and 3 allowed us to
achieve biosynthesis of the EPT core skeleton under
physiological conditions (e.g., pH 7.5), on-pathway inter-
mediates 4 and 5 were obtained only as minor products both
in vivo and in vitro. These observations led us to hypothesize
that an additional enzyme is required to facilitate the
cyclization of 9 and 10 in aqueous solutions in order to
prevent the formation of the shunt products 7 and 11. We
therefore revisited the results of the comparative genomic
analysis and focused on tri14, the only other strictly conserved
trichothecene gene whose function in trichothecene biosyn-
thesis remains elusive (Figure 2a). Bioinformatic analysis
shows that tri14 encodes a hypothetical protein belonging to
the Mala s 1 allergenic protein family (IPR054550).26 Genetic
studies have indicated that tri14 is critical for the formation of
F. graminearum infection and spread in wheat19 and also
required for its deoxynivalenol production on wheat.27 These
results suggest that Tri14 likely plays an important, yet
unknown, role in trichothecene biosynthesis. We hence
hypothesize that Tri14 is perhaps responsible for the
cyclization of 9 to 4.
When P. roridum tri14 was introduced into our coexpression
system (e.g., tri3 + 4 + 5 + 14), 4 was produced as the major
product, whereas only trace amounts of 7 were detected
(Figure 5a). In comparison, only the early intermediate 2 was
formed when tri3 was absent (i.e., tri4 + 5 + 14). These results
suggest that Tri3 and Tri14 together can efficiently
biosynthesize 4 from 2. While Tri14 alone is not sufficient
to transform 2 to 4, it is necessary to prevent the formation of
7.
To confirm the function of Tri14, we then expressed P.
roridum Tri14 in S. cerevisiae and purified it to homogeneity for
in vitro activity characterization (Figure S3). As shown in
Figure 5b, when Tri14 was included in a one-pot cascade assay
with the Tri3-catalyzed acetylation of 2, rapid and exclusive
formation of 4 was achieved at pH 7.5. In contrast, with boiled
Tri14, the major product became 7, which was preferentially
formed from the spontaneous allylic rearrangement of the
acetylated intermediate 9. Furthermore, Tri14 only acts on the
activated allylic acetate intermediate 9 to facilitate the ether
bridge formation as Tri14 could not convert 7 into 4. Steady-
state kinetic analysis of Tri14-catalyzed cyclization of 9
revealed a kcat of 10.2 ± 0.8 s−1 and a KM of 0.35 ± 0.09
mM (Figure S6). The turnover number kcat and catalytic
efficiency kcat/KM (2.9 × 104 M−1 s−1) are comparable to most
enzymes operating in secondary metabolism,28 further
demonstrating that Tri14 is a bona fide cyclase.

clean conversion of 3 to 5 was achieved when Tri3 and Tri14
were provided in one-pot, but 11 can no longer be converted

To gain deeper insights into the novel cyclase activity of
Tri14, we generated an AlphaFold29 model of P. roridum
Tri14. Structural analysis revealed a putative active site pocket
capable of accommodating the corresponding substrate and
product (Figure S7). Docking of rigid product 4 identified
several polar residues potentially involved in substrate
recognition and catalysis (Figure 6a). To evaluate their
catalytic role, we performed alanine-scanning mutagenesis,
selecting six residues based on their proximity to the docked
ligand. As shown in Figure 6b, the E266A and H218A mutants
significantly impaired enzyme activity, resulting in byproduct 7
becoming the major product. Multiple sequence alignment
further revealed that these two residues are strictly conserved
across all Tri14 homologues (Figure S8), supporting their
important roles in Tri14 enzyme catalysis.
Based on these results, we propose a plausible mechanism
for Tri14-catalyzed cyclic ether formation (Figure 6c). Tri14
may preferentially bind substrate 9 in a near-attack
conformation. Such substrate preorganization is a common
strategy employed by various cyclases to lower the activation
barrier.30 The highly conserved H218 may act as a general
base, deprotonating the nucleophilic C2-OH group, while
E266 could function as a general acid, facilitating the departure
of the acetate leaving group. Whether Tri14 follows a
concerted SN2 or stepwise SN1 mechanism remains unclear,
as the energy barriers for both pathways are predicted to be
similar (Figure S9). Further biochemical and structural
characterization, including cocrystal structural determination
of Tri14 in complex with the cyclized product or unreactive
substrate analogues, will be necessary to fully elucidate its
catalytic mechanism.
■DISCUSSION
A decades-old dilemma in trichothecene biosynthesis has
involved the formation of the tetrahydropyran ring (B ring) of
the central tricyclic skeleton (i.e., EPT). We now reject the
long-standing assumption that EPT formation is a spontaneous
process and demonstrate that Tri3 and Tri14 are the two
enzymes essential for the efficient cyclization of the bicyclic
trichothecene intermediates (i.e., isotrichodiol and isotricho-
triol) to afford the EPT framework in all trichothecenes. The
requirement of not just one but two enzymes for the formation
of the tetrahydropyran ring in the EPT underscores the
difficulty of this transformation in vivo.
We show that the acetyltransferase Tri3 catalyzes
regiospecific O-acetylation on the bicyclic intermediates to
make their 11-hydroxyl group a better leaving group. While
such an activation strategy facilitates subsequent oxide bridge
closure, the activated allylic acetate intermediate poses another
challenge for EPT biosynthesis in aqueous solution, that is, a
kinetically more favored pathwaywater attack at C9 after
allylic eliminationoutcompetes the cyclization reaction.
Consequently, a dedicated EPT cyclase, Tri14, is needed to
overcome the kinetic barrier of this intramolecular substitution
reaction, meanwhile shielding this unstable intermediate from
solvent to suppress the competing spontaneous allylic
rearrangement reaction (Figure 5d). This mechanism can
now reconcile discrepancies observed in the literature
regarding the biosynthetic formation of EPT. The chemical
instability of the acetylated intermediates 9 and 10 makes them
elusive in any previous natural product isolation efforts. The
spontaneous formation of 4/5 from either 2/3 or 7/11 now
explains why trichothecene production in cell culture is not
completely abolished when either tri3 or tri14 was
disrupted.11,21,22,27 However, spontaneous cyclization is
insufficient for efficient production of trichothecenes and
would reduce the virulence of plant pathogenic fungi. Indeed,
the reduced trichothecene production resulting from the
spontaneous cyclization of 9 in the absence of Tri14 explains
why F. graminearum tri14 mutants produced significantly less
deoxynivalenol and fungal biomass in wheat heads.18
The acetylation-then-substitution biosynthetic logic revealed
by this study (Figure 5d) is also reminiscent of the last two
steps in the biosynthetic pathway of thebaine.31 In the
biosynthesis of this important opium alkaloid, the allylic
alcohol intermediate (7S)-salutaridinol is first acetylated by an
acetyltransferase, SalAT, to yield the labile allylic acetate
intermediate, (7S)-salutaridinol-7-O-acetate.32 Next, thebaine
synthase (THS) catalyzes an intramolecular SN2′ displacement
reaction on this acetylated intermediate to afford the
hydrofuran ring and complete thebaine biosynthesis.31
While the biosynthetic strategies are similar, the key cyclase
proteins THS and Tri14 are not similar to each other. THS is a
member of the pathogenesis-related 10 protein (PR10)
superfamily,31 which also includes norcoclaurine synthase
(NCS), the gateway enzyme in benzylisoquinoline alkaloid
metabolism.33 However, Tri14 is most similar to the Mala s 1
allergenic protein from the yeast Malassezia sympodialis,26
adopting a β-propeller fold completely different from THS.34
Moreover, Tri14 catalyzes a formal stereoinverted nucleophilic
substitution reaction (Figure 6c), which is distinct from the
SN2′ syn displacement reaction catalyzed by THS.31
Using a thermodynamically activated allylic acetate inter-
mediate appears to be a common strategy in natural product
biosynthesis for constructing ether linkages at allylic carbons.
These intermediates take advantage of their inherent reactivity,
driven by the stability of the allylic system and the favorable
energetics of acetate as a leaving group, to facilitate the
formation of complex molecular structures. In each pathway, a
dedicated cyclase plays a critical role in guiding the reaction
trajectory of such reactive intermediates. By providing
exquisite control over regio- and stereospecific outcomes,
these enzymes prevent undesired side reactions and ensure
efficient product formation. Their ability to stabilize transition
states and impose structural constraints (e.g., binding substrate
in a near-attack conformation) likely promotes the desired
reaction.
■CONCLUSIONS
In summary, we elucidated the mechanism for the biosynthetic
formation of EPT, which is the central tricyclic skeleton for all
trichothecene natural products. The biosynthesis of the
tetrahydropyran ring in EPT involves an acetyltransferase
Tri3 and a hypothetic protein Tri14. This combination of
thermodynamic activation (by Tri3) and enzymatic control on
cyclization (by Tri14) exemplifies a sophisticated biocatalytic
process in natural product biosynthesis, where reactive
intermediates and specialized enzymes work in tandem to
