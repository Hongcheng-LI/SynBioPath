ABSTRACT: Merkel cell carcinoma (MCC) is a rare and
aggressive cutaneous cancer. Two new prenylated indole 2,5-
diketopiperazine alkaloids, brevianamides E1 (1) and E2 (2), were
isolated from a Penicillium fungus. Both compounds showed
moderate cytotoxic activity against select MCC cell lines (i.e.,
MCC13, MKL-1, UISO, and WaGa) in the low micromolar range.
The relative and absolute configurations of 1 and 2 were
determined by combined approaches, including NOESY spectros-
copy, DFT ECD and DP4 plus calculations, and Marfey’s reaction.
Literature research and the comparison of NMR and ECD data led
to the structure revision of three previously reported natural
analogues, notoamides K and P and asperversiamide L. The
structurally unstable 1 and 2 underwent steady interconversion
under neutral aqueous conditions. Investigation of the degradation of 2 in acidic methanol solutions led to the identification of a new
methoxylated derivative (6) and two new ring-opened products (7 and 8) with the rearranged, elongated, 4-methylpent-3-ene side
chain. The facile transformation of 2 to 7 and 8 was promoted by the intrinsic impurity (i.e., formaldehyde) of HPLC-grade
methanol through the aza-Cope rearrangement.
E
volution of natural product structures and their biological
functions is driven by natural selection, benefiting the
producing organism through the gain of advantageous traits for
survival and reproduction.1 During evolution, some natural
products acquired elevated chemical reactivity, possibly due to
selective pressures (i.e., predation, environmental changes) and
the need for adaptation to their specific environment.2
Chemically reactive natural products often exhibit reactivity
owing to the presence of specific functional groups, such as
strained ring-containing electrophiles (e.g., β-lactams, β-
lactones, epoxides, and cyclopropanes), hetero-Michael accept-
ors (e.g., unsaturated ketones, esters, and amides), aldehydes,
hemiacetals, hemiaminals, N,O-acetals, and aminals.3,4 These
molecules can participate in various chemical transformations,
such as solvolysis, oxidation, condensation, substitution,
(cyclo)addition, or rearrangement reactions.2 Studying the
reactivity of these natural products not only facilitates a better
understanding of their mechanistic properties, but it is also
important in drug discovery, to gain insight into natural
strategies for target engagement, enabling the development of
new therapeutic agents by leveraging the potential medicinal
properties of chemically reactive natural products.4,5 Examples
of the successful use of covalently acting natural products to
treat human disease include the β-lactam antibiotics (typified
by penicillin V),6,7 the hemiaminal-containing anticancer drug
ecteinascidin 743 (trabectedin),8 the anticancer clinical drug
candidates omuralide and salinosporamide A,9,10 and the
potent covalent proteasome inhibitor epoxomicin, the study of
which led to the discovery of the anticancer drug carfilzomib.11
Merkel cell carcinoma (MCC) is a rare, highly aggressive,
neuroendocrine skin malignancy with the 5-year overall
survival rate ranging from 0−18% for patients at the metastatic
stage.12−15 While approximately 80% of cases of MCC are
associated with the oncogenic Merkel cell polyomavirus
(MCPyV),16 older age (typically over 75), excessive exposure
to sunlight, and a weakened immune system comprise the
largest risk factors for this deadly disease, with an estimated
disease-associated mortality approximately three times that of
melanoma.17−19 For many years, chemotherapy has been the
standard treatment for advanced stage MCC. However, this

A recent high throughput screen campaign (HTS) has led to
the discovery of a series of selective small-molecule modulators
of MCC cell viability from various natural sources (i.e., plants,
marine invertebrates, microbes),30,31 including the fungal
metabolites asperphenamate and mycophenolic acid. Here
we describe the identification of two new prenylated indole
2,5-diketopiperazine alkaloids, brevianamides E1 (1) and E2
(2), along with three known metabolites brevianamides E
(3)32 (Figures S61 and S62) and A (4)32 (Figures S63 and
S64) and mycophenolic acid (5)33 (Figures S65 and S66). The
compounds were isolated through bioassay-guided fractiona-
tion of the active extract of Penicillium sp. obtained from a
deep-water Great Lakes sediment sample. Both 1 and 2 are
chemically reactive molecules that undergo skeletal inter-
conversion under neutral aqueous conditions. Their cytotox-
icity against select MCC cell lines is closely associated with the
intrinsic reactivities of the C-11-oxygenated 2,5-diketopiper-
azine motifs within their structures. The unexpected skeletal
transformation and equilibrium of 1 and 2 led to the structural
evaluation of related compounds, resulting in the proposed
structural revision of three previously reported natural
analogues (i.e., notoamides K34 and P35 and asperversiamide
L36). Further investigation of the facile transformation
pathways of 1 and 2 under acidic conditions affords insight
into the mechanistic properties of the chemically reactive, C-
11-oxygenated 2,5-diketopiperazine motifs.

To determine the absolute configuration of the proline
residues for 1 and 2, acidic hydrolysis followed by Marfey’s
reactions were applied (Figure S55). HPLC-MS analysis
revealed the retention time for the standard L-DLA derivatives
of L-proline and D-proline at 19.93 and 21.54 min,
respectively. The L-DLA derivatives of the prolines derived
from the hydrolysates of 1 and 2 were observed at 19.96 and
19.99 min, respectively, indicating the (L) configuration for the
proline residues. Thus, the absolute configuration of position
17 was established to be (17S) for both 1 and 2. Therefore,
based on the established relative configurations of 1 and 2,
they were determined to have the same absolute configuration
of (2S,3R,11R,17S).

A structural search of 1 with SciFinder returned three known
natural analogues, notoamides K34 and P35 and asperversia-
mide L,36 which were reported to share the same pentacyclic
substructure as 1 (Figure 3). However, the originally published
spectroscopic data on the three compounds, including 1H and
13C NMR data (Tables S1−S3), specific rotation values ([α]D
−103 ∼−140) and experimental ECD data [205−209 nm
(strong positive), 241−256 nm (strong negative)],34−36 were
almost identical to those of 2, strongly suggesting all four
compounds shared the same C-11-fused-spiro skeleton and
absolute configuration. As the presence of NH-19 (δH 7.67, s)
in 2 and the related HMBC correlations were the key for
distinguishing 2 from 1, we first examined these 1D and 2D
NMR signatures in the original publications of the three
known analogues. For both notoamides K and P, the NH
protons (δH 7.67 for notoamide K and δH 7.81 for notoamide)
that were mistakenly assigned for the indole NH-1 were both
reported as broad singlets with no related HMBC correlation
from them.34,35 In the case of asperversiamide L, HMBC
correlations were indeed observed from the free NH proton
(δH 8.88, misassigned as NH-1) to C-12 and C-17 in the
original HMBC spectrum (see Supporting Information of ref
36.) which only made sense for a C-11-fused-spiro skeleton
like that of 2. Thus, the structures of notoamides K and P and
asperversiamide L were tentatively revised as shown in Figure
3.
To confirm the revised structures and absolute configuration
of notoamides K and P and asperversiamide L, theoretical
ECD spectra for the originally determined and the tentatively
revised structures of the three molecules and a model
compound 2a were calculated and compared with the
experimental ECD spectrum of 2 (Figure 3). Compound 2a
was used as a control molecule by altering the proposed
structure of 2 based on the originally determined structures of
notoamides K and P and asperversiamide L. As expected, the
calculated ECD spectra of all three tentatively revised
structures matched well with the experimental ECD spectrum
of 2 and the reported ECD data of the three molecules34−36
while the calculated ECD spectra of the three originally
determined structures and the model compound 2a all
exhibited major disagreement with the experimental data.
Furthermore, DP4 plus analyses applying Gauge-Including
Atomic Orbital (GIAO) NMR chemical shift calculation were
conducted to reinforce the structure revisions.37 The calculated
1H and 13C chemical shifts of the originally determined and
tentatively revised structures were compared with those of the
experimental NMR spectroscopic data of notoamides K and P
and asperversiamide L. The same calculation and data
comparison were applied for 2a and 2. The DP4 plus analyses
demonstrated 100% probabilities of 2 and the tentatively
revised notoamides K and P and asperversiamide L as the
correct structures, while 0% probabilities were determined for
2a and the originally determined structures for the three
known compounds (Tables S5−S8). Therefore, the structures
of notoamides K and P and asperversiamide L were revised as
depicted in Figure 3 bearing the same C-11-fused-spiro
skeleton as that of 2.
Considering the close structural similarities of 1, 2, and
brevianamide E (3), it is reasonable to hypothesize that all
three compounds were derived from the same biosynthetic
pathways that have been well characterized for 3 and other
known brevianamides.38,39 To first investigate whether 1 was
an artifact formed by autoxidation of 3, purified 3 was
subjected to several oxidation conditions with hydrogen
peroxide (H2O2), singlet oxygen (1O2, Rose Bengal), and
superoxide anion (O2
•−, KO2), respectively. None of these
conditions led to any detectable yield of 1 or 2 (Figure S59).
Moreover, during the initial purification process by HPLC
using acidic aqueous buffers (MeCN/0.1% formic acid or 0.1%
TFA), both 1 and 2 were found to be unstable and quickly
interconverted and equilibrated to an approximate 1:9 (1: 2)
mixture (Figures S56A and S56B). The change to neutral
HPLC conditions (MeCN/H2O) led to the purified 1 and 2
which still underwent slow interconversion during prolonged
incubation (15 days) in neutral aqueous (MeCN/H2O)
solutions, resulting in the same 1:9 (1: 2) mixture eventually
(Figures S56C and S56D). The results seemed to support the
hypothesis that 1 was likely a real natural product that was
biosynthesized from 3 through a possible enzymatic oxidation
process, while 2, despite its significantly higher yield (∼10-fold
isolation yield) than that of 1, was probably an artifact derived
from the chemical conversion of 1 during the extraction and/
or purification process (Scheme 1A). To further investigate the
chemical mechanisms that drive the interconversion of 1 and 2,
the major stereoisomer 2 was subjected to controlled
degradation tests under acidic conditions. Over extended
incubation in MeOH solutions with 0.1% TFA for up to 35
days, the equilibrated 1/2 (1:9) mixture was further trans-
formed into a set of new products, including 6−8 that were
stable under neutral conditions for isolation and structural
characterization [Figures 4A and 4B (top panel)].

20 for 7 and 8) in the structures of 6−8. To test the
hypothesis,
13C-labeled 6−8 was successfully generated by
repeating the acidic transformation of 1 in 13C-labeled MeOH.
The 13C NMR data clearly showed significant enhancement of
the 13C resonances for OCH3-11(6−8) and CH2−20 (7 and
8) (Figures 4C, S37, S40, and S44). While it was apparent the
OCH3-11 originated from MeOH, the lack of proper reactivity
made it unlikely that MeOH was directly incorporated into the
4-methylpent-3-ene side chain. A literature search revealed that
formaldehyde (HCHO), a minor impurity commonly found in
commercial MeOH products,40,41 was a possible, reactive
precursor of the additional CH2 moiety in the elongated, 4-
methylpent-3-ene side chain of 7 and 8. Indeed, the addition of
HCHO (3 equiv) to the reaction of 2 in MeOH with 1% TFA
significantly and selectively enhanced the relative yields of 7
and 8 (Figure 4B). Detection and quantification of HCHO in
MeOH (HPLC) and 13C-labeled MeOH was achieved using
the sensitive 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (2,4-DNPH) probe
that specifically reacted with HCHO to form formaldehyde-
2,4-DNPH (MW 210.1) and with 13C-labeled HCHO to form
13C-labeled formaldehyde-2,4-DNPH (MW 211.1), respec-
tively, (Figures 4D, S57, and S58). Formaldehyde and 13C-
labeled formaldehyde were successfully detected from MeOH
(HPLC) and 13C-labeled MeOH at the concentrations of 93.9
and 25.0 μM, respectively.
Based on this evidence, a plausible facial transformation
pathway was proposed for the interconversion and degradation
of 1 and 2 under acidic aqueous conditions (Scheme 1). In the
structure of 1, the OH-11/C-11/N-19 spin system forms a
hemiaminal motif which is known to be unstable and reactive
under acidic conditions.42,43 In contrast, the NH-19/C-11/O-
11/C-2/NH-1 spin system in the structure of 2 forms two
conjugated N,O-acetals which are probably responsible for the
unique reactivity of 2. Briefly, starting from the structure of 2,
protonation of O-11 (intermediate a) followed by ring opening
at the C-2/O-11 single bond leads to the formation of an
intermediate b bearing a C-2/NH-1 iminium and a free OH-11
group. Further protonation of OH-11 (intermediate c),
dehydration, and deprotonation of NH-19 led to an
intermediate d with a new, C-11/N-19 imine moiety. Further
nucleophilic attack at C-11 by H2O or MeOH coordinates with
the ring closure at C-2/N-19 through the conjugation of the
two imine/iminium systems. Further deprotonation leads to
the formation of 1 or 6 (Scheme 1B). In a separate pathway,
ring opening takes place on intermediate a at the C-11/O-11
single bond resulting in a C-11/NH-19 iminium and a free
OH-2 (intermediate e). The stereochemistry-noncontrolled
attack of MeOH at the imine carbon (C-11) of intermediate e
leads to intermediate f which undergoes the aza-Cope

The activities of the prenylated indole alkaloids 1−4 and the
degradation products 6−8 against the viability of MCC cell

the immortalized skin cell line HaCaT were evaluated by XTT

found inactive (IC50s > 10 μM) against all the six tested cell

cell line MDA-MB-435 (GI50 1.7 μM), the breast cancer cell
line MDA-MB-468 (GI50 1.9 μM), and the non-small cell lung
cancer cell line NCI-H522 (GI50 2.2 μM).
The 2,5-diketopiperazine (2,5-DKP) core is widespread in
biologically active natural products and synthetic molecules.45
Many 2,5-DKP templates have been systematically studied for
their structure−activity relationship (SAR) leading to the
successful development of various therapeutic agents, such as
the anticancer drug plinabulin (NPI-2358/KPU-2),46 the
antiviral agent aplaviroc,47 the PDE5 inhibitor tadalafil48 for
erectile dysfunction, and the oxytocin antagonists retosiban49
and epelsiban.50 The tryptophan-proline 2,5-diketopiperazine
core represents the most common biosynthetic template for
2,5-DKP natural products, with particularly high abundance
and chemical diversity in fungal secondary metabolites.45,51−53

tryptophan residue is very rare and in most cases the
tryptophan β carbon is also oxygenated (e.g., OH, alkoxy, or

General Experimental Procedures. Optical rotations were
recorded on a Rudolph Research Analytical Autopol IV polarimeter
using a 0.25 dm cell in the solvent indicated. ECD spectra were
collected with a Jasco J-1500 CD spectrophotometer. UV data were
measured with a Varian Cary 50 UV−vis spectrophotometer.
Absorbance spectra were obtained on a Jasco V-770 spectropho-
tometer operated by Spectra Manager software. NMR data were
obtained on a Bruker Avance III NMR spectrometer equipped with a
3 mm cryogenic probe (600 MHz for 1H, 150 MHz for 13C). The
chemical shifts recorded in acetone-d6 and DMSO-d6 were referenced
to solvent peaks at 2.05 and 2.50 ppm for 1H NMR spectroscopic data
and 29.84 and 39.52 ppm for
13C NMR spectroscopic data,
respectively. HRESIMS data were collected on an Agilent Technology
6530 Accurate-mass Q-TOF LC/MS. HPLC separations were
performed using an Agilent PrepStar solvent delivery module
equipped with an Agilent ProStar UV−vis detector. All solvents
were of HPLC grade or better.
Fungal Material. The fungal isolate (identification code 5 PBA-2)
was obtained from a sediment sample (deposited in the University of
Oklahoma Fungal Collection with batch # NPDG 40035) collected at
a depth of 110 m in Lake Michigan, USA (GPS coordinates: 47.21,
−88.39) in July 18, 2014.54 Identification of the isolate was performed
by sequencing the nucleotides spanning the ITS1−5.8S−ITS2 regions
of genomic DNA. The resulting sequence was compared to the data
available through GenBank. Several matches at the 100% identity level
were observed, which were consistent with sequences for Penicillium
sp. Sequence data were deposited in Genbank (accession number
OR805563). For natural product chemistry studies, the fungus was
grown on monolayers of Cheerios breakfast cereal supplemented with
0.3% sucrose and 0.005% chloramphenicol in 12 large mycobags
(Unicorn Bags, Plano, TX, USA). After 4 weeks of growth at 21 °C
under constant lighting, the fungal biomass was subjected to overnight
extraction in ethyl acetate. The resulting organic soluble material was
partitioned (×2) against water (1:1, vol/vol). The ethyl acetate layer
was retained, and the solvent was evaporated in vacuo, yielding 31.4 g
of dark-orange-colored organic residue.

separately dissolved in 6 N HCl (500 μL) and heated in a sealed glass
vial at 110 °C for 2 h. The hydrolysates were partitioned with EtOAc
(500 μL x 2) then the H2O layers were evaporated to dryness and

NaHCO3. The reaction mixtures were stirred at 35 °C for 1 h. After
cooling to room temperature, 10 μL of 2 N HCl was added to each
reaction mixture. After mixing, the reaction samples were dried down

Computational Details. Conformational analyses were carried
out using ComputeVOA v1.1 and resulted conformers within 3 kcal/
mol energy window were used as input structures.
ECD Calcualtion.56,57 Geometry, frequency, and ECD calculations
were performed using DFT and TD-DFT with Gaussian 16 on NIH
High Performance Computer Biowulf. Specifically, for compound 1,
input conformers were optimized using DFT with B3LYP/6-31G(d).
Subsequently, TDDFT calculations were applied for the optimized
conformers using IEFPCM in MeOH together with CAM-B3LYP/6-
31+G(d,p) to simulate the ECD spectrum. Output spectra were
extracted and averaged based on their Boltzmann distributions at 298
K using SpecDis.58 Similarly, ωB97XD/Def2SV and ωB97XD/
Def2SVP/IEFPCM in MeOH were applied for ECD calculation of
2 and 2a. For notoamides K and P and asperversiamide L, the
conformers of originally determined and tentatively revised structures
were optimized using B3LYP/6-31G(d) or ωB97XD/Def2SV
followed by ECD calculation using IEFPCM in MeOH together
with B3LYP/6-31+G(d,p), CAM-B3LYP/6-31+G(d,p), or ωB97XD/
Def2SVP, respectively. For 7 and 8, B3LYP/6-31G(d) and LC-
ωHPBE/6-31+G(d,p)/IEFPCM in MeOH were used for optimiza-
tion and ECD calculation of their truncated models with a methyl
group at position N-1.
DP4 Plus Calculation.59 Probabilities of isomers were obtained
following the procedure described in the literature.37 Briefly, input
conformers of 2, 2a, originally determined, and tentatively revised
structures of notoamides K and P and asperversiamide L were
optimized using B3LYP/6-31G(d). Subsequently, individual shielding
tensors were calculated using GIAO mPW1PW91/6-31+G(d,p) with
solvation in DMSO-d6 (for asperversiamide L) or acetone-d6 (for
remaining structures). They were averaged based on their Boltzmann
distributions at 298 K then justified by the respective tensors of TMS
to produce the calculated NMR spectroscopic data. The calculated
NMR chemical shifts were finally applied to the provided Excel sheet
freely accessible in the literature37 to obtain the probabilities of the
isomers.
Cell Viability Assay.30,31 Merkel cell lines were maintained in
RPMI medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and
penicillin−streptomycin. The control cell line, HaCaT, was
maintained in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine
serum and 1% penicillin−streptomycin. Adherent subconfluent cells
(HaCaT, MCC13, MCC26, UISO) were harvested by trypsinization
and resuspended in the same medium. The nonadherent cell lines
(MKL-1 and WaGa) were treated with Accutase and passed through a
cell strainer to establish a single cell solution suspension. Cells were
counted, then transferred to 384-well tissue culture-treated plates (45
μL per well) using a μFill microplate liquid dispenser (BioTek). Cells
were incubated for 4 h before treatment with test or control samples
(prediluted to 10× final concentration in RPMI). For primary
screening, cell viability was estimated by XTT assay after 3 days of
incubation. Based on XTT response and optimization, the adherent
subconfluent cell line and the HaCaT cell line were plated at 2500
cells/well while the nonadherent cell line was plated at 15,000 cells/
well for screening. Active growth inhibitory/cytotoxic samples were
assessed in a dose−response format using the Promega CellTiter Glo
Assay according to the manufacturer’s protocol using 2500 cell/well
for all cell lines. The conditions (cell numbers and incubation times)
were validated as discussed in the screening publication.31
