Chevalinulins A and B, Proangiogenic Alkaloids with a
Spiro[bicyclo[2.2.2]octane-diketopiperazine] Skeleton from Deep-
Sea Cold-Seep-Derived Fungus Aspergillus chevalieri CS-122

ABSTRACT: Chevalinulins A (1) and B (2), two indole
diketopiperazine alkaloids containing an unprecedented spiro-
[bicyclo[2.2.2]octane-diketopiperazine] skeleton, together with a
known analogue neoechinulin B (3), were isolated from the deep-
sea cold-seep-derived fungus Aspergillus chevalieri CS-122. Their
structures were determined by spectroscopic analysis, single-crystal
X-ray diﬀraction, speciﬁc rotation (SR), and NMR calculations.
Compounds 1 and 2 exhibited signiﬁcant in vivo proangiogenic
activity in transgenic zebraﬁsh.
D
eep-sea cold seeps are representative chemosynthetically
driven ecosystems among the most extreme and vibrant
environments on earth, characterized by methane-rich ﬂuid
emissions and distinctive sulfur oxidation−reduction reac-
tions.1−3 Microorganisms surviving in the deep-sea cold-seep
areas are a promising new source with great potential to
produce secondary metabolites with novel structures and
various bioactivities.4−7 However, the distinctive and attractive
natural products from deep-sea cold-seep-derived microbes are
only beginning to be investigated. Within the very limited
number of examples, verruculoid A, an unusual cytochalasin
dimer bonded via a sulﬁde bridge, has been reported from the
deep-sea cold-seep-derived fungus Curvularia verruculosa CS-
129 based on our chemical isolation and structural
identiﬁcation.7 In addition, we have also found a group of
novel ophiobolin sesterterpenoids and farnesylated phthalide
derivatives with broad-spectrum antibacterial activity from
Aspergillus insuetus SD-512, which was obtained from a deep-
sea cold-seep area.4 The above reports have proved that fungi
from deep-sea cold seeps might open a door to a new frontier
in the investigation of marine natural products.
In our continuing research on cold-seep-derived fungi,4−7
chemical investigations of the fungus Aspergillus chevalieri CS-
122, obtained from sulfate-reducing sediments collected from
the deep-sea cold seeps in the South China Sea that displayed
unique HPLC proﬁles in the preliminary screening, were
carried out. As a result, two novel indole diketopiperazine
derivatives chevalinulins A (1) and B (2) that possess an
unprecedented spiro[bicyclo[2.2.2]octane-diketopiperazine]
scaﬀold, together with a known analogue neoechinulin B
(3),8 were identiﬁed (Figures 1 and S1). The in vivo
proangiogenic activity of the isolated compounds was
evaluated using transgenic zebraﬁsh. Herein, the isolation,
structural elucidation, stereochemical assignment, and bio-
activity of 1 and 2 are described.

powder. Its molecular formula was deduced as C26H27O6N3 on

absorption bands for an indole diketopiperazine chromophore
at λmax of 223, 254, 283, and 348 nm.9 The 13C NMR and
DEPT data displayed 26 carbon resonances (Table S1),
accounting for 2 methyls, 2 methylenes (with 1 oleﬁnic), 11

The 1H NMR spectrum also displayed signals typical for an
ortho-substituted benzene ring (H-4 through H-7). Combined
with the HMBC cross-peaks (Figure 2), the indole moiety
could be conﬁrmed. Meanwhile, resonances for a 2-methylbut-
3-en-2-yl group adjacent to C-2 were also observed from the
1H and 13C NMR and HMBC spectra (Table S1 and Figure 2).
Detailed analysis of its 1D and 2D NMR data allowed for the
establishment of fragments 1A and 1B for compound 1 (Figure
2). Fragment 1A was deduced to be a prenylated indole
diketopiperazine derivative similar to neoechinulin B (3),8
except that the signals of two oleﬁnic carbons C-12 (δC 135.1)
and CH2-20 (δC 99.6) in the
13C NMR spectrum of

carbons, with one nonprotonated carbon (δC 61.2, C-12, in
DMSO-d6) and one methylene (δC 27.2, CH2-20, in DMSO-
d6), respectively. Also, key HMBC correlations from 14-NH to
C-12 and from H-20 to C-12 and C-13 further determined the
assignment of fragment 1A.
Fragment 1B was deduced as a 4,5-dihydroxycyclohex-1-
ene-1-carboxylic acid moiety based on the COSY cross-peaks
from H-26 to H-21 and H-25 and from H-24 to H-25 and H-
23 together with key HMBC correlations from H-24 and H-26
to C-22 and from H-23 to C-21 and C-27 (Figure 2).
Most importantly, the connection between fragments 1A
and 1B via the bond of C-20−C-21 was established by the
COSY cross-peak from H-20 to H-21. The other linkage via
the bond of C-12−C-24 was observed by key HMBC
correlations from H-20 to C-22 and C-26 and from H-21,
H-23, and H-25 to C-12. Thus, the planar structure of 1 was
constructed.
The geometry of the double bond at C-8 was determined to
be Z as the chemical shift of H-8 (δH 7.02 in DMSO-d6)
shifted downﬁeld under the inﬂuence of the deshielding eﬀect
of the C=O group.10−12 With many attempts, a single crystal of
1 suitable for X-ray diﬀraction was obtained by slowly
crystallizing in solvent MeOH at 4 °C, which not only
conﬁrmed the planar structure of 1 (Figure 3, CCDC
2142461) but also determined its relative conﬁgurations as
12R*, 21R*, 24R*, 25R*, and 26R*. However, the Flack
parameter 0.0(9) with a large standard uncertainty does not
support a rigorous assignment of the absolute conﬁguration, so
theoretical calculations of speciﬁc rotation (SR)13 were thus
performed on a pair of enantiomers (12R,21R,24R,25R,26R)-1
and (12S,21S,24S,25S,26S)-1. The calculated SR values for
(12R,21R,24R,25R,26R)-1 at BH&HLYP/TZVP, CAM-
B3LYP/TZVP, and PBE0/TZVP levels (Table S3) agreed
with the experimental SR [α]25
D + 330.0 (c 0.13, MeOH).
Thus, the absolute conﬁguration of 1 was determined as 12R,
21R, 24R, 25R, and 26R.
Compound 2 was isolated as a brown amorphous powder.
The molecular formula of 2 was established as C26H27O6N3
from the positive HRESIMS data (m/z 478.1970 [M + H]+,
calcd 478.1973), which was the same as that of 1. The 1H and
13C NMR spectra of compound 2 were very close to those of 1,
with only some minor variations on the chemical shifts for Hβ-
20, Hα-20, H-23, and H-25 and for C-10, C-12, C-20, and C-
21 (Table S1). Detailed inspection of 2D NMR data suggested
that 2, which possessed the identical planar structure to 1, was
an isomer of 1 but transformed on fragment 1B. However, it
was diﬃcult to assign the stereostructure of 2 solely by NMR
data due to its complex ring system, especially for lacking some
diagnostic NOESY correlations.
From a biosynthetic viewpoint, compound 1 might be
biosynthesized from diketopiperazine neoechinulin B (3),
which was supposed to be the dienophile,14 coupled with a
diene group of the cyclohexadiene (3R,4R)-3,4-dihydroxycy-
clohexa-1,5-diene-1-carboxylic acid (3,4-trans-CHD) through a
[4 + 2] Diels−Alder cycloaddition (Scheme 1, see the section
of the biosynthetic pathway below for more details).
Therefore, it is reasonable to infer that 2 was also formed
from the same biosynthetic precursors based on the similar
NMR data between 1 and 2. Since the diene 3,4-trans-CHD
could attack the dienophile neoechinulin B (3) with four
diﬀerent postures in two diﬀerent directions theoretically, the
two precursors could form eight isomers (A−H) in total
(Figure 4). The structure of 2 could be considered within
seven (B−H) of the eight isomers except for isomer A
(compound 1). The COSY cross-peaks among H-20/H-21/H-
26/H-25/H-24/H-23 for 2 (Figure 5) ﬁt with isomers D, F,
and G and ruled out isomers B, C, E, and H, while the key
NOE correlation from 11-NH to H-25 further excluded the
isomers D and G from the candidates (Figure 4). The COSY
and NOESY correlations were only compatible with the isomer
F, which allowed the assignment of the planar structure and
stereoconﬁguration of 2. To further conﬁrm the above
inference, NMR calculations at mPW1PW91/6-31+G(d,p)
and B3LYP/6-31G(d) levels were performed on isomers D, F,
and G. The experimental 1H and 13C NMR data of 2 matched

The putative biosynthesis of 1 and 2 (Scheme 1) might start
from the production of cyclo-L-Trp−L-Ala catalyzed by a
nonribosomal peptide synthetase (NRPS) or by a cyclo-
dipeptide synthase (CDPS), followed by monoprenylation
catalyzed by a prenyltransferase to form preechuinulin.15
Sequential dehydrogenations of preechuinulin catalyzed by a
P450 enzyme would lead to the formation of the likely
precursor neoechinulin B (3). Another key precursor cyclo-
hexadiene 3,4-trans-CHD could be aﬀorded by removing
pyruvate from chorismate, which was derived from the
shikimate pathway.16 Then, neoechinulin B (3) and 3,4-
trans-CHD underwent an intermolecular [4 + 2] Diels−Alder
cycloaddition to yield 1 and 2 from the diﬀerent attack
postures and directions.
Diels−Alder cycloadditions are demonstrated to be involved
in the biosynthesis of many novel natural products,17 and these
cycloadditions are sometimes considered as spontaneously
driven in some cases. In this study, we have detected
compounds 1 and 2 from the crude extract of A. chevalieri
CS-122 by LC-MS (Figure S10), which implied that 1 and 2
were not likely formed in the processes of isolation and
puriﬁcation. Although the precursor neoechinulin B (3) is
commonly found in many kinds of fungi,11,18,19 chevalinulins A
(1) and B (2) were only found in the deep-sea cold-seep-
derived fungus A. chevalieri CS-122 until now. Therefore,
compounds 1 and 2 were most likely naturally occurring
products that were produced by the speciﬁc strain of fungus A.
chevalieri CS-122. However, further investigations on the
biosynthetic mechanism of these compounds are also required
to verify the above deduction.
Compounds 1−3 were evaluated for in vivo proangiogenic
activity in a transgenic zebraﬁsh Tg(vegfr2:GFP) model.
Compared to the model group, in which zebraﬁsh’s
intersegmental blood vessels (ISVs) were inhibited by
PTK787, compounds 1 and 2 both remarkably increased the
number of ISVs in model zebraﬁsh at the concentrations of 40
and 80 μg/mL (Figure 6), indicating that 1 and 2 exhibited
signiﬁcant proangiogenic activity. Meanwhile, neoechinulin B
(3) only exhibited weak proangiogenic activity at the
concentration of 80 μg/mL (Figure 6), which was weaker
than those of 1 and 2 at the same concentrations.
In conclusion, chevalinulins A (1) and B (2) strikingly
possess a rare spiro[bicyclo[2.2.2]octane-diketopiperazine]
skeleton and were characterized from A. chevalieri CS-122, a
fungal strain isolated from sulfate-reducing sediments collected
at deep-sea cold seeps. Especially, the proangiogenic activities
of 1 and 2 provide additional potential leads in the treatment
of a series of diseases related to insuﬃcient angiogenesis, such
as ischemic heart disease, coronary artery disease, and stroke.
