                               An ongoing challenge in chemical research is to design catalysts that select the
   Check for updates                   outcomes of the reactions of complex molecules. Chemists rely on organocatalysts or
                                            transition metal catalysts to control stereoselectivity, regioselectivity and
                                              periselectivity (selectivity among possible pericyclic reactions). Nature achieves
                                        these types of selectivity with a variety of enzymes such as the recently discovered
                                         pericyclases—a family of enzymes that catalyse pericyclic reactions1. Most
                                         characterized enzymatic pericyclic reactions have been cycloadditions, and it has
                                   been difficult to rationalize how the observed selectivities are achieved2–13. Here we
                                        report the discovery of two homologous groups of pericyclases that catalyse distinct
                                            reactions: one group catalyses an Alder-ene reaction that was, to our knowledge,
                                         previously unknown in biology; the second catalyses a stereoselective hetero-Diels–
                                        Alder reaction. Guided by computational studies, we have rationalized the observed
                                           differences in reactivities and designed mutant enzymes that reverse periselectivities
                                   from Alder-ene to hetero-Diels–Alder and vice versa. A combination of in vitro
                                       biochemical characterizations, computational studies, enzyme co-crystal structures,
                                  and mutational studies illustrate how high regioselectivity and periselectivity are
                                       achieved in nearly identical active sites.

1. Preparation of overexpression plasmids for A. nidulans.
          Full length pdxC was amplified by PCR with three sets of primers of pMO20001-
f1/r1, pMO20001-f2/r2, pMO20001-f3/r3 using the genomic DNA of Aspergillus bombycis

NRRL26010 as the template. The three overlapping DNA fragments and NotI/SwaI-digested
pYTU1 expression vector were transformed into yeast to generate pMO20001 by yeast
homologous recombination. The genes of pdxD and pdxA were individually amplified with

primer pairs of pMO20002-f1/r1 and pMO20003-f1/r1, and ligated into expression vector
pYTR1 (pdxD) and pYTP1 (pdxA) in the sites of BamHI/SwaI to create plasmids pMO20002
(pdxD) and pMO20003 (pdxA), respectively. The genes of apdE and sdxA were amplified with

the primer pairs of pMO20011-f1/r1 and pMO90003-f1/r1 using genomic DNA of Aspergillus
nidulans and Sarocladium oryzae, respectively. Each amplified fragment was ligated into
expression vector pYTP in the sites of BamHI/SwaI to create plasmids pMO20011 (apdE) and

pMO90003 (sdxA). The promoter of gpdA and sdxA were amplified with the primer pairs of
pMO20060-f1/r1 and pMO20060-f2/r2, respectively. The two overlapping DNA fragments and
SwaI-digested pMO20001  expression  vector were  transformed  into  yeast  to  generate

pMO20060 by yeast homologous recombination. The promoter of pogpdA (from Penicillium
oxalicum) and sdxA were amplified with the primer pairs of pMO20061-f1/r1 and pMO20061-
f2/r2. The two overlapping DNA fragments and PshAI/NotI-digested pMO20002 expression

vector were transformed into yeast to generate pMO20061 by yeast homologous recombination.

2. Heterologous expression in A. nidulans.

        A. nidulans A1145 was initially grown on oatmeal agar plates containing 10 mM uracil,
0.5 µg/mL pyridoxine HCl and 2.5 µg/mL riboflavin at 30 °C for 5 days. Fresh spores of A.
nidulans were inoculated into 45 mL liquid CD media (1 L: 10 g Glucose, 50 mL 20 x Nitrate

salts, 1 mL Trace elements, pH 6.5) in 125-mL Erlenmeyer flask and germinated at 30 °C and
250 rpm for approximately 16 h. For the preparation of 20 x Nitrate salts, 120 g NaNO3, 10.4 g
KCl, 10.4 g MgSO4・7H2O, 30.4 g KH2PO4 were dissolved in 1 L double distilled water. The

100 mL trace elements with pH 6.5 contains 2.20 g ZnSO4・7H2O, 1.10 g H3BO3, 0.50 g,
MnCl2・4H2O, 0.16 g FeSO4・7H2O, 0.16 g CoCl2・5H2O, 0.16 g CuSO4•5H2O, and 0.11 g
(NH4)6Mo7O24 ・4H2O. Mycelia were harvested by centrifugation at 4000 g for 10 min, and

washed with 10 mL Osmotic buffer (1.2 M MgSO4, 10 mM sodium phosphate, pH 5.8). Then

the mycelia were transferred into 10 mL of Osmotic buffer containing 100 mg lysing enzymes

from Trichoderma and 60 mg Yatalase in a 125-mL flask. The flask was kept in shaker at 80

rpm for 4 h at 30 °C. Cells were collected in a 30 mL Corex tube and overlaid gently by 10 mL

of Trapping buffer (0.6 M sorbitol, 0.1 M Tris-HCI, pH 7.0). After centrifugation at 3,500 rpm
for 15 min at 4 °C, protoplasts were collected in the interface of the two buffers. The protoplasts

were then transferred to a sterile 15-mL falcon tube and washed by 10 mL STC buffer (1.2 M

sorbitol, 10 mM CaCl2, 10 mM Tris-HCI, pH 7.5). The protoplasts were resuspended in 1 mL

STC buffer for transformation. Then, the plasmids (see Supplementary Fig.1) were added to
60 µL protoplast suspension and the mixture incubated 60 min on ice. Then 600 µL of PEG
solution at pH 7.5 (60% PEG, 50 mM calcium chloride and 50 mM Tris-HCl) was added to the

protoplast mixture, and the mixture was incubated at room temperature for additional 20 min.

3. Analysis of metabolites and isolation of compounds.
         For small scale analysis, the transformants of A. nidulans were grown for 3 to 4 days
in 10 mL liquid CD-ST. Then, 750 µL of mycelia containing culture was extracted with 750 µL
of 90% ethyl acetate and 10% methanol mixtures. The organic phase was dried by speed
vacuum and dissolved in methanol for analysis. LC–MS analyses were performed on a
Shimadzu 2020 EV LC–MS (Kinetex 1.7 µm C18 100 Å, LC Column 100 × 2.1 mm) using
positive- and negative-mode electrospray ionization with a linear gradient of 5–95% acetonitrile
MeCN–H2O with 0.5% formic acid in 15 min followed by 95% MeCN for 3 min with a flow

rate of 0.3 ml/min.
        For isolation of compounds, transformants of A. nidulans were grown for 96 h in 4 x
1 L liquid CD-ST and then filtered to collect the cells from liquid culture. The cells were

extracted with 1 L acetone and the extracts were evaporated to dryness and partitioned between
ethyl acetate/H2O two times. After evaporation of the organic phase, the crude extracts were
separated by silica gel chromatography. Fractions containing the target compounds were

combined and used for further purification by HPLC with a semi-preparative C18 column of
Kinetics New column, 5 µm, 10 × 250 mm. For elucidation of chemical structures, 1D and 2D
NMR spectra were obtained on Bruker AV500 spectrometer  at the UCLA Molecular

Instrumentation  Center. High  resolution mass  spectra were  obtained from an  Agilent
Technologies 6545 Accurate Mass QTOF LC/MS at the UCLA Molecular Instrumentation
Center.

4. Isothermal titration calorimetry analysis of SAM binding to PdxI and EpiI
       ITC measurements were  carried  out on an  Microcal PEAQ-ITC  (Malvern)

calorimeter at 25 °C. All protein samples and SAM were in the same buffer containing 25 mM
Tris (pH 8.0), 150 mM NaCl and 3 mM DTT. The concentrated 40 µM proteins were loaded
into the cell and 400 µM of SAM were loaded into the syringe, respectively. The titration

processes were performed by injecting 40 μL aliquots of the proteins in syringe into cell at time
intervals of 2 minutes to ensure that the titration peak returned to the baseline. The titration data
were analyzed using the program Origin 8.5 from Micro Cal and fitted using the one-site

binding model.





5. Synthetic Materials and Methods.
         Unless stated otherwise, reactions were conducted in flame-dried glassware under an

atmosphere of nitrogen using anhydrous solvents (either freshly distilled or passed through
activated alumina columns). Commercially obtained reagents were used as received unless
otherwise specified. Reaction temperatures were controlled using an IKAmag temperature

modulator, and unless stated otherwise, reactions were performed at room temperature (rt,
approximately 23 °C). Thin-layer chromatography (TLC) was conducted with EMD gel 60
F254 pre-coated plates (0.25 mm) and visualized using a combination of UV, anisaldehyde,

iodine, phosphomolybdic acid and cerium (IV) sulfate in water with sulfuric acid (Seebach),
and potassium permanganate staining. Silicycle P60 (particle size 0.040–0.063 mm) silica gel
was used for flash column chromatography. 1H NMR spectra were recorded on Bruker

spectrometers (at 500 MHz) and are reported relative to deuterated solvent signals (7.26 ppm
for CDCl3, 7.16 ppm for C6D6, 4.78 ppm for CD3OD). Data for 1H NMR spectra are reported
as follows: chemical shift (d ppm), multiplicity, coupling constant (Hz), and integration. 13C
NMR spectra were recorded on Bruker spectrometers (at 125 MHz) and are reported relative to
deuterated solvent signals (77.16 ppm for CDCl3). Data for 13C NMR spectra are reported in
terms of chemical shift, and when necessary, multiplicity, coupling constant (Hz) and carbon

type.

Supplementary Figure 2. Heterologous reconstitution of the ketone 5 in A. nidulans.
          In order to obtain 5 for coupled in vitro reaction of PdxG and pericyclases, we
attempted the heterologous reconstitution of 5 in A. nidulans. Co-expression of pdxC (PKS-

NRPS) and pdxD (ER) gave the tetramic acid tolypocladenol C. However, further expression
of the ring expansion P450 pdxA did not convert 4 to 5. Thus, we turned to homologous gene
of pdxA such as apdE, a well-characterized ring expansion P4502 (60% identity). However,

again, no coversion of 4 to 5 was observed by the addition of apdE. Finally, we found a putative
ring expansion P450 SdxA from Sarocladium oryzae (89% identity) can convert 4 to 5, albeit
still with low conversion. To increase formation of 5, we expressed three copies of sdxA in A.

nidulans, which yielded 5 at about 0.5 mg/L from the large scale cultivation in CDST media.

to define the geometry of the QMs and the subsequent periselective pericyclic reactions.

Supplementary Note 2. Targeting pyridoxatin biosynthesis to find enzyme that catalyzes the

Supplementary Note 5. Additional discussion of PdxI-5 cocrystal structure.

        The binding-cavity of PdxI has a wide opening and a small total volume of ~600 Å3.

The substrate analogue 5 enters the cavity from the upper-left (Fig. 2c) and nestles its way into

the active site, past phenylalanine (F204) and tyrosine (Y205), to make hydrogen bonds with

histidine (H336) via a water molecule, lysine (K337), histidine (H161), glutamine (Q412), and

threonine (T232) and aspartate (D233) via water molecules (Fig. 2c). The substrate analogue 5

adopts an extended chain conformation in the active site of the PdxI-5 structure as seen in the

structure of LepI-ketone substrate analog, indicating PdxI could utilize a similar strategy as

LepI for the dehydration step. However, in clear contrast to LepI catalyzing the stereoselective

trans- dehydration of the alcohol to (E)-QM (Supplementary Figure 7), 5 is positioned for a

stereoselective syn-dehydration yielding (Z)-7 (Fig. 2c). By aligning the complex structure with

the LepI-substrate analogue complex structure (PDB ID: 6IX5), we can see the active site of

PdxI is composed of less residues in chain B than in LepI. More importantly, the nitrogen of

the pyridone core is hydrogen bonded with His161 in PdxI which is the opposite in LepI

(corresponding H133 doesn’t interact with the nitrogen of the pyridone core) (Supplementary

Figure. 7). In other words, the binding modes of the pyridone cores in PdxI and LepI are flipped

by 180o. The syn-dehydration would be assisted by deprotonation of the pyridone C4 alcohol

via lysine (K337) and the extensive water hydrogen bonding network with alcohol at C7

mediated by H336 and D233. Consistent with the structural interpretation, substitution of K337

to alanine completely abolished enzymatic activity and can be rescued by arginine mutation

(Extended Data Figs. 7 and 9a). This supports the role of K337 as a general base for the

dehydration step.

Supplementary Note 8. The pericyclases studied here do not require SAM.
         Since none of the pericyclases we used in this study were copurified with SAM, and

do not require SAM or SAH for catalysis, we concluded these pericyclases catalyze the Alder-
ene reaction and the O4-hetero-Diels–Alder reaction in a SAM-independent fashion. As shown
in Extended Data Fig. 4b, the presence of SAM (or SAH) does not have impact on the

periselectivity. We further showed no rate acceleration by SAM or SAH was observed in either
PdxI or EpiI-catalyzed reactions, judged from the rates of formations of 8 or 9, respectively
(Supplementary Fig. 11). The remaining question here is whether these pericyclases still retain

the SAM-binding capability. Multiple sequence alignments of the pericyclases with reported
O-methyltransferases (AsqD, GsfD, and Hmp5) indicate that the central glycine of the SAM-
binding motif (GxGxG)12 in the pericyclases is mutated to isoleucine or valine (Supplementary

Fig. 12). Thus, these pericyclases do not have an intact SAM-binding motif. Further, crystal
structures of PdxI and HpiI showed that the SAM-binding pockets of PdxI and HpiI are smaller
than that of SAM-dependent pericyclase LepI, and should not be able to accommodate SAM

(Supplementary Fig. 13). To test this, we performed isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC)
experiments on PdxI and EpiI with SAM. As expected, the result showed that no detectable
binding of SAM to PdxI or EpiI was observed (Supplementary Fig. 14). Overall, these data

indicate that PdxI and EpiI (and the homologs) cannot bind SAM, in clear contrast to LepI, thus
catalyzing the pericyclic reactions in a SAM-independent fashion.

Supplementary Note 10. Pericyclic nature of calculated theozyme models.
          Pericyclic reactions are concerted chemical transformations in which all bonding

changes occur in a cyclic array of atoms; there are no intermediates (defined by the IUPAC as
“a molecular entity with a lifetime appreciably longer than a molecular vibration,” which
corresponds to a stabilization of >0.6 kcal·mol–1 at room temperature)13 and partial bonding of

all centers in the transition state that are carried through to products. This is contrasted with
stepwise, non-pericyclic processes where bonds are formed sequentially in separate transition
states that are linked together by a stabilized intermediate; for a Diels–Alder reaction the first

transition state forms a single carbon-carbon bond that then leads to an intermediate with a
Gibbs free energy a >0.6 kcal·mol–1 (at room temperature) below the preceding transition state
that then continues on to products by traversing a second transition state in which the second

carbon-carbon bonds is formed.
          In the main text of the paper, we omitted TS-8 that leads to a transient bridged non-
classical cation from Figure 3. The entire energy surface of the calculated Alder-ene theozyme

is shown below in Supplemental Figure 16, in which all structures were confirmed to be
connected by intrinsic reaction coordinate calculations. We were intrigued by this transition
state TS-8 that shows upon lysine-mediated proton transfer to O4, the alkyl chain folds back on

itself to form a bridged non-classical cation. This bridged non-classical cation is a pre-reactive
state that is well-organized to undergo a pericyclic Alder-ene (TS-3) or hetero-Diels–Alder
(TS-4) reaction to form 8’ or 9. In the transient bridged non-classical cation, no new carbon-

carbon bonds have formed, but there are interactions that lead to a stabilized cation; the bond
lengths are significantly longer than the typical carbon-carbon bond length of 1.54 Å.
       Despite our interest in these structures, as a precaution, these structures were not

included in the main text due to the following reasons: (1) the minute ∆∆H of TS-8 and the
transient bridged non-classical cation (0.1 kcal·mol–1) are well-within the error of our
calculations (>1 kcal·mol–1); (2) structural similarity (r.m.s.d.) indicates that the geometries are

identical (<0.15). These two facts led us to believe that these structures could be insignificant
fluctuations on the calculated potential energy surface and, therefore, did not discuss them in
the main text.

     When analyzing these calculated models as pericyclic or non-pericyclic, we do note that
in the rate determining TS-3 and TS-4, there is partial bonding of all centers in the transition
state that smoothly proceed without intervention of intermediate, by the IUPAC definition, to

product.
