ABSTRACT: Tropolones are the core of numerous remarkable
bioactive natural products, such as stipitatic acid and eupenifeldin.
The key step of the biosynthetic pathways of tropolones is the ring
expansion of a six-membered ring. TropC is the first identified
enzyme capable of catalyzing the tropolone ring expansion process,
which belongs to the α-ketoglutarate-dependent dioxygenase (α-
KGD) family. However, the molecular mechanism underlying this
enzymatic process remains elusive. Here, we present biochemical
evidence indicating that SpoC, an enzyme closely related to TropC,
also catalyzes ring expansion to construct the tropolone core with
stringent catalytic specificity. Molecular insight into the oxidative
rearrangement mechanism of SpoC has been demonstrated through stable isotope labeling, protein mass spectrometry,
crystallographic analyses, mutagenesis studies, and quantum mechanical calculations. These findings support an intriguing radical
ring expansion mechanism facilitated by imine-assisted monodentate coordination of the substrate to an oxo-iron center, enabling an
efficient radical ring expansion reaction. This understanding is essential for the synthesis of various tropolone-containing natural
products, pharmaceuticals, and functional materials with unique properties.
■INTRODUCTION
Tropolones, one class of compounds containing seven-
membered aromatic rings, are the core of some essential
bioactive natural products, such as colchicine,1 stipitatonic
acid,2 and stipitatic acid3 (Figures 1a and S1). Numerous
significant natural products utilize tropolone as a biosynthetic
precursor, including but not limited to antitumor meroterpe-
noids eupenifeldin,4 pycnidione,4b−d,5 neosetophomone B,4c
and xenovulene A6 (Figures 1a and S1). Biosynthetic pathways
of tropolones have been intensively studied for over 70 years,
and the key step is the ring expansion of an alkylated six-
membered ring.7
Stipitaldehyde (1) is one of the earliest discovered
tropolones, first identified in 1942.8 However, the mechanism
of its ring expansion remained unclear until the recent
characterization of a nonheme iron α-ketoglutarate-dependent
dioxygenase (α-KGD) TropC.4a The gene that encodes TropC
was identified within a gene cluster responsible for the
biosynthesis of the fungal tropolone stipitatic acid, which was
discovered in Talaromyces stipitatus (Penicillium stipitatum)
(Figure S2a).4a In this cluster, the gene tspks1 encodes a
nonreducing polyketide synthase (NR-PKS) TropA, which
generates 3-methylorcinaldehyde (2). This compound is
subsequently hydroxylated and dearomatized by a FAD-
dependent monooxygenase TropB to produce a 1,3-dione
intermediate (3) (Figures 1b and S2a).4a TropC catalyzes an
intriguing ring expansion reaction, converting the intermediate
3 into the tropolone stipitaldehyde (1)4a (Figure 1b). Despite
these advances, the detailed enzymatic and chemical
mechanisms underlying this ring-expansion process remain to
be fully elucidated.
According to previous studies, TropC-catalyzed ring
expansion possibly occurs through two different mechanistic
pathwaysradical rearrangement (involving an uncoordinated
substrate) or semipinacol ring rearrangement (with a bis-
coordinated substrate), both originating from the shared
radical intermediate 4 (Figures 1c and S3).4a,9 One early
hypothesis suggested that the radical intermediate 4 undergoes
hydroxylation, forming the diol intermediate 5 via coordination
to FeIII−OH. The subsequent ring expansion would then
proceed through a semipinacol rearrangement (Figures 1c and
S3b). This hypothesis was based on the detection of trace
amounts of trihydroxybenzaldehyde (6), a potential oxidation
product that indirectly supports the existence of intermediate 5

(Figure S3a).4a The proposed semipinacol ring expansion
mechanism has not been experimentally verified to date.
Alternatively, a radical rearrangement mechanism was
proposed (Figure 1c).9 In this mechanism, substrate 3 interacts
with the FeIVO active center in a noncoordination manner,
generating a free radical intermediate 4, which undergoes a
radical ring expansion to produce a noncoordination product 1
or engages in a rebound hydroxylation process to generate
shunt product 6 (Figures 1c and S3c). However, in vitro
cascading reactions involving TropB and TropC using 2 as the
substrate yielded both 1 and the shunt product 6 (Figure S4b).
This cascade reaction may overlook potential interactions or
interference introduced by TropB.
In this study, we examine the mechanism of SpoC, a
homologue of TropC, in catalyzing the ring expansion
reaction. In vitro enzymatic reactions using direct substrate 3,
crystallographic analyses, site-directed mutagenesis, and
computational modeling methods were employed. An imine
bond between the ε-nitrogen of active site K110 and the
aldehyde group of the substrate/product is formed during both
crystallization and the catalytic process. This covalent linkage
enables precise substrate positioning, thereby facilitating
substrate monodentate coordination to a nonheme oxo-iron
center. Ultimately, the imine-anchored six-membered substrate
undergoes radical ring expansion to a seven-membered ring via
monodentate coordination to the oxo-iron center. These
findings reveal that SpoC utilizes a novel covalent and
coordinated substrate to catalyze the radical ring rearrange-
ment reaction, resulting in the efficient synthesis of the
tropolone core of stipitaldehyde (1) without producing the
shunt product 6 (Figure 1c). These results provide new
insights into the radical rearrangement pathway of α-KGDs
and pave the way for leveraging these pathways in the
biosynthesis of biologically active natural products, particularly
those with challenging tropolone-based scaffolds.

■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
SpoC Catalyzes Ring Expansion to Form Stipitalde-
hyde (1). To investigate the molecular mechanism underlying
ring expansion in tropolone biosynthesis, we concurrently
investigated the structural and biochemical properties of
TropC and its homologue SpoC, which share 56.1% sequence
identity (Figure S5). Both TropC and SpoC are typical α-
KGDs.10 The α-KGDs family is widely distributed in bacteria,
fungi, plants, and vertebrates.10d,11 Among these, only several
enzymes, such as DAOCS,12 EasH,13 AusE,14 PrhA,14a,15
BrvJ,16 and AndA,17 catalyze complex rearrangement reactions
which play an essential role in the formation of the skeleton of
bioactive molecules (Figure S6).
SpoC, from fungi Sporormiella sp., was found to be involved
in the formation of C−C coupled orsellinic acid derivative
dimers, such as Sporormielone A (Figure S2).18 In contrast
with TropC, SpoC was thought to catalyze hydroxylation of
the 3-methyl group of 3 only (Figure S2b),18a but this has not
been experimentally proven until now.
Recombinant TropC and its homologue SpoC were
heterogeneously expressed in Escherichia coli for subsequent
crystallization and in vitro analysis (Figure S7). TropC
catalyzes the production of 1 from 3 in vitro, as reported4a,9
(Figure S8). In marked contrast to what has been reported,
SpoC also catalyzes ring expansion of 3, producing 1 in vitro
(Figures S8−S9). No significant difference was observed in the
ring expansion capacity between SpoC and TropC (Figure S8).

This evidence strongly supports that SpoC possesses the
capability to catalyze ring rearrangement in vitro (Figure 2a).
This ring expansion reaction, catalyzed by SpoC, is highly
efficient. The substrate 3 (2 mM) can be completely converted
into product 1 by SpoC (100 μM) in 1 h at 28 °C, and almost
no byproducts were observed, as evidenced by time-course in
vitro assays (Figure S9). This is similar to Acn2 or XenC,
ancestral proteins of TropC; the ring-expansion reaction
catalyzed by them hardly produces shunt products.19 Shunt
product trihydroxybenzaldehyde (6) was identified in in vitro
cascading reactions involving TropB and TropC using 2 as
substrate9 (Figure S4b). It was also observed in reactions
catalyzed by crude cell lysates of other engineered ancestral
proteins of TropC in the presence of TropB reaction19(Figure
S4b). However, TropB not only uses substrate 2 to produce 3
(Figure S10) but also exhibits unexpected catalytic activity to
unnatural substrate stipitaldehyde 1 that leads to the formation
of a novel product having a congruent mass spectrum and UV
characteristics with shunt product 6 in our in vitro experiment
(Figures S11−S13). The molecular formula of 6 was
determined to be C8H7O4, as suggested by the (+)-HRESIMS
data (m/z 167.03511 [M −H]−, calcd for C8H7O4:
167.03498, Figure S14). We proposed that TropB catalyzes
the oxidative ring contraction converting compound 1 to 6 by
a mechanism analogous to that reported for the FAD-
dependent monooxygenase AsL4, AsL6, and EupR5 in
tropolone meroterpenoid ring contractions5b (Figure S15).
These data indicate that 6 is an interference product
introduced by TropB rather than a shunt product resulting
from the oxidative ring expansion of 3.
The crystal structures of the apo form of TropC and SpoC
were solved. The structure of TropC we determined has the
same space group and close cell parameters as the published
one (PDB ID: 6XJJ).9 Their overall structures are nearly
identical, with a root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) value of
only 0.14 Å (for 275 pairs of Cα atoms). SpoC exhibited a
highly similar overall structure to TropC (6XJJ) with the
RMSD value of 0.65 Å (for 233 pairs of Cα atoms) (Figure
S16). Like other α-KGDs, SpoC adopts a conserved double-
stranded β-helix fold (DSBH), which has eight β-sheets (III−
X) forming a squashed barrel, serving as the core architecture
(Figure S17). In addition, this core is extended by four β-
strands (I−II and XI-XII) at the N-terminal and C-terminal
regions (Figure S17). We modeled an iron ion in the active site
based on the conserved requirement for the Fe ion as a
catalytic cofactor in α-KGDs.11e This rationale is supported by
the strong fit of the iron ion to the electron density map during
structure refinement and by the potential binding of
endogenous trace Fe2+ to recombinant protein SpoC during
its heterologous expression in E. coli (without exogenous metal
supplementation in purification/crystallization buffers). The Fe
ion is bound by the 2-His-1-Asp facial triad (H214, D216, and
H273), a conserved HXD/E···H motif for the metal ion in α-
KGDs family members (Figure S18).10b−d This motif is
essential for catalysis, and the corresponding alanine single-
point mutation of any of the three residues in the motif
(H214A/D216A/H273A) resulted in the complete loss of
SpoC activity (Figure 2e).
Collectively, this demonstrates that TropC and SpoC exhibit
similarity not only in sequence but also in their crystal
structure and in vitro catalytic behavior. Like TropC, SpoC
facilitates the ring expansion of 3 to form 1.
Substrate Recognition of SpoC. As in most α-KGDs, α-
KG is essential for TropC and SpoC turnover,10b−d and no
product was detected without α-KG in the in vitro experiments
(Figure S8). Attempts to crystallize a premixed sample of α-
KG and enzyme yielded crystals; however, analysis revealed
them to be ligand-free enzyme, which might be due to the
rapid dissociation of α-KG or its fast turnover to succinate.
The enzyme was then cocrystallized with the commonly used
nonreactive α-KG analogue N-oxalylglycine (NOG) and the
frequently used Fe2+ surrogate Mn2+ to obtain a NOG-SpoC
complex20 (Figures 2b,c and S19−S20). NOG induces little
difference in the overall conformation compared with α-KG.21
Mn2+ exhibits coordination behavior similar to that of Fe2+.
Both metal ions commonly adopt octahedral geometries and
are typically coordinated by His/Asp residues.22
In the NOG-SpoC structure, the Mn ion is coordinated by
the H214XD216···H273 motif, and NOG coordinates with the Mn
ion in a bidentate manner through its oxo- and a terminal
oxygen atom of its oxalyl moiety (Figure 2c). A water molecule
completes the metal coordination sphere, leading to a six-
coordinate octahedral geometry (Figure 2c). The NOG is also
stabilized by hydrogen bonds with the side chains of Y198 and
S285 (Figure 2b). In addition, several hydrophobic residues,
including L196, L223, V211, and F289, form a hydrophobic
pocket in the active site (Figure 2c).
Sequence alignment shows that all of the residues involved
in NOG binding are highly conserved in different species
containing tropolone homologue clusters and other homolo-
gous α-KGDs (Figure S21). Mutations Y198A, Y198F, and
S285A resulted in an approximately 80% reduction in activity
(Figure 2e). It demonstrates that the hydrogen interactions
between Y198 or S285 and cosubstrate are important for
catalysis (Figure 2e). Mutation of a single hydrophobic amino
acid to Ala, such as L196A, V211A, and L223A, did not
severely impair the activity (Figure 2e). F289 contributes more
to the hydrophobic binding pocket, so the F289A variant
largely lost its activity, while mutant F289Y, which also has a
hydrophobic aromatic ring, retained 60% activity.
The structures of the enzyme−substrate would provide more
critical information to further study the mechanism of SpoC in
ring expansion. It is possible that substrate binding requires
either prior binding of α-KG or cobinding with α-KG to
stabilize its interaction with SpoC. Attempts to cocrystallize
the complex of SpoC and 3 all failed, and only the apo
structure of SpoC was obtained, even when the substrate-to-
protein ratio was increased to 20:1. Therefore, we modeled α-
KG-3-bound SpoC by docking substrate 3 to the NOG-SpoC
structure (Figure 2d). According to this model, the substrate 3
and α-KG bind simultaneously to SpoC and 3 is bound into a
large hydrophobic cavity close to α-KG. This cavity is primarily
contributed by the two aromatic amino acids F289 and F217.
The corresponding mutants F289A and F217A exhibited
remarkably decreased activity; F289Y and F217Y, which
maintained partial hydrophobic interactions, retained nearly
half of the activity (Figure 2e). R194, a potentially conserved
amino acid residue for α-KG binding in other reported α-
KGDs,11a forms a hydrogen bond with 3 (Figures 2d and S22−
S24). Alanine mutation R194A or an amino acid with opposite
charge variant R194D has no activity (Figures 2e and S25).
Mutations of R194 to similarly charged basic amino acids,
whether with a long side chain (R194K) or a shorter side chain
(R194H), still resulted in the complete loss of SpoC activity
(Figure S25). These findings indicate that R194 may play a

The Active Site and Imine Linkage for Ring
Expansion. Efforts to cocrystallize the substrate-cosubstrate-
enzyme complex were made by mixing SpoC, 3, and α-KG
together during crystallization. Instead of obtaining the
structure of the substrate complex, we obtained the structure
of the product 1-SpoC complex (Figures S26−S30), which is
reasonable because of the robust activity of SpoC even in
crystallization conditions (Figure S31).
In the structure of 1-SpoC, K110 and Y198 are vital amino
acid residues for the binding of 1 (Figure 3a). The oxygen
atom at the C4 hydroxyl in 1 forms a hydrogen bond with the
OH of Y198 (Figure 3a), and the relative mutants Y198F/A
have lowered activity, as described before (Figure 2e).
Additionally, sequence alignment shows that Y198 is strictly
conserved in α-KGDs (Figure S21) and is often involved in the
binding of α-KG, as we have seen in our NOG-SpoC structure
(Figure 2c).
The clear and continuous electron density map shows that
an imine bond (CN) was formed between the aldehyde
group at C9 of product 1 and the amino group of K110
(Figure 3b). Protein mass spectrometry (MS) studies involving
the incubation of SpoC with product 1 in vitro were performed
to confirm this covalent linkage. Initial characterization of 100
μM SpoC yielded the most prominent mass peak of 41,249 Da,
matching the calculated mass of apoenzyme (Figures 3d and
S32). Upon incubation with 2 mM product 1, a new peak at
41,411 Da emerged (+162 Da mass shift), consistent with
imine bond formation between product 1 and SpoC (Figures
3d and S32). The characteristic mass shift (+162 Da) was also
observed when SpoC was incubated with substrate 3 under
standard reaction conditions for 90 min, confirming the
conversion of substrate 3 to product 1, which is covalently
linked to SpoC (Figures 3d and S33). This two-stage mass
transition (apo enzyme - imine adduct) in mass spectrum
assays provides definitive evidence for the covalent interaction
of 1 and SpoC.
The persistent dark yellow color (the identification color of
product 1) in the enzyme pellet after reaction termination and
centrifugation was observed in our in vitro assays (Figure S34),
which is consistent with previously reported in vitro data of
TropC.23 This observation further suggests that the imine
bond is formed between SpoC and the product during catalysis
and is relatively stable toward hydrolysis. Consequently,
preincubation with the product significantly reduced SpoC
activity (Figure S35), likely due to this stable covalent
interaction formation.

adduct formation. To capture the 3-SpoC imine adduct
intermediate during catalysis, we performed MS analysis of
SpoC incubated with substrate 3 under reaction conditions
where the reactive Fe2+ was replaced by nonreactive Mn2+ (to
prevent catalysis). The detected mass shift (+164 Da) precisely
matched the theoretical value for substrate conjugation via
imine bond formation, followed by dehydration (Figure S36).
This result provides direct evidence that the Schiff-base
intermediate forms between substrate 3 and the catalytic
lysine residue (K110) of SpoC. Furthermore, SpoC and
substrate 3 were coincubated under standard reaction
conditions (with Fe2+ and α-KG) for short periods, followed
by MS analysis. Short-term incubation for 15 min (quenched
with 10 mM EDTA) revealed two coexisting adducts: the
substrate 3-SpoC imine adduct (+164 Da) and the product 1-
SpoC adduct (+162 Da) (Figures 3d and S36). These data
establish a covalent catalytic trajectory: an initial nucleophilic
attack by K110 on substrate 3 generates the substrate-enzyme
intermediate, which is subsequently converted to the product-
enzyme adduct (Figure 3d).
To confirm that only K110 can undergo Schiff base
formation in reaction among the 22 lysine residues in SpoC,
the point mutant K110A was subjected to the same analysis,
which did not lead to any detectable imine adducts (detected
41,191 Da vs calculated 41,192.90 Da for the apoenzyme)
(Figures 3d and S37). Peptide analysis of 1-incubated SpoC by
tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) further confirmed the
covalent modification at K110 by 1 (Figure S38).
To further support that K110 is indispensable for covalent
bond formation, we reperformed systematic enzymatic assays
using K110 variants. Substituting K110 with nonpolar, polar,
charged, or uncharged residues such as Alanine, Histidine,
Glutamine, Arginine, and Asparagine all resulted in the
complete loss of activity of the enzyme (Figure S39),
demonstrating that other residues (Alanine, Arginine,
Tyrosine, Aspartic Acid, Glutamic Acid, Asparagine, Gluta-
mine, Histidine) at position 110 can not mediate the imine
bond formation essential for catalysis.
Notably, even though both lysine (K) and arginine (R) can
function as a general base via hydrogen bonding, mutant
K110R is also inactive (Figures S39 and S40). Consistent with
it, the protein mass spectrometry analysis shows that imine
adducts were not detectable in the K110R mutant when
incubated with substrate 3 in the enzymatic conditions
(detected 41,278 Da vs calculated 41,278.00 Da for the
apoenzyme) (Figure S37). This conclusively confirms that the
lysine ε-amine’s unique nucleophilicityunattainable by
arginine substitution, is mechanistically required for covalent
intermediate formation.
Sequence alignment shows that K110 is unique among α-
KGDs associated with ring expansion of tropolone, while it is
not conserved in other α-KGDs catalyzing hydroxylation
(Figure S24). This indicates that K110 is a key and unique
amino acid residue for tropolone-related α-KGDs, particularly
in the activity of ring expansion.
Comprehensive analysis reveals that the formation of the
covalent bonding capability of K110 is a prerequisite for
catalytic activity. This capacity implies that the substrate or
intermediate may preform an imine bond with K110, allowing
for precise positioning at the active site, thereby facilitating
catalysis. However, since the substrate exists in its enol form at
the initial state (unable to directly form a covalent bond with
the K110 ε-amine group), we propose that arginine-assisted
deprotonation likely mediates this covalent interaction. The
parallel inactivation of both R194A and R194K mutants
robustly supports this hypothesis and highlights the central
role of R194 within the proposed catalytic cycle, as neither
steric substitution (R194A) nor isosteric charge preservation
(R194K) restores activity (Figures S40).
Collectively, these data establish a synergistic R194-K110
catalytic model. R194 stabilizes the substrate/intermediate
through hydrogen bonding and effectively deprotonates it,
facilitating conversion of the enol tautomer to the reactive
aldehyde intermediate. This aldehyde then undergoes a
nucleophilic attack by the ε-amino group of K110, forming
the essential imine bond required for catalysis.
Catalytic Mechanism. The oxygen atoms of C2 hydroxyl
and C1 ketone groups in compound 1 are coordinated to the
Fe ion in a bidentate manner (Figure 3a) (1 was numbered in
Figure 2a). The binding mode of 1 to the Fe ion is similar to
NOG, and the structural alignment revealed the overlapping of
product 1 and cosubstrate analogue NOG (Figure 3c). This
observed binding pose of product 1 indicates that the
substrates/intermediates were coordinated to the Fe ion in
the active site during catalysis. Critically, the K110-mediated
imine bond between ligand and enzyme is biochemically
essential, stabilizing the substrate/intermediates positioning
and thereby facilitating coordination to the Fe center.
Consequently, the methyl oxidation and ring expansion are
likely to occur within a preorganized Ligand−Metal coordina-
tion environment.
The binding mode of the coproduct also supports the
Ligand−Metal coordination model. Arginine (Arg) is often
near the active site to direct the positioning of the oxygen
ligand of α-KG in several α-KGDs,21,25 such as hydroxylase
NcT7H11aa structural homologue of SpoC (RMSD value of
1.06 Å for 206 pairs of Cα atoms). Surprisingly, conserved
R194 and R283, whose counterparts R190 and R286
contribute to cosubstrate binding in α-KGD NcT7H,11a do
not interact with NOG in our structure (Figures S22−S23).
This binding mode without hydrogen bonding/ionic inter-
action with arginine suggests that NOG binds relatively
loosely. In line with this observation, the R283A mutation in
SpoC results in a moderate decrease in activity (Figure 2e).
Tight binding of α-KG in PHD2 results in its reacting slowly
with O2.10d,26 In contrast, the relatively loose binding of NOG
in the NOG-SpoC structure may imply rapid turnover of α-KG
in SpoC, thereby providing sufficient space for the substrate to
coordinate with Fe.
The first step of α-KGDs’ catalytic cycle, the oxidative
decarboxylation of α-KG, has been widely investigated.10d,27
Usually, α-KGDs couple the oxidative decarboxylation of α-
KG (to form succinate and CO2) to the activation of molecular
oxygen, generating a reactive FeIVO that can abstract a
hydrogen atom (H•) from the substrate to generate a radical
intermediate.10d,11e,27c,28 A variety of chemical transformations,
including hydroxylation, desaturation, epimerization, halogen-
ation, oxidative ring expansion, and ring closure, can be
catalyzed by α-KGDs from this radical intermedia-
te.10a,d,11d,e,14a In our proposed Ligand−Metal coordination
model, after the formation of FeIVO intermediate, the
substrate 3 in the pocket would soon coordinate to Fe due to
the electrophilic attraction of the high-valent oxo-iron(IV)
species, being anchored by the imine linkage with K110
simultaneously. Then, the substrate undergoes a hydrogen

rearrangement mechanism.
To elucidate the mechanism, we carried out a series of
Quantum Mechanics (QM) computations on a cluster model
based on the crystal structure of product 1-SpoC complex
(Figure 4).27b,29 This model incorporated 10 amino acid
residues and 6 explicit water molecules (one of them remains
coordinated to the iron center throughout the process) to
simulate the catalytic environment of the enzyme. Results from
QM computations are listed in Figure 4. We divided the whole
process into three phases, which were the initial methyl
oxidation, radical-type ring expansion, and radical hydrox-
ylation. For the methyl oxidation phase, two Fe multiplicities
were evaluated (quintet: black dashed line; septet: black solid
line in Figure 4). The hydrogen abstraction barrier was 16.7
kcal/mol for the quintet state (TS15) versus 1.5 kcal/mol for
the septet state (TS17). This significantly lower septet barrier
indicates a facile process at room temperature.
Following methyl radical generation, the intermediate
INT27 preferentially underwent radical-mediated ring ex-
pansion rather than radical hydroxylation, as evidenced by
substantial energy disparities between competing transition
states: TS27 (ΔG‡ = 11.1 kcal/mol for INT27 →TS27) vs
TS37 (20.4 kcal/mol for INT27 →TS37). This energy
disparity (ΔΔG‡ = −9.3 kcal/mol) strongly favors the radical
ring expansion pathway, leading to INT37, suppressing the
formation of hydroxylated intermediate INT47. These results
suggest that the coordination dynamics of iron could facilitate
the ring-expansion reaction but bypass undesired C−H
hydroxylation. This mechanism is similar to those reported
for other α-KGDS, such as TaqL30 and H6H,30b,31 which
utilize substrate coordination to pentacoordinate iron centers
to stabilize transition states while suppressing hydroxylation
side reactions to promote C−O/N coupling. Once INT37 was
generated, the reverse reaction to INT27 via TS27 is kinetically
prohibitive due to the high backward barrier (ΔG‡ = 28.1 kcal/
mol for INT37 →TS27). The INT37 would be oxidated by
Fe(III) via TS47 (ΔG‡ = 21.8 kcal/mol for INT37 →TS47),
generating intermediate INT57. This is followed by an
exothermic intersystem crossing process (ISC) from INT57
to INT55 (ΔG = −41.2 kcal/mol), yielding the final hemiketal
product. Additionally, we also perform quantum chemical
calculations using cluster models for the noncovalently bound

■CONCLUSIONS
Experimental observations and QM calculations suggest that
SpoC employs a substrate-coordinated (to oxo-Fe) radical ring
expansion mechanism facilitated by an imine covalent linkage
during its catalytic process. Based on these findings, a
coordinated radical ring expansion mechanism is proposed
(Scheme 1b): (I) The prime substrates 3 and α-KG
simultaneously bind to SpoC, forming a ternary complex that
activates O2. The substrate 3 is positioned in the α-KG-
proximal binding pocket. R194 stabilizes substrate 3 via
hydrogen bonding and facilitates its deprotonation to generate
an aldehyde intermediate (Figure S53). Subsequently, this
aldehyde intermediate reacts with K110 to form a catalytically
essential Schiff base linkage. (II) Following the decarboxylation
of the α-KG and generation of the FeIVO intermediate, the
substrate is recruited to the reactive site, covalent anchoring via
the K110-Schiff base, and coordinating to the Fe ion. (III) A
classical hydrogen atom abstraction from the methyl group at
C7 of 3 by FeIVO leads to the generation of a radical
intermediate 4 and FeIII−OH. (IV) The radical intermediate 4
undergoes a imine bond-assisted, nonheme Fe-coordinated
radical ring rearrangement, expanding to a seven-membered
ring. (V) The product coordinates to the Fe ion in a bidentate
manner and tautomerizes to 1, which is stabilized by a covalent
interaction with K110 and a hydrogen interaction with Y198,
as observed in the crystal structure (Figure 3b). (VI) The
protonated R194 acts as an intrinsic proton shuttle that
delivers a proton directly to the imine group. This process
catalyzes hydrolysis of the K110-imine bond, enabling release
of the covalently bound product 1thereby regenerating the
free enzyme for subsequent catalytic turnovers (Figure S54).
In conclusion, the α-ketoglutarate-dependent dioxygenase,
SpoC, efficiently and specifically catalyzes the biosynthesis of
tropolone stipitaldehyde (1) in vitro, without forming a
hydroxylation byproduct. It operates via a mechanistically
distinct radical rearrangement, characterized by two unique
substrate-engagement features: (i) a conserved residue pair
R194-K110-mediated imine bond formation that stabilizes the
substrate’s catalytic conformation and (ii) direct coordination
of the substrate to the oxo-iron. This study offers novel
mechanistic insights into the ring expansion process of α-KG-
dependent dioxygenases. These findings not only deepen our
understanding of this type of enzymatic reaction but also
provide valuable guidance for its application in organic
biosynthesis and pharmaceutical development.
