ustilaginoidin mycotoxins in Ustilaginoidea virens

Fuhao Cui1 and Wenxian Sun

Summary
Ustilaginoidins, toxic to plants, animals and human,
are one of major types of mycotoxins produced by
Ustilaginoidea virens. In this study, a gene cluster
containing the polyketide synthase gene UvPKS1 was
analysed
via
gene
replacement
and
biochemical
studies to determine ustilaginoidin biosynthetic path-
way in U. virens. UvPKS1 was ﬁrst proven to be
responsible for the ﬁrst step of ustilaginoidin biosyn-
thesis, since neither ustilaginoidin derivatives nor
intermediates
were produced
when UvPKS1 was
deleted. Replacement of ugsO greatly reduced ustila-
ginoidin production but increased the ratios of dehy-
drogenated/hydrogenated
ustilagioidin
derivatives.
The enhanced growth rate of the ΔugsO mutant indi-
cates that accumulation of certain ustilaginoidin deriva-
tives may adversely affect mycelial growth in U. virens.
Deletion of ugsT encoding a putative MFS transporter
disrupted the ability to generate ustilaginoidins. The
ustilaginoidin derivatives produced in the ΔugsJ mutant
all lack C3-methyl, indicating that UgsJ is responsible
for C3-methylation. Only monomeric intermediates, such
as 3-methyl-dihydro-nor-rubrofusarin, but no ustilaginoi-
din derivatives were generated in the ΔugsL mutant,
indicating that UgsL is responsible for the dimerization
of nor-rubrofusarin derivatives to produce ustilaginoi-
dins. However, ugsR2 deletion had no dramatic effect
on ustilaginoidin biosynthesis. Together, biochemical
analyses with bioinformatics and chemoinformatics
uncover a multiple-step enzyme-catalysed pathway
for ustilaginoidin biosynthesis in U. virens.
Introduction
Rice false smut (RFS), caused by the ascomycete Ustilagi-
noidea virens Takahashi (teleomorph: Villosiclava virens
Tanaka and Tanaka), has recently become one of the most
destructive diseases in major rice-growing regions world-
wide, particularly in China, India and Southeast Asia
(Tanaka et al., 2008; Guo et al., 2012). U. virens colonizes
around living ovaries through infecting stamen ﬁlaments of
rice ﬂorets and forms a number of sporeballs in rice spike-
lets (Tang et al., 2013; Hu et al., 2014). The disease causes
a signiﬁcant yield loss in epidemic years. More importantly,
chlamydospore balls formed during U. virens infection con-
taminate rice grains by generating two major types of myco-
toxins
including
the
cyclopeptide
ustiloxins
and
the
polyketide ustilaginoidins (Guo et al., 2012; Lu et al., 2014;
2015). Ustiloxins exhibit an antimitotic activity to inhibit
microtubule assembly and cell skeleton formation in plants
and animals (Koiso et al., 1994; Luduena et al., 1994;
Hutton and White, 1997; Morisaki et al., 1998). Ustilaginoi-
dins, a family of bis-naphtho-γ-pyrone mycotoxins, were
ﬁrst isolated from false smut balls in the 1930s (Sumiki and
Yabuta, 1937). To date, 26 ustilaginoidin derivatives,
namely ustilaginoidins A-W and E1, isochaetochromin B2
and 2,3-dihydroustilaginoidin T have been isolated and their
structures have been determined (Shibata et al., 1963;
Shibata and Ogihara, 1963; Lu et al., 2014; Sun et al.,
2017). Ustilaginoidins have been previously shown to have
a certain toxicity and teratogenicity to embryonic limb bud
and midbrain cells in mouse (Tsuchiya et al., 1987). Ustila-
ginoidins A was revealed to uncouple the oxidative phos-
phorylation, and thus, strongly inhibiting mitochondrial
respiration (Kawai et al., 1991). Ustilaginoidins D and E
exhibit a strong effect on mouth epidermal carcinoma cells
in human by inhibiting the synthesis of DNA, RNA and pro-
teins (Koyama et al., 1988). Ustilaginoidins O, E and F
exhibit a moderate ability to inhibit radicle elongation of ger-
minating seeds (Lu et al., 2015).
Polyketides are a class of secondary metabolites pro-
duced by different organisms ranging from prokaryotes
to eukaryotes. Many polyketides produced by fungi are

mycotoxins (Yang et al., 1996; Hranueli et al., 2001;
Walsh, 2004). Fungal polyketides are usually produced
by the type I polyketide synthases (PKSs), which consist
of
multiple
functional
domains
including
β-ketoacyl
synthase (KS), acyl transferase (AT), acyl carrier protein
(ACP), dehydratase (DH), β-ketoacyl reductase (KR) and
thioesterase (TE) domains (Khosla et al., 1999). A fungal
PKS gene generally forms a tandem gene cluster with
other tightly linked genes encoding transcription factors,
transporters and tailoring enzymes involved in the modiﬁ-
cation of the primary PKS product (Weissman, 2009). For
example, the biosynthesis of aﬂatoxins (AFs), a carcino-
genic secondary metabolite, produced by Aspergillus ﬂa-
vus and A. parasiticus is controlled by a 70 kb-long gene
cluster including a pksA and 24 other genes (Yu et al.,
1995; Bhatnagar et al., 2003). Bikaverin is a tetracyclic
nonaketide-derived metabolite in Fusarium fujikuroi. Its
biosynthesis is mediated by the nonreducing PKS gene
bik1, which is located in a cluster together with ﬁve other
bik genes (Linnemannstöns et al., 2002; Wiemann et al.,
2009). The aur gene cluster consisting of PKS12 and
11 other genes has been revealed to be responsible for
the biosynthesis of the red mycelium pigment aurofusarin
in F. graminearum (Malz et al., 2005). The synthesis of
the bibenzoquinone oosporein is regulated by an OpS
gene cluster in the ascomycetous insect pathogen Beau-
veria bassiana. In this cluster, the polyketide synthase
OpS1 catalyses to form the precursor orsellinic acid,
which is subject to OpS4 hydroxylation and OpS7 oxida-
tion to form benzenetetrol. Oosporein is generated from
benzenetetrol dimerization under the catalysis of the lac-
case OpS5. The gene cluster is transcriptionally regu-
lated by the transcription factor OpS3 (Feng et al., 2015).
Ustilaginoidins are the ﬁrst isolated and identiﬁed polyke-
tides in U. virens. Previous studies focused on the isolation,
characterization and biological activities of ustilaginoidins.
However, little is known about the genes responsible for the
biosynthesis of these metabolites. The recently-released
U. virens genome facilitates the prediction of the gene clus-
ter responsible for ustilaginoidin biosynthesis in U. virens
(Zhang et al., 2014). Since the core structures of ustilaginoi-
din and chaetochromin are highly similar, it is speculated
that ustilaginoidins are most likely generated by dimeriza-
tion of two nor-rubrofusarin molecules. Therefore, the gene
cluster containing UvPKS1 (UV_2086) was predicted to be
responsible for the biosynthesis and modiﬁcation of ustilagi-
noidins and for transcriptional regulation of the gene cluster
(Zhang et al., 2014).
In this study, multiple knockout mutants of the predicted
ustilaginoidin biosynthetic genes in U. virens were generated
and subsequently their ability to generate ustilaginoidins was
determined by high performance liquid chromatography
(HPLC). The polyketide synthase UvPKS1 was identiﬁed to
catalyse the formation of monomeric primary products for
ustilaginoidin biosynthesis. The intermediate products in the
ugsJ and ugsL knockout mutants were also identiﬁed to be
ustilaginoidins F/G/A and 3-methyl-dihydro-nor-rubrofusarin
using mass spectrometry (MS) and nuclear magnetic reso-
nance (NMR) spectra, respectively. Through these genetic
and biochemical studies, we conﬁrmed that the ugs gene
cluster containing UvPKS1 is responsible for ustilaginoidin
biosynthesis in U. virens and proposed a model for the ustila-
ginoidin biosynthetic pathway. These results lay a solid foun-
dation for further functional studies on ustilaginoidins in
U. virens physiology and virulence.
Results
Prediction of the PKS gene cluster
By analogy with the aur gene cluster in F. graminearum, the
UvPKS1 gene cluster was predicted to be responsible for
the biosynthesis of ustilaginoidins in U. virens (Zhang et al.,
2014). Gene annotations in this cluster were updated here
according to a new version of U. virens genome. The gene
cluster was renamed as ugs (for ustilaginoidins synthesis)
cluster (Fig. 1A). According to antiSMASH, the ugs gene
cluster spanning over 49.7 kb contains 14 genes including a
newly-annotated gene UV_2079.5. The aurF and aurL2
genes in F. graminearum are absent from the ugs gene clus-
ter, while ﬁve ugs genes are absent from the aur gene clus-
ter (Fig. 1A, Table 1). The UvPKS1 protein, a putative type I
polyketide synthase, carries the starter ACP transcylase
(SAT), KS, AT, ACP and TE domains arranged from N- to
C-termini (Fig. 1B). The synthase is predicted to be a nonre-
ducing PKS and lacks the enoyl reductase, DH and KR
domains responsible for reduction of a polyketide backbone
(Hertweck, 2009). Besides, the ugsT, ugsO, ugsJ and ugsL
genes in this cluster are predicted to encode major facilitator
superfamily (MFS) transporter, the catalytic enzymes oxido-
reductase, methyltransferase and laccase required for the
modiﬁcation of the primary PKS products, respectively
(Table 1).

synthesis, the gene replacement mutants were generated
via homologous recombination. The upstream and down-
stream ﬂanking sequences (~1 kb) of UvPKS1, ugsO, ugsJ,
ugsL and ugsR2 were PCR-ampliﬁed and were then sub-
cloned into the binary vector pGKO2-hyg after restriction
digestion (Khang et al., 2005) (Supporting Information
Fig. S1A). The plasmid constructs were introduced into
U. virens conidia via Agrobacterium tumafaciens-mediated
transformation (ATMT). The hygromycin B phosphotransfer-
ase (hph) and herpes simplex virus thymidine kinase
(HSVtk) genes in pGKO2-hyg are used for dual selection of

To increase the efﬁciency of gene replacement, the
clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats
(CRISPR)/Cas9 system was then utilized here. In this
modiﬁed system, Cas9 speciﬁcally binds to the target
DNA under the direction of single guide RNA (sgRNA)
and creates double-strand breaks in nearby genomic
DNA (Sander and Joung, 2014). Double-strand breaks
will induce homologous recombination and thus enhance
the efﬁciency of gene replacement. The ugsT-speciﬁc
sgRNA oligos were designed and inserted into the BsmB
I-digested vector pCas9-tRp-gRNA after annealing (Liang
et al., 2018). The upstream and downstream ﬂanking
sequences (~1 kb) of ugsT were also ampliﬁed and fused
to 50- and 30-termini of the hph gene by fusion PCR,
respectively. The constructed plasmid and fusion PCR
products were introduced into the protoplasts isolated
from the wild-type strain P1. The events after homolo-
gous recombination were screened via PCR and the
gene-replacement mutants were conﬁrmed by Southern
blot analysis (Supporting Information Fig. S2F). The efﬁ-
ciency of ugsT replacement was greatly increased up to
15.7%. RT-PCR analyses further conﬁrmed that the six
genes UvPKS1, ugsO, ugsJ, ugsL, ugsT and ugsR2
were no longer transcribed in the respective mutants
(Supporting Information Fig. S8B).
UvPKS1 is the key enzyme responsible for the
biosynthesis of ustilaginoidins
The ability to generate ustilaginoidins of the wild-type,
mutant and complementary strains was assessed by
HPLC and MS. The wild-type, mutant and complementary
strains were all cultured on PSA medium plates and ustila-
ginoidin intermediates and derivatives were extracted from
the collected hyphae using ethyl acetate (EtOAc) (Meng
et al., 2015). The extracts were dissolved in methanol and
subjected to HPLC analysis. Eight peaks (compounds
1–8) were detected in the wild-type culture, which were
identiﬁed as ustilaginoidins A, D–G and ustilaginoidins
M–O, respectively (Lu et al., 2015) (Fig. 2).
The UvPKS1 gene consists of three exons separated by
two introns, with a total coding sequence of 6315 bp,
which encodes a protein of 2104 amino acids. Targeted
replacement of UvPKS1 resulted in a light yellow pheno-
type on the backside of the mutant culture while the
wild-type strain exhibited a brownish-yellow colony after
culturing on the PSA plates for 4 weeks. Neither ustilagi-
noidin derivatives nor other intermediates were detectable
in the ΔUvPKS1 strain via HPLC analysis (Fig. 2A). The
plasmid-based complementation with gene expression
driven by the RP27 promoter (Zhang et al., 2018) partially
restored the ability to produce ustilaginoidins (Fig. 2A).
The results indicate that UvPKS1 encodes a functional
polyketide synthase that catalyses the ﬁrst step of ustilagi-
noidin biosynthesis, in which acetyl-CoA and malonyl-CoA
are cyclized to form the primary PKS product.
UgsO is involved in the redox modiﬁcation of
ustilaginoidins
UgsO is predicted to generate a 1.8 kb transcript after
three putative introns are spliced, which encodes a puta-
tive FAD binding domain-containing oxidoreductase. Tar-
geted replacement of ugsO caused a signiﬁcant reduction
in ustilaginoidin production down to 25.3%  5.4% of the
wild-type level. Although, eight major ustilaginoidin deriva-
tives in the wild-type strain were all detected in the mutant,
the percentages of dehydrogenated ustilaginoidins N
(5) and M (8) were signiﬁcantly increased and the percent-
ages of hydrogenated ustilaginoidins E (4) and D (7) were
decreased compared with those in the wild type (Fig. 2B,
Supporting Information Fig. S3). The ability to produce
ustilaginoidins was largely restored up to 63.9%  10.4%
of the wild-type level and the ratios of dehydrogenated/hy-
drogenated ustilaginoidin derivatives N/E and M/D were
also restored in the complementary strain ΔugsO/ugsO
(Fig. 2B). The ﬁnding indicates that UgsO is involved in
redox modiﬁcation of ustilaginoidins.
UgsT is a major transporter responsible for ustilaginoidin
biosynthesis
The ΔugsT mutant exhibited a bleached-white colony on
the PSA plates. UgsT encodes a protein with 574 amino
acids,
which
includes
13
predicted
transmembrane
domains and shows high similarity to major facilitator
superfamily efﬂux pumps. HPLC proﬁles illustrated that
only a trace amount of ustilaginoidins were detected in the
ΔugsT mutant in comparison with the wild type, indicating
that targeted deletion of ugsT greatly disrupts the ability of
U. virens to produce ustilaginoidins (Fig. 2C). To further
conﬁrm the function of UgsT, the complementary strain
was constructed for the ΔugsT mutant. The plasmid-borne
ugsT with the native promoter partially recovered the abil-
ity to generate ustilaginoidins of the mutant strain. The
ustilaginoidin extracts from the complementary strain
showed the same HPLC proﬁles as those from the wild-
type strain, but were at a lower level compared with P1
(Fig. 2C).

derivatives

UgsL is responsible for monomer dimerization in
ustilaginoidin biosynthesis
The 2639 bp-long ugsL gene consists of seven exons,
encoding a 710 amino-acid protein. The protein contains
three copper oxidase-like domains and is predicted to be
a laccase responsible for the dimerization of two mono-
mer molecules. In HPLC analysis, eight compounds in the
wild-type culture were all missing from the ugsL-deletion
mutant, while three novel peaks (the metabolites 9, 10 and
11) were detected in the mutant with shorter retention
times of 10.4, 12.7 and 14.2 min, respectively (Fig. 3A).
These data indicate that these compounds are more
hydrophilic than the ustilaginoidin derivatives identiﬁed
above. These compounds had a maximal UV absorption
at 280 nm while the UV absorption of ustilaginoidins
peaked at 290 nm (Supporting Information Fig. S6). The
UV–visible spectra of these compounds displayed high
similarity to those of rubrofusarin and nor-rubrofusarin, but
were different from those of ustilaginoidins, suggesting that
these compounds were related with rubrofusarin and nor-
rubrofusarin with respect to their conjugated ring systems
(Frandsen et al., 2011). HR-ESI-MS analysis showed that
the three compounds had an m/z of 259.0581, 273.0738
and 273.0742 (= [M-H]−), equivalent to molecular formula of
C14H12O5, C15H14O5 and C15H14O5, respectively (Fig. 3B).
To generate enough amount of the new compounds for
determining their molecular structure, the ΔugsL mutant
was cultured in a large scale on solid rice medium. After
being puriﬁed from the fungal EtOAc extract, the compound

Discussion
As an important newly-emerging rice disease, false smut
causes severe mycotoxin contamination on rice grains,
which imposes a major health issue to human and animals.
The polyketide ustilaginoidins are one of major types of
mycotoxins produced by U. virens. The biosynthetic genes
and pathways for some polyketide-type mycotoxins in phy-
topathogenic fungi have been revealed. For instance, zeara-
lenones from F. graminearum, nonsteroidal estrogenic
mycotoxins associated with estrogenic syndromes in swine,
are synthesized by a 25 kb zearalenone biosynthetic gene
cluster (Kim et al., 2005; Gaffoor and Trail, 2006; Desjardins
and Proctor, 2007). The biosynthesis of the polyketide fumo-
nisins that induce cancer and neural tube defects in labora-
tory rodents is regulated by a gene cluster consisting of
15 contiguous genes in Fusarium (Desjardins et al., 1996;
Proctor et al., 2003; Proctor et al., 2006). Although the bio-
synthetic gene cluster for ustilaginoidins has been recently
predicted in U. virens (Zhang et al., 2014), we provided,
here, the ﬁrst experimental evidence to support that the ugs
gene cluster is responsible for ustilaginoidin biosynthesis.
In fungi, polyketides are generally produced by type I iter-
ative PKSs. These large multidomain enzymes iteratively
catalyse several rounds of condensation of coenzyme A
(CoA)-thioesteriﬁed carboxylic acids, usually acetate and
malonate (Fujii et al., 2004). The chemical structure of usti-
laginoidins suggests that the polyketide may be synthesized
through condensation catalysed by a nonreducing PKS, fol-
lowed by additional catalytic steps, such as methylation and
oxidation. In this study, neither ustilaginoidins nor the inter-
mediates were detected via HPLC in the ΔUvPKS1 mutant,
supporting the hypothesis that UvPKS1 catalyses the ﬁrst
step of ustilaginoidin biosynthesis in U. virens (Fig. 2A).
Since ustilaginoidin is structurally similar to chaetochromin
and aurofusarin, UvPKS1 may catalyse the condensation of
one acetyl-CoA and six manonyl-CoA units resulting in a lin-
ear heptaketide, followed by cyclization, to form the hepta-
ketide nor-rubrofusarin (Fig. 5) (Frandsen et al., 2006).
UgsO was predicted to be an FAD-binding domain con-
taining reductase. The higher ratios of dehydrogenated usti-
laginoidins N and M to hydrogenated derivatives E and D in
the ΔugsO mutant (Fig. 2B, Supporting Information Fig. S3)
suggest
that UgsO
may catalyse the
hydrogenation
between C-2 and C-3 of ustilaginoidins N and M to produce
ustilaginoidins E and D, respectively (Fig. 5). Besides, sig-
niﬁcantly reduced amount of ustilaginoidins in the ΔugsO
mutant implies that the UgsO-mediated hydrogenation is
somehow important for ustilaginoidin biosynthesis.

Using the transporter classiﬁcation database (TCDB; http://
www.tcdb.org) (Saier and Ren, 2006), UgsT is predicted to be
a member in the drug: H+ antiporter efﬂux family of MFS trans-
porters with 13 predicted transmembrane domains. MgMfs1, a
MFS protein from the wheat pathogenic fungus Mycosphaer-
ella graminicola, was previously shown to export natural
mycotoxins and a variety of fungicides (Roohparvar et al.,
2007). The sequence similarity between UgsT and MgMfs1
(40.0% amino acid sequence identity) suggests that UgsT
may transport ustilaginoidins and/or its intermediates across
cell membrane. The hypothesis is supported by the fact that
the ΔugsT mutant almost lost the ability to produce ustilaginoi-
dins and its intermediates (Fig. 2C). Similarly, the deletion of
bik6 encoding an MFS transporter greatly reduces the ability
to generate bikaverins in F. fujikuroi (Wiemann et al., 2009). In
another case, the ΔaurT mutant in F. graminearum produces
less aurofusarin, but generates more amount of the intermedi-
ate rubrofusarin than the wild-type (Frandsen et al., 2011).
Therefore, UgsT is most likely responsible for the transport of
ustilaginoidins across the membrane and is essential for ustila-
ginoidin biosynthesis.
The C3/30-methylated ustilaginoidin derivatives were no
longer generated in the ugsJ mutant, instead C3/30-
nonmethylated ustilaginoidins F, G and A were accumulated
much more (Fig. 2D). These ﬁndings support the prediction
that UgsJ is a methyltransferase, which is responsible for
C3-methylation on monomeric precursors or dimerized
ustilaginoidins.
The ugsL mutant generated three novel metabolites, one
of which was identiﬁed as 3-methyl-dihydro-nor-rubrofusarin
according to its molecular formula and the NMR data
(Fig. 3, Supporting Information Fig. S5). The other two com-
pounds were speculated to be dihydro-nor-rubrofusarin and
a
chiral
molecule
of
3-methyl-dihydro-nor-rubrofusarin
according to the LC–MS data (Fig. 3B). Therefore, it is sug-
gested that nor-rubrofusarin is the primary UvPKS1 product
and may be further reduced to form its dihydro derivative
(Fig. 5). Ustilaginoidin D is almost identical to chaetochro-
min A in molecular formula except the absolute conﬁgura-
tion (Lu et al., 2014). Both chemicals are formed by
dimerization of heptaketides with the nor-rubrofusarin core
structure through a C9-C9 bond (Frandsen et al., 2006).
These types of dimerization are generally catalysed by dif-
ferent laccases that oxidize polyphenols, hydroquinones
and a considerable range of other compounds (Thurston,
1994). This type of oxidation generates free radicals that
react with each other and induces the dimerization of
monomers covalently coupled by C-C, C-O and C-N bonds
(Claus, 2004). Based on the high similarity of UgsL to cop-
per oxidase-like laccases (Table 1), we propose that this
protein is responsible for oxidizing monomeric intermedi-
ates to create free radicals, and that the oxidative coupling
of these monomeric naphtho-γ-pyrone radicals yields differ-
ent types of dimeric compounds (Fig. 5).
Collectively, the pathway for ustilaginoidin biosynthesis
is proposed based on our experimental results and is sum-
marized in Fig. 5. The condensation of one acetyl-CoA
and six malonyl-CoA molecules by UvPKS1, followed by
cyclization, forms the heptaketide nor-rubrofusarin. No
nor-rubrofusarin was detected in the ΔugsL mutant, indi-
cating that the primary product is readily reduced to give
its dihydro derivative. However, the reductase responsible
for the catalytic step needs to be revealed. As 3-methyl-
dihydro-nor-rubrofusarin can be detected in the ΔugsL
mutant, UgsJ-catalysed C3-methylation most likely occurs
prior to UgsL-mediated dimerization. Different types of
heptaketides
including
nor-rubrofusarin,
dihydro-nor-
rubrofusarin and 3-methyl-dihydro-nor-rubrofusarin could
be then oxidized and dimerized by UgsL to yield different
types of ustilaginoidins. Some derivatives, such as ustila-
ginoidins M and D, may be reversibly reduced or oxidized
by UgsO or unidentiﬁed enzymes.
In ﬁlamentous fungi, the biosynthesis genes for certain
secondary metabolite are often physically clustered in the
genome and coordinately regulated (Osbourn, 2010). The
synergetic and positional effects among the genes in the
ugs cluster might explain partial restoration of ustilaginoidin
biosynthesis in the complementary strains. Incomplete
complementation might be also caused by inefﬁcient pro-
tein translation and protein misfolding due to the fusion tag.
In the ugs cluster, ugsR1 and ugsR2 are predicted to
encode a transcription initiation factor and a MADS-box
type transcription factor. We showed that the deletion of
ugsR2 did not signiﬁcantly alter expression of the ana-
lysed genes in the ugs gene cluster and had no dramatic
effect on the biosynthesis and chemical proﬁles of ustilagi-
noidins (Fig. S8A and B). The results indicate that UgsR2
may be involved in ﬁne-tuning gene expression, rather
than a major regulator of the ugs cluster. Similarly, dele-
tion of aurR2 encoding a GAL4-like Zn2Cys6 transcription
factor in F. graminearum showed no detectable effect on
gene expression in the aur cluster. However, the ΔaurR2
mutant generates a higher rubrofusarin to aurofusarin ratio
compared with the wild type (Frandsen et al., 2006). In
addition, GIP2/aurR1 in F. graminearum, the counterpart
of ugsR1, is required for the transcription of the aur gene
cluster (Frandsen et al., 2006; Kim et al., 2006). Interest-
ingly, multiple attempts to generate the ΔugsR1 mutant
failed. Because ugsR1 is predicted to encode the tran-
scription initiation factor TFIID subunit 1 (TAF1), an essen-
tial component of TFIID complex, we speculate that
ugsR1 deletion may have a lethal effect to U. virens.
The biological signiﬁcance of the secondary metabo-
lite polyketides has been revealed in many fungi. In
F. graminearum, aurofusarin accumulation affects the
mycelial growth rate and conidiation (Malz et al., 2005).
The phenotypes in mycelial growth of the ugs mutants
indicate that different ustilaginoidin derivatives might

To generate pGKO2-hyg, a approximately 1.4 kb fragment
