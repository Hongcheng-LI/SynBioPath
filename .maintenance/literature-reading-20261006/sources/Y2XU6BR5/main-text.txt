ABSTRACT: Biosynthesis of hydroxy terpenes, which possess better solubility and
target-binding capability than terpene hydrocarbons, generally needs cascaded catalysis of
terpene synthase (TS) and cytochrome P450 oxygenase. Interestingly, some TSs can
directly generate hydroxy terpenes (mostly monohydroxy terpenes) independent of
oxygenases. There are even rare TSs that can form dihydroxy terpenes directly.
Nevertheless, the structure and catalytic mechanism of dihydroxy terpene synthases
(DHTSs) remain elusive to date, hindering their practical applications. Through protein
crystallography, multiscale simulations, and site-directed mutagenesis, we elucidate a
stepwise carbocation quenching mechanism. In this process, two water molecules are
strictly manipulated by a dynamic hydrogen-bond network to quench the carbocation
intermediates. Most importantly, Tyr312 was identified as the indispensable and
irreplaceable residue for initiating the reprotonation of the monohydroxy terpene. The
spatiotemporally precise manipulation mechanism of DHTSs enriches the knowledge of
TSs and lays a foundation for developing an oxygenase-independent biosynthesis system of multihydroxy terpenes.
■INTRODUCTION
The hydroxylation of terpenes is beneficial toward improving
the solubility of terpenoids in aqueous media and provides
hydrogen-bonding capacity that enables specific binding to
(macro)molecular targets.1 The biosynthesis of hydroxy
terpene generally contains two stages:2−7 (1) terpene synthase
(TS)-catalyzed cationic cascade reactions for cyclization of
oligoprenyl diphosphates; (2) cytochrome P450 (CYP450)
monooxygenase-catalyzed alkane radical oxidation reaction for
hydroxylation modification of terpenes (Figure 1A). However,
the application of CYP450 monooxygenases for the biosyn-
thesis of hydroxy terpenes is usually limited by catalytic
promiscuity, poor regio-/stereoselectivity, low heterologous
soluble expression level, or coupling efficiency.8−13 Besides, the
requirement of cofactors [e.g., NAD(P)H] during oxidation
reactions through CYP450 monooxygenases further increases
the costs.
An interesting phenomenon occasionally observed in the
TS-catalyzed cationic cascade reactions is that the final cationic
intermediate is quenched by a solvent molecule (H2O),
resulting in direct formation of hydroxy terpenes (Figure
1B).14−16 Due to the fact that water molecules and carbocation
intermediates are generally limited to specific locations within
the active pocket of TSs, TS-catalyzed water-mediated
carbocation quenching reactions usually form hydroxy terpenes
with high regio-/stereoselectivity.17−22 Hence, these hydroxy
terpene synthases (HTSs) can be applied to directly synthesize
hydroxy terpenes without CYP450 monooxygenases. However,
the formation of multisite hydroxy terpenes is still generally
catalyzed by CYP450 monooxygenases,23,24 since HTSs are
commonly monohydroxy terpene synthases (MHTSs).2,4,5,25
Although some CYP450 monooxygenases can achieve multi-
step oxidations,26,27 most TS-CYP450 coupled systems still
need combination of several CYP450 monooxygenases for
forming multisite hydroxy terpenes (Figure 1A).28,29 Hence, a
system for biosynthesizing multisite hydroxy terpenes is usually
a complicated multienzyme system, which is a challenge for the
construction of a cell factory due to the difficulty in controlling
the metabolic balance.30−32
Recently, ZmEDS from Zea mays was found to directly form
eudesmane-2,11-diol, a sesquiterpenoid with broad antimicro-
bial activities against a suite of fungal and bacterial
pathogens,33 marking the discovery of the first dihydroxy
terpene synthase (DHTS).34,35 However, the relatively low
proportion (52%) of eudesmane-2,11-diol formed by ZmEDS
limits its application. Another reported DHTS is TwCS from
Tripterygium wilfordii that can produce cryptomeridiol with a
high proportion (90%).36 The discovery of these two DHTSs
indicates that there should exist other DHTSs, which will

Herein, we report the first microbial DHTS, PeTS3 from
Penicillium expansum, producing eudesmane-2,11-diol with a
higher proportion (72%) than that of ZmEDS, which is more
suitable for the biosynthesis of eudesmane-2,11-diol (Figure
1B). Structures of PeTS3 were obtained in both the apo form
and the complex with a trinuclear Mg2+ cluster, a diphosphate
(PPi) group, and a benzyltriethylammonium cation (BTAC),
representing the first DHTS structures resolved. A wide site-
directed mutagenesis and the hybrid quantum mechanical/
molecular mechanical (QM/MM) calculation revealed a step-
by-step proton transfer and water-mediated carbocation
quenching processes under the manipulation of an intricate
H-bond network, giving the first detailed explanation on the
whole dihydroxy terpene formation process under the catalysis
of DHTS.

Structures of PeTS3 Reveal an Induced-Fit Model. In
spite of the fact that both PeTS3 and ZmEDS can form
dihydroxy terpene 5, they only share a sequence identity of
22%. Besides, PeTS3 shows better fidelity on product profile
than ZmEDS (2 major products of PeTS3 versus 7 major
products of ZmEDS).34 Hence, the formation mechanism of
dihydroxy terpenes manipulated by PeTS3 might be more
precise than that manipulated by ZmEDS.
Although the structural knowledge of DHTSs is no doubt
very important for deep mechanistic insights into the
formation of unique dihydroxy terpene, there is no structural
information on DHTSs reported to date. Hence, we resolved
the first structure of DHTS, in both an apo form and a PeTS3-
BTAC-PPi-Mg2+ complex, with resolutions of 1.68 and 1.99 Å,
respectively (Table S8), which provides an ideal opportunity
for analyzing the detailed formation mechanism of dihydroxy
terpene.
The structure of PeTS3 (Figure 2A) shows a canonical class
I terpene synthase fold,40,41 consisting of 10 core α-helices
(defined as A−J from N-terminal to C-terminal), 4 short
helices (defined as α1−α4), and 1 antiparallel β sheet with 2 β
strands (defined as β1 and β2). The conserved trinuclear Mg2+
cluster binding motifs
106DDVTE110 (Asp-rich motif) and
237NDYFSWNIE245 (NSE motif) are located on helices C and
H, respectively. The PPi group (Figure 2B) is fixed by the
trinuclear Mg2+ cluster, Asp-rich motif, NSE motif, Arg193 (as
a pyrophosphate sensor, located on helix G1), and Arg325-
Tyr326 (RY dyad that is located on helix α4).42 The conserved
effector triad associated with the initiation of the cyclization
cascade is Arg193-Asn196-Cys197 (sensor-linker-effector
triad). There is an occasional event that the conserved effector
residue, mainly Gly, Ala, or Ser, is replaced by Cys, as occurred
in PeTS3 (Cys197).42
The overall structures of PeTS3-BTAC-PPi-Mg2+ and apo-
PeTS3 show slightly different conformations with a Cα root-
mean-square deviation (RMSD) value of 0.302 Å. The obvious
changes are that helices H, I, and C move toward the active site
in PeTS3-BTAC-PPi-Mg2+ complex as compared with the apo-
PeTS3. These structural differences between them exhibit the
change of the PeTS3 conformation from the open state to the
closed state (Figure 2A).
Due to its flexibility, the loop 246−253 of apo-PeTS3
exhibits a missing structure (Figure 2C) as compared to that of
PeTS3-BTAC-PPi-Mg2+. This phenomenon suggests an
induced-fit model42 (Figure 2D) of PeTS3: When Mg2+ and
the substrate enter the active site pocket of PeTS3, Glu245
rotates clockwise and coordinates with MgB
2+, which promotes
the loop 246−253 to enter the top space of the PPi-Mg2+
complex. Along with the movement of loop 246−253, Arg252
interacts with Thr109 and Met112, which are located on helix
C. Besides, Glu111 (on helix C) moves toward the active site
pocket and interacts with Gln248. These conformational
changes prompt the formation of an H-bond network between
loop 246−253 and helix C. Asp177 on the loop between helix
F and helix G1 rotates to interact with Arg252, which further
stabilizes the loop 246−253. Hence, Gln248 and Arg252 may
serve as a lock to fix the flexible loop 246−253, which acts as a
lid for the closed active site pocket.
To verify this hypothesis, variants PeTS3Q248A, PeTS3R252A,
and PeTS3Q248A/R252A were introduced into the E. coli chassis
(Table S3). Variant PeTS3Q248A showed an identical product
profile as that of wild-type PeTS3 (Figure 2E and Table S9).
However, variants PeTS3R252A and PeTS3Q248A/R252A gave only
compound 1 (Figure 2E and Table S9) as the sole
monohydroxylated product. The SDS-PAGE and circular
dichroism (CD) analysis (Figures S16−S17) showed no
obvious alteration with respect to either the protein’s overall
structure or expression level. Hence, it is suggested that Arg252
plays a crucial role in fixing loop 246−253, while Gln248 has
little effect. Considering the previously proposed cyclization
mechanism of dihydroxy terpene 5 (Scheme 1),34 we deduced
that the flexibility of loop 246−253 will be enhanced when
Arg252 is mutated to Ala, which potentially causes the
premature release of neutral intermediate 1, thus terminating
the cascade reaction process forming dihydroxy sesquiterpene
5.
The RY dyad is widely encountered in class I TSs.43,44 In the
structure of PeTS3-BTAC-PPi-Mg2+, the RY dyad forms a
stable H-bond network (Figure 2F) with Asp107 and the PPi
group, which results in the formation of the short helix α4 and
reduces the flexibility of residues 324−330, serving as a lid for
closure of the active pocket of PeTS3.
Essential H-Bond Network for Forming Unique
Dihydroxy Terpene. As shown in Scheme 1, the first
hydroxyl group of compound 5 is formed through the water-
mediated carbocation quenching of intermediate IM-A. The
reprotonation of neutral intermediate 1 produces a new
carbocation, which is finally quenched by water, giving the
second hydroxyl group of compound 5. Hence, there are two
necessary conditions for forming the unique compound 5 with
double hydroxyl groups: (a) the water molecule for quenching
the carbocation; and (b) the reprotonation of neutral
intermediate 1.
The TS-catalyzed cationic cascade reaction can form diverse
and highly reactive cationic intermediates, which might
potentially alkylate and inactivate the enzyme.5,25,45 Hence,
TSs typically contain a nonpolar active site pocket to
effectively manage and manipulate these intermediates.
However, this does not necessarily preclude the presence of
occasional polar residues or solvent molecules in the active
sites of TSs. Polar residues play an essential role in regulating
the water-mediated quenching process of carbocation and the

Spatiotemporally Precise Manipulation Mechanism
toward Forming the Unique Dihydroxy Terpene. To
explore the role of this hydrogen-bond network more precisely,
BTAC was replaced with the cyclic carbocation IM-A
according to the position of the cationic center, yielding the
PeTS3_IM-A complex model. In this binding mode, IM-A and
BTAC share comparable spatial volumes and display excellent
structural superposition with the cationic centers well aligned
(Figure S18). Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were
subsequently performed on the PeTS3_IM-A complex, where
IM-A exhibited good stability across multiple MD trajectories
(Figure S19). This complex was further subjected to QM/MM
MD simulations, which consistently verified the stability of this
binding mode (Figures S20−S21). To ensure a reliable
intermediate-binding model, the TerDockin approach48 was
further applied, and the lowest-energy binding pose obtained
from TerDockin was found to be consistent with the stable pose
observed in the molecular dynamics simulations, providing
additional validation for the rationality of the current binding
model (for details, see Supporting Information, Figures S22−
S27).
In the QM/MM-optimized complex structure of PeT-
S3_IM-A, the water molecule Wat519, as trapped by Tyr172
and the exposed main-chain carbonyl oxygen of Cys197
(Cys197-O), is close to the C11 atom of intermediate IM-A.
The average distance between the oxygen atom of Wat519 and
the C11 atom of carbocation IM-A, as observed during QM/
MM MD simulations, is 3.6 Å (Figure S21). This distance
facilitates the subsequent nucleophilic attack by the water
molecule on carbocation IM-A, giving compound 1 in an
oxonium ion form (IM-A2) with a predicted energy barrier of
7.6 kcal/mol (Figures 4A and S28).
In IM-A2, the H1 atom of the oxonium ion is situated 1.4 Å
away from Cys197-O. Notably, Cys197 is located within the
flexible G1/2 break region. The exposed backbone carbonyl
oxygen atom (Cys197-O in PeTS3) in the G1/2 break exhibits
basicity, deviating from canonical α-helical stabilization
mechanisms where such carbonyl typically functions as a
hydrogen bond acceptor.49,50 Previous studies have suggested
that this backbone carbonyl oxygen can act as a general base in
catalysis, particularly in the process of proton transfer.51,52
Herein, Cys197-O abstracts a proton from IM-A2, giving
neutral monohydroxy intermediate 1, with a predicted energy
barrier of only 4.3 kcal/mol (Figures 4A and S29). The
flexibility of the G1/2 break region allows the carbonyl group
to twist (Figure S30), enabling it to act as a “proton courier”
that facilitates the proton transfer to Wat535. Hence, the H-
bond network consisting of Tyr172-Wat519-Cys197 is
deduced to be essential for forming neutral intermediate 1.
Furthermore, site-directed mutagenesis was applied to
examine the roles of Tyr172 and Cys197 in the formation of
neutral intermediate 1. Tyr172 was mutated to Phe, giving a
variant of PeTS3Y172F for in vivo analysis. Breakage of the H-
bond between Tyr172 and Wat519 impedes the biosynthesis
of compound 1, which disrupts the whole cyclization reaction
and causes the decrease in total sesquiterpenes production of
PeTS3Y172F by 51% compared with that of parental strain
PeTS3WT (Figure 4B and Table S9). Besides, the proportion of
compound 5 in the total sesquiterpenes produced by
PeTS3Y172F also decreased obviously (22%) compared with
that by PeTS3WT (72%), indicating the key role of Tyr172 in
the proton transfer from IM-A2 to Wat535 (Table S9).
Additionally, Tyr172 also showed a stabilizing effect on the
precyclization conformation of substrate FPP, since the
introduction of smaller-size residues into site-172 resulted in
the formation of acyclic sesquiterpene nerolidol (6) (Figures
4B and S31).
In contrast to PeTS3Y172F, PeTS3C197A showed no obvious
change in the product profiles and sesquiterpenes production,
verifying that the side-chain residue of site-197 has no impact
on the cyclization reaction process (Figure 4B). Gly198 was
mutated to Ala in order to disturb the hydrogen bond between
Cys197-O and Wat519 (Figure S32). As a result, variant
PeTS3G198A showed a dramatically decreased sesquiterpene
production, only 4.0% of that of PeTS3WT (Figure 4B). The
SDS-PAGE and CD analyses of PeTS3G198A showed no
obvious alteration with respect to either the overall structure
or the expression level of the protein (Figures S16−S17).
Hence, breaking the H-bond between Wat519 and Cys197-O
disrupts the formation of neutral intermediate 1, resulting in
the termination of the whole cyclization reaction. Combining
the results of calculations and site-directed mutagenesis
together, the H-bonding network of Wat519-Tyr172-Cys197
is essential for the first stage of the PeTS3-catalyzed cyclization
reaction cascade, forming neutral intermediate 1.
It is evident that intermediate 1 resides in a relatively deep
potential well (Figure S33), suggesting a substantial lifetime
that may permit its direct release from the pocket. This
conforms to the formation of minor monohydroxy product 1
as experimentally observed in the wild-type enzyme.

It is worth noting that the Tyr312-Ser202-His84 triad

attack process. Ser202 and Tyr312, as hydrogen bond
acceptors binding Wat535, participate directly in the
reprotonation and carbocation quenching processes. His84
supports these processes by stabilizing a third water molecule
(Wat571) that forms a hydrogen bond with Tyr312. This
interaction ensures that Tyr312 acts as a hydrogen bond

thereby further enhancing the nucleophilicity of the oxygen
atom in Wat535 (Figure 4A).
The Tyr312 was supposed to be highly essential for the
reprotonation of neutral intermediate 1, since no compound 5
was formed when Tyr312 was substituted by Ala (Figure 4C).
To exclude the potential sterically bulky positioning effect of
Tyr312 on the formation and reprotonation of neutral
intermediate 1, which can also disrupt the cyclic cascade35
and cause misjudgment of Tyr312 function, residues with
varied steric hindrance (Val, Ile, Leu, Phe, and Trp) were
introduced into site-312 individually. Consequently, only
compound 1 was detected among variants, further verifying
that Tyr312 does activate the reprotonation of 1 and initiates
the second stage of cyclization cascade through its phenolic
hydroxyl group, rather than affecting the spatial position of
neutral intermediate 1 through the sterically bulky positioning
effect. It is worth noticing that only compound 1 was formed
as long as other residues were chosen to substitute Tyr312
(except PeTS3Y312P and PeTS3Y312R, producing almost no
sesquiterpene), indicating that Tyr312 is irreplaceable for
unique reprotonation of monohydroxy compound 1 (Figure
4C).
The nonpolar-residue substitutions of His84 (Figure 4D)
and Ser202 (Figure 4E) showed an apparently lower
proportion of dihydroxy compound 5, compared with that of
the polar-residue substitution, suggesting that residues at site-
84 and site-202 should remain polar to favor the H-bond
network formation and to play a beneficial role in the
reprotonation and carbocation quenching steps (Table S9).
Together above, the intricate H-bond network (Cys197-
Wat535-Ser202-Tyr312-Wat571-His84) is necessary and ex-
tremely important for the easy-to-terminate second-stage
cyclization reaction catalyzed by PeTS3, which ultimately
leads to the formation of dihydroxy terpene 5.
■DISCUSSION
Current strategies of TSs generally control intermediate
reactive conformations or change the stability of carbocation
intermediates in TSs, yielding diverse skeletons without
exploring TS-mediated hydroxylation modification.14,53−56
This study disclosed the participation of the H-bond network
in the cascaded dihydroxylation reactions, displaying the
important role of polar residues in the hydroxylating
modification by DHTSs. The investigation on the spatiotem-
porally precise water-mediated regulation mechanism of
PeTS3 by polar residues will expand the pool of design
strategies to obtain more HTSs and guide the development of
the HTS-based hydroxy terpene biosynthesis system, which is
simpler than the TS-P450 monooxygenase coupling systems
and more suitable for constructing cell factories.
Structural alignment between the open and closed states of
PeTS3 reveals a significant conformational change of loop
246−253. Previous studies indicated that this loop is a
conserved structure in class I TSs and encloses the active
pocket to protect it from bulk solvent.57,58 Our study shows
that enhanced flexibility of this loop results in premature
release of neutral intermediate 1, which suggests its role in
manipulating the cyclization cascade. In other TSs, this loop is
identified as important for product diversity.59,60 Modifying
this structurally conserved but sequence-unconserved loop
may therefore represent a general strategy for obtaining diverse
terpene skeletons with promising potential applications.
The effector residue of TSs is generally regarded as a
structural motif, which is closely associated with substrate
binding and active site closure. A recent study showed that the
main-chain carbonyl oxygen of the effector motif has a dual
role as a general base and a general acid.51,52 In this study,
Cys197-O not only exhibited a similar dual effect but also
participated in the capture of water to quench carbocation
intermediate, showing a triple catalytic role of the main-chain
carbonyl oxygen of effector motif during the TS-catalyzed
reactions. Our work conforms to a previous hypothesis that
this main-chain-mediated deprotonation and reprotonation
may commonly be adopted in TS-catalyzed neutral inter-
mediate-involved reactions.51 However, it is not clear whether
the main-chain carbonyl oxygen of the effector motif would
take little part in the TS-catalyzed reactions without
involvement of the neutral intermediate. More research is
needed to answer this question.
This work generated many PeTS3 variants that only produce
monohydroxy terpene 1. Besides the essential residue Tyr312
for reprotonation, other residues desired for the H-bond
network or structural function can also disturb the
reprotonation of neutral intermediate 1 to form dihydroxy
terpene 5. These results indicate that a more precise
manipulation mechanism is easier to be disturbed. Although
we have elucidated the PeTS3-mediated dihydroxy terpene
formation mechanism, converting these theoretical under-
standings into practical applications still requires further
efforts. Although prediction tools for protein structure have
achieved significant progress,61 predicting the accurate location
of water molecules inside a protein is still a challenge. Hence,
more structural information and precise simulation of the
DHTS-catalyzed reaction for forming dihydroxy terpenes will
supplement our knowledge of the internal regulation
mechanism. Recently, the rapid development of protein design
tools62 will also promote the application of HTSs to the
synthesis of multihydroxy terpenes.
■CONCLUSIONS
In summary, we report the crystal structure and manipulated
mechanism of DHTS for the first time, filling a critical gap in
terpenoid enzymology and presenting a biocatalytic platform
for oxygenase-free synthesis of eudesmane-2,11-diol, which
exhibits broad antimicrobial activities. With the newly
established catalytic model of DHTS and the inherently strong
plasticity of TSs,25,63−65 new rational strategies to create novel
TSs might be developed, supporting future applications for
synthetic biology and semisynthetic chemistry.
