ABSTRACT: Serratiomycin (1) is an antibacterial cyclic
depsipeptide, first discovered from a Eubacterium culture in
1998. This compound was initially reported to contain L-Leu, L-
Ser, L-allo-Thr, D-Phe, D-Ile, and hydroxydecanoic acid. In the
present study, 1 and three new derivatives, serratiomycin D1−D3
(2−4), were isolated from a Serratia sp. strain isolated from the
exoskeleton of a long-horned beetle. The planar structures of 1−4
were elucidated by using mass spectrometry (MS) and nuclear
magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy. Comparison of the
NMR chemical shifts and the physicochemical data of 1 to those of
previously reported serratiomycin indeed identified 1 as
serratiomycin. The absolute configurations of the amino units in
compounds 1−4 were determined by the advanced Marfey’s
method, 2,3,4,6-tetra-O-acetyl-β-D-glucopyranosyl isothiocyanate derivatization, and liquid chromatography−mass spectrometric
(LC−MS) analysis. Additionally, methanolysis and the modified Mosher’s method were used to determine the absolute
configuration of (3R)-hydroxydecanoic acid in 1. Consequently, the revised structure of 1 was found to possess D-Leu, L-Ser, L-Thr,
D-Phe, L-allo-Ile, and D-hydroxydecanoic acid. In comparison with the previously published structure of serratiomycin, L-Leu, L-allo-
Thr, and D-Ile in serratiomycin were revised to D-Leu, L-Thr, and L-allo-Ile. The new members of the serratiomycin family,
compounds 2 and 3, showed considerably higher antibacterial activities against Staphylococcus aureus and Salmonella enterica than
compound 1.

he insect microbiome refers to the community of
microorganisms (such as bacteria, fungi, viruses, and
other microbes) that inhabit the bodies of insects.1 These
microorganisms play crucial roles in the overall health,
development, and survival of insects.2 The insect microbiome
has various functions, such as aiding digestion, protecting
against pathogens, and influencing the metabolism and
behavior of insects.3 In the field of natural product discovery,
insect microbiomes are now recognized as valuable sources of
bioactive natural products with a pharmaceutical potential.
This is based on the continuous discovery of structurally and
biologically interesting compounds from microbes associated
with insects. In addition, the enormous biodiversity of insects,
which is estimated at 5 million species, further enhances their
importance.4−9
Recent studies on microbial natural products derived from
insects have consistently reported bioactive compounds. A
novel bicyclic macrolide, hamuramicin C, was isolated from
Streptomyces sp. MBP16, which is a gut bacterium found in the
wasp Vespa crabro flavofasciata. Hamuramicin C demonstrated
considerable inhibitory activity against various human cancer
cell lines.10 Pentaminomycins C and E were isolated from the
Streptomyces sp. GG23 strain, which resides in the gut of the
mealworm beetle Tenebrio molitor. These compounds showed
significant in vitro activities, inducing autophagy and exhibiting
cytoprotective effects against oxidative stress.11 More recently,
tiuslactones A−E were isolated from Streptomyces sp. SN5431,
which is a bacterial strain derived from the intestines of Larva
holotrichiae. Among these, tiuslactone B displayed potent
antifungal activity against Bipolaris maydis, a pathogen
responsible for corn leaf blight.12 Recently, 20 metabolites,
including three previously unreported obscurolide-type metab-
olites, were isolated from Streptomyces neopeptinius, which was
obtained from the surface of the termite Odontotermes
formasanus. Among these 20 metabolites, indole-3-carboxylic
acid exhibited antifungal activity against C. neoformans.13 These
findings collectively emphasize the importance of insect-
microbiome-derived metabolites, which provide structural

Chemical investigations of natural products derived from
insect microbiomes have occasionally led to structural revisions
of previously reported compounds. For instance, the absolute
configuration of antitubercular lydiamycin A14 had remained
unresolved for approximately a decade, even after its chemical
synthesis.15,16 However, its structure was revised when it was
isolated from a Streptomyces strain found in the gut of the
mealworm beetle (T. molitor) and subsequently analyzed.17
Furthermore, tripartilactam was discovered from the Strepto-
myces sp. SNA112 strain, which was isolated from the brood
ball of a dung beetle (Copris tripartitus). Subsequently, a
comprehensive reinvestigation of the nuclear magnetic
resonance (NMR) data, including the
13C−13C correlated
spectroscopy COSY NMR data, was conducted for triparti-
lactam, which resulted in the unequivocal revision of its
structure as niizalactam C.18
In this study, a bacterial strain (#HSH236) was isolated
from a longhorn beetle (Neocerambyx raddei) specimen, which
was collected from the Gwanak mountain. This beetle is one of
the most common and dominant beetle species within its
genus in East Asia. Based on the 16S rRNA gene sequence
similarities, the HSH236 strain was most closely related to
Serratia marcescens (98% similarity). The HSH236 bacterial
strain was cultured in eight different media, and the ethyl
acetate extracts were chemically profiled using liquid
chromatography−mass spectrometry (LC−MS). Chemical
analysis of the extracts led to the identification of a series of
serratiomycin family peptide metabolites. Subsequent large-
scale cultivation of the strain enabled the chromatographic
isolation of serratiomycin (1) and its three derivatives (2−4).
In 1998, 1 was isolated from a Eubacterium culture, and its
planar structure was determined using 2D NMR and MS.19
The comparison of NMR chemical shifts and the analysis of
chemical properties, including UV, IR, optical rotation, and
MS data, strongly indicated that compound 1 is indeed the
previously reported serratiomycin. The chirality of the amino
acid residues was determined via LC−MS of the
D-α-
methylbenzyl thiourea derivatives of
L- or
D-amino acid
oxidase-treated acid hydrolysate. The structure of 1 was
initially reported to contain L-Leu, L-Ser, L-allo-Thr, D-Phe, D-
Ile, and D-hydroxydecanoic acid and exhibited a weak Gram-
positive antibacterial spectrum in vitro. Detailed structural
analysis of 1 and its three derivatives (2−4) allowed for
structural revision of serratiomycin and the discovery of three
new compounds in the serratiomycin family with their
antibacterial activities.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Revised serratiomycin (1) was purified as a white powder, and
its molecular formula was determined to be C38H61N5O9 using
high-resolution electrospray ionization mass spectrometry
(HR-ESI-MS). The unsaturation number of compound 1
was found to be 11. All one-bond 1H−13C correlations were
established by analyzing the 1H, 13C, and HSQC NMR spectral
data. The 1H and HSQC NMR spectra of 1 indicated the
presence of five exchangeable protons (δH 8.50, 8.10, 8.08,
7.82, and 7.46) and five aromatic protons (δH 7.0−7.2) in the
downfield region below δH 7.0. Two more heteroatom-bound
protons were detected at δH 5.13 and 5.04, and five α-amino
protons were detected between δH 3.8 and 4.5 (δH 3.76, 4.10,
4.35, 4.43, and 4.46). This initial analysis indicated that 1 is a
peptide derivative and contains five amino acids and one
aromatic ring. Further analysis of the COSY couplings of H-2−
10 disclosed the structure of a lipid chain containing a methine
proton (δH 4.92) directly bonded to a carbon bearing an
oxygen atom (δC 71.7), seven aliphatic methylene protons (δH
1.2−2.7), and one methyl proton (δH 0.84). Further analysis of
the 1H NMR and HSQC spectra revealed that 1 contains four
aliphatic methylene and three methine protons (δH 1.12−3.62
ppm) and five methyl groups (δH 0.97, 0.83, 0.82, 0.81, and
0.80).
The 13C NMR spectrum of 1 (in DMSO-d6 at 200 MHz)
revealed six carbonyl carbon signals (δC 172.1, 171.3, 170.6,
170.5, 169.5, and 168.8), six aromatic carbon signals (δC 136.7,
129.1, 129.1, 128.1, 128.1, and 126.4), three oxygenated
carbons signals (δC 71.7, 65.3, and 61.2), and five amino acid
α-carbon peaks (δC 58.3, 57.5, 56.6, 53.5, and 50.6), reflecting
the characteristic features of a peptide-class compound. The
13C and HSQC NMR spectra of 1 also displayed seven
aliphatic methylene carbon signals at δC 22.0−40.1 (δC 40.1,
31.8, 31.0, 28.5, 28.3, 24.7, and 22.0) with one methyl carbon
at δC 13.9. This spin system was also confirmed by using total
correlation spectroscopy (TOCSY).
Because 1 is a peptide that contains several amino acid units,
the structures of individual amino acid moieties, leucine,
serine, threonine, phenylalanine, and isoleucine, were eluci-
dated using COSY, TOCSY, and heteronuclear multiple bond
correlation (HMBC) experiments (Figure 1). The resultant
peptide ring was constructed based on HMBC and rotating
frame Overhauser enhancement spectroscopy (ROESY). The
18-NH (δH 8.10) signal of serine correlated with the amide
carbonyl carbon C-17 (δC 172.1), which belonged to the
leucine residue. This leucine residue was, in turn, connected to
a serine unit. The HMBC correlation between the 23-NH (δH
8.08) signal of threonine and the carbonyl carbon C-22 (δC
170.6) of serine established the sequence from threonine to
serine. The HMBC correlation between 29-NH (δH 7.46), the
amide proton of phenylalanine, and C-28 (δC 169.5), the
carbonyl carbon of threonine, suggested that phenylalanine was
adjacent to threonine. The isoleucine unit was placed adjacent

The absolute configurations at the α-carbons of the five
amino acid units were determined using the advanced Marfey’s
method with L- and D- forms of 1-fluoro-2,4-dinitrophenyl-5-
alanine amide (FDAA).20 Revised serratiomycin (1) was
hydrolyzed to obtain free amino acids, which were derivatized
with
L- and
D-FDAA and analyzed by using LC−MS. A
comparison of the LC−MS results revealed the presence of D-
leucine, L-serine, L-threonine, D-phenylalanine, and L-isoleucine
(Table S1). The additional chiral center at C-25 of threonine
was subjected to further stereochemical analyses. L-Threonine
and L-allo-threonine standards were derivatized with D-FDAA
and analyzed by using LC−MS. The free amino acid in
compound 1 was determined to be L-threonine by comparing
its retention time with that of the derivatized standards (Table
S1). To elucidate the absolute configuration at C-41 of the L-
isoleucine unit, 2,3,4,6-tetra-O-acetyl-β-D-glucopyranosyl iso-
thiocyanate (GITC) derivatization was used.21 First,
L-
isoleucine and
L-allo-isoleucine standards were derivatized
with GITC, and their retention times were compared with
those of free isoleucine obtained from 1 by co-injection
analysis. These results determined that the isoleucine in 1 is L-
allo-isoleucine (Figure 2).
The first report of serratiomycin19 proposed the absolute
configurations of the amino acids based on the LC−MS
analysis of the D-α-methylbenzyl isothiocyanate derivatives of
the L- or D-amino acid oxidase-treated acid hydrolysate of
serratiomycin. However, this previous work employed steep
gradient conditions (10−100% CH3CN in H2O over 15 min)
for the LC−MS analysis, which may not be optimal in
separating the derivatives of the amino acids such as isomeric
L-Thr and L-allo-Thr. In addition, the identical molecular ions
of Leu and Ile might have led to the misinterpretation by
identifying D-Leu as D-Ile and L-allo-Ile as L-Leu under the
steep gradient conditions.
The chiral center at the C-3 position of 3-hydroxydecanoic
acid was determined by Mosher derivatization of the
methanolysis product of 1.22 Briefly, 1 was subjected to
methanolysis to yield methyl ester 1a (Figure S28), which was
then used to confirm the planar structure of 1. The structure of
1a, which contains a C-3 stereogenic center with a secondary
hydroxy group, was clearly determined from the 1D and 2D
NMR spectra (Figures S30−S35). The methyl ester alcohol
was acylated with R-(−)- and S-(+)-α-methoxy-α-
(trifluoromethyl)phenyl acetyl chloride to furnish the corre-
sponding S- and R-MTPA esters (1b and 1c), respectively
(Figure S28).
1H NMR and COSY spectra were used to determine the
proton chemical shifts for 1b and 1c. Calculation of the ΔδS−R
values clearly established the absolute configuration of C-3 as R
(Figure 3). Consequently, the structure of serratiomycin,
which was initially reported to contain L-Leu, L-Ser, L-allo Thr,
D-Phe, D-Ile, and D-hydroxydecanoic acid, was revised. NMR
spectroscopic data and chemical derivatization results disclosed
that the revised structure of 1 contains D-Leu, L-Ser, L- Thr, D-
Phe, L-allo-Ile, and D-hydroxydecanoic acid.
Serratiomycin D1 (2) was purified as a white powder, and its
molecular formula was determined to be C40H65N5O9 using
HR-ESI-MS and 1D 1H and 13C NMR spectral data (Tables 1
and 2). Based on its molecular formula, 2 contains two more
methylene protons (δH 1.21 and 1.22) and carbons (δC 28.7
and 28.7) than 1. The NMR and UV spectra of 2 were similar
to those of 1, indicating that this compound is a derivative of
serratiomycin (1). Comparison of the 1D and 2D NMR
spectroscopic data of both compounds revealed that the (3R)-
hydroxydecanoic acid moiety in 1 was replaced with (3R)-
hydroxydodecanoic acid in 2; however, the other amino acid
units were identical. The absolute configurations of the amino
acid units in 2 were deduced to be identical to those in 1
because of their common biosynthetic origin.
Serratiomycin D2 (3) was purified as a white powder, and its
molecular formula was determined to be C40H63N5O9 using
HR-ESI-MS. Its unsaturation number was found to be 12. 1D
NMR and HSQC spectral data revealed that 3 contained two
more double-bond protons (δH 5.28 and 5.43) and carbons
(δC 124.7 and 132.3) than 2. The position of the double bond
was determined by interpreting the COSY and HMBC NMR
spectra. The COSY couplings from H-4 (δH 2.23 and 2.36) to
H-5 (δH 5.28), H-5 (δH 5.28) to H-6 (δH 5.43), and H-6 (δH
5.43) to H-7 (δH 1.98) revealed that the double bond was
included in the hydrophobic chain. The HMBC correlations
from H-5 (δH 5.28) to C-3 (δC 71.7), C-4 (δC 30.2), and C-7
(δC 26.7) and from H-6 (δH 5.43) to C-4 (δC 30.2) and C-7
(δC 26.7) established the sequence from 3-hydroxy-5-
dodecenoic acid. Regarding the geometry of the double
bond, the 1H−1H coupling constant between H-5 and H-6
could not be measured because of the multiplicity of the two
protons. Instead, the
13C chemical shifts established the
configuration of the olefinic bond. Reportedly, the aliphatic
carbons flanking a Z-olefin in a long hydrocarbon chain
resonate at δC 28−30, while those next to an E-double bond
resonate at δC 32−33, clearly distinguishing the E/Z
geometries.23,24 The chemical shifts of C-4 and C-7 were
detected at δC 30.2 and 26.7, respectively, thus assigning the
5Z configuration. Other amino acid units, including leucine,

Bacterial Isolation. Insect samples were collected from Gwanak
Mountain, Seoul, Republic of Korea, in March 2018. The sample was
suspended in sterile water and spread onto various agar media for
strain isolation. The following agar media were used: actinomycete
isolation agar medium, YPM agar medium, A4 agar medium, K agar
medium, Czapek-Dox agar medium, ISP1 agar medium, and ISP4 agar
medium. All media were supplemented with 100 mg/L of
cycloheximide. Subsequently, the plates were incubated at 30 °C for
2 weeks. Single strains of HSH236 were isolated on a Czapek-Dox
agar. Analysis of the 16S rRNA gene sequences showed that the
HSH236 strain was most closely related to Serratia marcescens (98%
identity, GenBank accession no. OQ359418).
Cultivation and Extraction. The HSH236 strain was cultured in
50 mL of YEME medium in a 125 mL Erlenmeyer flask. Next, the
contents of the flask were centrifuged at 200 rpm (30 °C). After 2
days of incubation, 10 mL of the liquid culture was transferred into a
500 mL baffled Erlenmeyer flask containing 200 mL of the YEME
medium and shaken for 3 days. Fifteen milliliters of the culture from
the HSH236 strain was inoculated in 1 L of modified K medium in
2.8 L flasks. Each 2.8 L flask contained 1 L of distilled water, 3 g of
yeast extract, 5 g of malt extract, 5 g of soytone, 5 g of soluble starch, 5
g of mannitol, and 2 g of glucose. The contents of these flasks were
stirred at 170 rpm for 5 days at 30 °C. The HSH236 culture medium
(90 L) was extracted twice with 135 L of ethyl acetate (EtOAc) using
separation funnels. Water remaining in the EtOAc layer was removed
by using anhydrous sodium sulfate. The resulting extract was
concentrated in vacuo to yield dry material (20 g).
Purification of Serratiomycins (1−4). The extracts were divided
into five fractions and separately adsorbed on 4 g of Celite, loaded on
a C18 resin column (5.0 × 12.0 cm), and fractionated sequentially
with 500 mL aliquots of 60%, 80%, and 100% MeOH in H2O to yield
three fractions. Compounds 1−4 were detected in the 100% MeOH−
H2O fraction. Further purification using reversed-phase chromatog-
raphy was performed to obtain the purified compounds. The 100%
MeOH−H2O fraction was injected directly into a preparative
reversed-phase HPLC (Kromasil C18, 5 μm, 250 × 10 mm, flow
rate: 2 mL/min, detection: UV = 210 nm) using an isocratic solvent
system of 85% CH3CN−H2O over 40 min. Serratiomycins (1−4)
were isolated after 28, 28.5, 29, and 31 min. They were further
purified by using a reversed-phase HPLC column (CHIRALPAK IB,
5 μm, 250 × 4.6 mm). Gradient conditions of 50−80% CH3CN−
H2O were used over 30 min (UV detection at 210 nm, flow rate: 0.6
mL/min) to yield 1 (tR 28 min, 10.3 mg), 2 (tR 30 min, 4.5 mg), 3 (tR
31.5 min, 4.1 mg), and 4 (tR 33 min, 3.7 mg).

Determination of the Absolute Configurations at the α-
Carbons of the Amino Acid Units. One milligram of 1 was
dissolved in 1 mL of 6 N HCl and heated at 115 °C for 1 h. The
heated suspension was cooled in an ice bath for 3 min, and HCl was
vaporized in vacuo. To eliminate the residual HCl, 1 mL of water was
added to the vial, which was evaporated twice under low pressure.
The hydrolysate mixture was lyophilized for 24 h and divided into two
vials. Each hydrolysate sample was dissolved in 480 μL of 1 N
NaHCO3. Next, 2 mg of L-FDAA and D-FDAA were added to each
vial. The two vials were then heated at 80 °C for 3 min to accelerate
chemical derivatization. Then, 80 μL of 6 N HCl was added to both
vials to neutralize their contents, and the two reaction mixtures were
diluted using 500 μL of MeOH. Ten microliters of each reaction
mixture was injected into the LC−MS instrument under a gradient
solvent system (flow rate: 0.7 mL/min; UV detection: 360 nm; 10%
to 60% CH3CN−H2O with 0.1% formic acid over 40 min) using a
Phenomenex column (Luna, 100 × 4.6 mm, C18, 5 μm) (Table S1).
The retention times of the FDAA derivatives of free amino acids were
determined by using LC−MS analysis.
Determination of the Absolute Configurations at the β-
Carbons of the L-allo-Isoleucine Units. A hydrolysate of 1 (1.0
mg) was obtained using the procedure described above. For the
chemical derivatization of the L-allo-isoleucine residue in 1, 100 μL of
triethylamine and 100 μL of a 1% GITC solution in acetone were
added to the reaction vial. The reaction was performed at room
temperature (25 °C) for 20 min, and the reaction mixture was
quenched with 100 μL of 5% acetic acid solution. Subsequently, 20
μL of the reaction mixture was analyzed using HPLC under a gradient
solvent system (flow rate: 0.3 mL/min; UV detection: 254 nm; 40%
to 50% CH3CN in water with 0.1% formic acid over 80 min) with a
Phenomenex column (Gemini, 250 × 4.6 mm, C18, 5 μm). GITC
derivatives of the L-isoleucine and L-allo-isoleucine standards were
prepared via the same procedure and injected into the HPLC system
under the same analysis conditions. The GITC derivatives of L-
isoleucine and L-allo-isoleucine were eluted at 36.2 and 37.3 min,
respectively. Finally, the amino acid residue in 1 was identified as L-
allo-isoleucine using HPLC coinjection analysis.
