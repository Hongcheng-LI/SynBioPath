ABSTRACT: Fungal polyketide synthase−nonribosomal peptide
synthetase (PKS−NRPS) hybrids have been characterized to
produce polyketide−amino acid compounds with striking struc-
tural features and biological activities. In this study, a PKS−NRPS
hybrid enzyme was found in Aspergillus terreus by genome mining.
By activating the cluster-speciﬁc transcriptional regulator, this
cryptic PKS−NRPS gene cluster was successfully activated and ten
products (1−10) were identiﬁed as pyranterreones. Using
functional genetics, bioinformatics, and isotope-labeling feeding
analysis, the biosynthetic pathway was revealed. This is the second
PKS−NRPS hybrid identiﬁed in A. terreus.
F
ilamentous fungi are a huge resource of bioactive nature
products, producing, for example, the antibiotic penicillin
and the antihypercholesterolemia lovastatin.1 The growing
number of sequenced fungal genomes revealed that a great
number of biosynthetic gene clusters of secondary metabolites
are presenting in ﬁlamentous fungi, but most of these gene
clusters are silent under laboratory conditions.2,3 These
bioinformatics studies suggest that the potential of fungi to
produce natural products is far more than previously
expected.3,4 Aspergillus terreus produces lovastatin and itaconic
acid. The genome of A. terreus NIH2624 contains 28
polyketide synthase (PKS) genes, 22 nonribosomal peptide
synthetase (NRPS) genes, and 1PKS−NRPS hybrid gene.5
Most of these gene clusters are silent, and products remain to
be identiﬁed.
To investigate the products from these clusters, the genome
of A. terreus MEFC01 was resequenced. The ATEG_00913,
which was previously regarded as a PKS in A. terreus NIH2624,
was reannotated as a polyketide synthase−nonribosomal
peptide synthetase (PKS−NRPS) hybrid (Figure 1A).5 This
diﬀerence may be due to errors in the sequencing or the
automatic annotation of genome. The protein encoded by the
reannotated ATEG_00913 gene consists of a highly reducing
PKS module (KS-AT-DH-ER-KR-ACP), a single NRPS
module (C-A-PCP), and C-terminal TD domain. It is the
second PKS−NRPS hybrid found in A. terreus besides
ATEG_00325.5,6 In addition, the gene cluster was identiﬁed
by antiSMASH and two new genes (g7163, g7169) were
discovered by reannotation (Figure 1B). The GenBank
accession numbers of the reannotated genes are listed in the
Experimental Section.
The transcriptome analysis showed that this gene cluster
(ATEG_00913) was transcriptionally silent under laboratory
conditions. Overexpression of the pathway-speciﬁc tran-
scription factor (sTF) is a useful way to awaken the silent
gene clusters, and this approach has been successfully applied
in many species.7−10 In the ﬂanks of the gene cluster of
ATEG_00913, there are two putative sTF encoding genes,
ATEG_00903 and ATEG_00919. To activate the expression of
these two sTF candidates, the endogenous promoters were
exchanged by the constitutive promotor PgpdAt, respectively,
resulting
in
the
mutants
MEFC01-PR00903
and
MEFC01-PR00919 (Figure S1a). Compared to the control
strain MEFC01-Δku80, the mutant MEFC01-PR00919 showed
signiﬁcant diﬀerences in both solid and liquid cultures, while
mutant MEFC01-PR00903 did not show any diﬀerence (Figure
2A). Additionally, the HPLC traces demonstrated that six new
peaks (1−6) emerged in the liquid submerged cultures of
MEFC01-PR00919 (Figure 2B). Similar results were observed
in the solid state cultures (Figure S2). The coding sequence
(CDS) of ATEG_00919 in the strain MEFC01-PR00919 was
selectively disrupted. The mutant lost the ability to produce
these compounds, which further conﬁrmed that the bio-
synthetic pathway was speciﬁcally activated by ATEG_00919
(Figure 2).
The structures of these metabolites were elucidated after a
scaled up cultivation and puriﬁcation (Figure 2C). Five
compounds were characterized as new natural products, and
one, cordylactam (4), which has been isolated previously from
the culture of a Cordyceps sp., was also identiﬁed.11

Compounds 1 and 2 are two cis−trans-isomers on the Δ7
double bond, and 3 possesses an exo-methylene attached to C-
7 rather than a methyl in 4. Compounds 5 and 6 have γ-lactam
moieties and may be biosynthetic intermediates of 3 and 1. All
of these metabolites contain the characteristic structure of
tetramic acid, which is similar to the pyranonigrin G generated
by pyn cluster in A. niger and denoted as pyranterreones12 but
diﬀering aliphatic chains between pyranonigrin G and
pyranterreones. In addition, a methyl group is present on the
amide nitrogen of pyranonigrin G, which is absent in
pyranterreones.

signiﬁcantly improved in the mutant MEFC01-PR00919
(Figure 3A). These results indicated that these genes were
speciﬁcally activated by ATEG_00919 and belonged to the
cluster. The ATEG_00913 displays a similar domain structure
and 49% sequence identity to that of PynA of A. niger, implying
ATEG_00913 maybe the homologue of PynA. Although the
gene order is diﬀerent, the assemblages of the tailoring

The biosynthetic pathway was proposed according to the
gene deletion results and the similarity with that of
pyranonigrins in A. niger (Figures 4 and 5).12 As expected,
the ΔpytA mutant did not produce any detectable amounts of
pyranterreones, which further demonstrated that the backbone
was formed by PytA. The deletion of PytI, a thioesterase,
showed the same proﬁle to ΔpytA mutant, implying that PytI
was responsible for the release of the intermediate from the
ACP domain of PytA (Figure 4). The structures and functions
of C-terminal domain of PKSs and NRPSs are diverse.13 In
some identiﬁed PKS−NRPSs, TD domains were considered to
be involved in the products cyclization and release.14 Here, the
TD domain of PytA might be involved only in the formation of
tetramic acid but not in product release.
The metabolite proﬁles from mycelium and supernatant of
MEFC01-PR00919 fermentation culture were detected, which
revealed that the pyranterreones are mainly extracellular. The
titers of the pyranterreones in mutant ΔpytF were reduced
with varying degrees, and the decrease of compound 1 was the
biggest. Except for intermediate 5, all other compounds were
only detectable in trace amounts. It implies that the MFS
encoded by pytF is involved in the transportation of the
pyranterreones (Figure 4). The secretions of pyranterreones
were blocked by the deletion of pytF, which led to feedback
suppression of the biosynthetic pathway.
Compounds 7 and 8 accumulated in mutant ΔpytH, while
1−4 disappeared (Figure 4). According to the structures, it was
expected that 7 would be either dehydrated to aﬀord 3 or
oxidized to give 8. Furthermore, the function of aspartyl
protease PynH, a homologue protein of PytH, was reported to
dehydrate a serine side chain to give the exo-methylene
structure.12 On the basis of the above information, PytH could
catalyze the dehydration of 7 to provide 3. The deletion of
pytH blocked the conversion from 7 to 3, and the redundant 7
might be further oxygenated to 8.
PytD is a ﬂavin-dependent oxidase, and PytG is a
cytochrome P450. The deletions of pytD or pytG showed the
same HPLC proﬁles with abolished production of compounds
1−4 and signiﬁcant accumulation of 5 (Figure 4). The absence
of γ-pyrone ring in compound 5 suggested PytD and PytG
were involved in the formation of γ-pyrone core.12 Compound
4 vanished while 3 accumulated in mutant ΔpytE. This result
indicated that PytE, a NADPH-binding reductase, could
catalyze the reduction of 3 to form 4 (Figure 4). This
speculation is consistent with the function of the homologue
PynE, which can reduce the exo-methylene into a pendant
methyl in the previous report in A. niger.12
Compared with the parental strain, mutant ΔpytB
accumulated two additional compounds 9 and 10 (Figure 4).
Compound 9 has been found previously in Warcupiella
spinulosa and named as 7-hydroxycordylactam.15 Compound
10 is the O-methylated derivative of 9 at the C-7 hydroxy. PytB
is annotated as FAD/FMN-containing dehydrogenase by
conserved domain analysis and shows 54% identity to PynB
of A. niger. Thus, according to the function of PynB, PytB
might catalyze the dehydrogenation of 4 to aﬀord 3. Therefore,
deletion of pytB blocked the conversion of 4 to 3, which
facilitated the following oxidization to produce compounds 9
and 10.
Compound 6 has one more methyl group at C-8 than 5.
This diﬀerence may be created in either one of two ways. One
is that the methyl is introduced in the process of the carbon
skeleton synthesis by PKS−NRPS due to the low substrate
speciﬁcity of adenylation (A) domain. Thus, compound 5 was
synthesized when the A domain selected serine as an extender
unit, and compound 6 was synthesized when threonine was
selected as an extender unit. The other is that compound 6 is
synthesized by a methyltransferase in a later modiﬁcation step.
To test these hypotheses, we fed L-[1-13C]-serine or L-[1-13C]-
threonine to MEFC01-PR00919 in an in vitro incorporation
assay and analyzed the isotopic peaks by HRESIMS. The 13C
abundance of 5 and 6 isolated from the
L-[1-13C]-serine
feeding culture was enhanced to diﬀerent extents, while the 13C

Thus, the biosynthetic pathway of the cryptic PKS−NRPS
gene cluster of ATEG_00913 was identiﬁed. First, four
malonyl-CoA units are condensed to the acetyl starter unit
by the modular PKS of PytA, and then the acyl chain is
connected to an L-serine through the amide bond by the
modular NRPS. A tetramic acid is formed and released from
the PKS−NRPS to give compound 5 with the help of
thioesterase PytI. Compound 5 could be methylated by PytC
to aﬀord 6. Both 5 and 6 are subsequently oxidized by FMO
(PytB) and P450 (PytD) to form the fused γ-pyrone core, and
this resulted in 7 and 11, respectively. The hydroxy group at C-
8 of 7 and 11 are dehydrated by the aspartyl protease PytH to
form a Δ7 double bond to give compounds 3 and 1, 2
accordingly. The exo-methylene of compound 3 could be
reduced into a pendant methyl by reductase PytE to provide 4.
Compound 4 can be reconverted to 3 through PytB-catalyzed
dehydrogenation or further oxidized to compounds 9 and 10.
This is the second PKS−NRPS gene cluster found in A. terreus

Compound 1, a yellow amorphous solid, has a molecular
formula of C14H15NO4 (eight degrees of unsaturation), as
determined by HRESIMS. The IR spectrum indicated the
presence of hydroxy (3255 cm−1) and conjugated carbonyl
(1716 cm−1) groups. The 1H and 13C NMR data of 1 (Table
S3) suggested the presence of γ-lactam-fused 4-pyrone moiety
and a trans-1-pentenyl side chain, which closely resembled
those of cordylactam (4) isolated from Cordyceps sp. BCC
12671.11 However, the methine (δH 4.57, δC 50.0) and methyl
(δH 1.36, δC 17.4) in 4 were absent in 1. Instead, one
trisubstituted double bond (δC 129.5, δH 5.98, δC 109.9) and
one methyl (δH 1.92, δC 12.9) connected with a double bond
were observed in the 1H and 13C NMR spectra of 1. The
1H−1H COSY correlations of H-8/H-9 and the HMBC
correlations from H-8 (δH 5.98) to C-7 (δC 129.5) and C-7a
(δC 162.6) revealed the linkage of an ethyl moiety at C-7 via a
double bond. In the NOE diﬀerent spectrum, irradiation of H-
6 enhanced the resonance of 9-CH3, indicating the Z geometry
of C7−C8. The NMR data of 2 closely resembled those of 1,
indicating that 2 might be a stereoisomer of 1 at double bond
position. The E geometry of C7−C8 was determined by the
NOE spectrum in which the signal of 6-H was enhanced when
irradiated the 8-H.

■EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
General Experimental Procedures. Optical rotations were
measured on a JASCO P-1020 digital polarimeter. UV spectra were
recorded on a Beckman DU-800. ECD spectra were recorded on a
JASCO J-815-150S circular dichroism spectrometer. IR spectra were
recorded on a Nicolet iN10 spectrometer using KBr pellets. NMR
spectra were acquired using a Bruker AVANCE III 600 NMR
spectrometer (600 MHz for 1H and 150 MHz for 13C). The 1H and
13C NMR chemical shifts are reported in parts/million (ppm) using
the resonance of residual solvent as reference. HRESIMS spectra were
obtained from a Dionex Ultimate 3000 system coupled with Bruker
Maxis Q-TOF spectrometer using positive and negative mode
electrospray ionization. Packing materials for column chromatography
were silica gel (Qing Dao Hai Yang Chemical Group Co.; 200−300
mesh) and octadecylsilyl (ODS) silica gel (Ji Nan Bo Na Biological
Technology Co.; 40 μm 120 Å).

Construction of A. terreus Mutant Strains. The genome of A.
terreus MEFC01 was resequenced and submitted to antiSMASH
analysis for gene cluster prediction. The PKS (ATEG_00913) was
reannotated as PKS−NPPS. The ﬂanking genes ATEG_00919 and
the corrected ATEG_00903 were both predicted as GAL4-like
Zn2Cys6 transcription factors. To activate this gene cluster, the native
promoter of ATEG_00903 and ATEG_00919 were replaced by
promoter PgpdAt. In brief, 1−1.5 kb upstream and downstream ﬂanks
of the core promoter region of ATEG_00903 and ATEG_00919 were
ampliﬁed from the genomic DNA and fused with the pyrGAn and
PgpdAt by fusion PCR, respectively. The gene-targeting cassettes for
promoter replacement were ampliﬁed from fusion PCR products
using nested primers and transformed into the parental strain A.
terreus MEFC01-Δku80-ΔpyrG using the protoplast−PEG method
(Figure S1a). The transformants were selected on PDAS and puriﬁed
through single spore isolation.
The same gene-targeting method was used for gene deletion except
for some details. The 1−1.5 kb upstream and downstream ﬂanks of
the target genes were ampliﬁed and fused with the hph marker by
fusion PCR. The gene deletion DNA cassettes were ampliﬁed using
nest primers and transformed into A.terreus MEFC01-PR00919 (Figure
S1b). Transformants were regenerated and selected on PDASH
(PDAS supplemented with 50 mM Hygromycin B). Oligonucleotides
are listed in Table S2.
Semiquantitative PCR on pyt Cluster Genes. A. terreus
MEFC01-Δku80 and MEFC01-PR00919 were grown on LPM
medium at 30 °C for 84 h. The total RNA samples were extracted
using TaKaRa MiniBEST plant RNA extraction kit. The cDNA were
synthesized using the PrimeScript RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser
(Perfect Real Time). Target genes were then ampliﬁed with Easy Taq
Mix using gene-speciﬁc primers, and genomic DNA of A. terreus
MEFC01 was used as a positive control during PCR ampliﬁcations.
The cDNA levels were normalized against that of actin gene
(ATEG_06973). The oligonucleotides used for fragment ampliﬁca-
tion are listed in Table S2.
Analysis of Secondary Metabolites. For fermentation analysis,
1 × 107 spores of each strain were inoculated in 50 mL of LPM
medium in 250 mL shake ﬂasks for 7 days at 30 °C and 200 rpm. The
cultures were extracted by equal volume of EtOAc, and the organic
solvent was concentrated in vacuo to provide an oily residue. The
extraction was redissolved in MeOH to prepare the concentration at
20 mg/mL. The samples were ﬁltered using 0.22 μm organic
membranes and examined by a Waters ACQUITY UPLC H-CLASS
(Waters Technologies) coupled with PDA eλ detector analysis with
an InﬁnityLab Poroshell 120 EC-C18 column (150 mm × 2.1 mm,
1.9 μm). Water (A) and acetonitrile (B), both with 0.05% (v/v)
formic acid, were used as solvents at a ﬂow rate of 0.5 mL/min. The
solvent gradient system for UPLC was 0−2.08 min 95%−76% A,
2.08−8.34 min 76%−38% A, 8.75−9.59 min 0% A, and 10.00−12.00
min 95% A. Semipreparative HPLC was performed on a HITACHI
Primaide 1110−1430 diode array detector system with a Waters
XBridge C18 column (150 mm × 10 mm, 5 μm) at a ﬂow rate of 3.0
mL/min.
Extraction and Isolation. A large-scaled fermentation (10 L) of
MEFC01-PR00919 and other mutants were performed in LPM
medium. The resulting cultivation was repeatedly extracted with
EtOAc until the organic phase was almost colorless. The crude extract
obtained by concentration of organic solvent was subjected to a
column chromatography for isolation. For the isolation of 1, 2, 3, 4, 5,
and 6, the extracts of MEFC01-PR00919 were subjected to
octadecylsilyl silica gel-vacuum liquid chromatography (ODS-VLC)
using gradient elution with MeOH−H2O (40%, 60%, and 100%) to
give three fractions (Fr.1−Fr.3). Fr.3 was directly puriﬁed by
semipreparative HPLC eluting with 35% ACN−H2O to yield
compounds 5 and 6 at retention times of 15.7 and 18.4 min,
respectively. Fr.2 was continuously separated on ODS-VLC eluting
with gradient mixtures of MeOH−H2O (50% and 60%) to give two
subfractions (Fr.2-1 and Fr.2-2). Compounds 3 and 4 were detected
at Fr.2-1 and puriﬁed by semipreparative HPLC eluting with 32%
ACN−H2O at retention times of 13.5 and 10.7 min. Compounds 1
and 2 were aﬀorded from Fr.2-2 through the HPLC eluting with 35%
ACN−H2O at retention times of 8.1 and 6.7 min, respectively. For
the isolation of 7 and 8, the extracts of MEFC01-PR00919-ΔpytH
were subjected to ODS-VLC using gradient elution. Compound 8 was
detected in 40% MeOH fraction and puriﬁed by semipreparative
HPLC eluting with 32% ACN−H2O at a retention time of 4.6 min.
Compound 7 was directly obtained by ODS column eluting with 60%
MeOH. For the puriﬁcation of 9 and 10, the extracts of

was carried out in MEFC01-PR00919 strain. MEFC01-PR00919 was
cultured in 50 mL AMM medium (http://www.fgsc.net/methods/
anidmed.html) with 70 g/L glucose (AMM-G70) at 30 °C for 48 h
with 220 rpm. The mycelia were ﬁltered by sterile miracloth and
washed twice using AMM-G10 liquid medium. Then the mycelia were
divided and transferred into 5 mL of fresh AMM-G10 liquid medium
containing 2.5 mM L-[1-13C]-serine or L-[1-13C]-threonine, and the
fermentation was performed for an additional 72 h at 30 °C with 220
rpm. The culture was extracted by 10-fold volume (50 mL) of EtOAc,
and the organic solvent was concentrated in vacuo to provide crude
extracts. Compounds 5 or 6 were isolated from the extracts by HPLC
and subjected to HRESIMS detection.
