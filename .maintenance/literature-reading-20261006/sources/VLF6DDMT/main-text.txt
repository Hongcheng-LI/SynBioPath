ABSTRACT: Iso-Migrastatin (iso-MGS) and lactimidomycin
(LTM) are glutarimide-containing polyketide natural products
(NPs) that are biosynthesized by homologous acyltransferase
(AT)-less type I polyketide synthase (PKS) assembly lines. The
biological activities of iso-MGS and LTM have inspired numerous
efforts to generate analogues via genetic manipulation of their
biosynthetic machinery in both native producers and model
heterologous hosts. A detailed understanding of the MGS and
LTM AT-less type I PKSs would serve to inspire future
engineering efforts while advancing the fundamental knowledge
of AT-less type I PKS enzymology. The mgs and ltm biosynthetic
gene clusters (BGCs) encode for two discrete ATs of the
architecture AT-enoylreductase (AT-ER) and AT-type II thioesterase (AT-TE). Herein, we report the functional characterization
of the mgsB and ltmB and the mgsH and ltmH gene products, revealing that MgsB and LtmB function as type II thioesterases (TEs)
and MgsH and LtmH are the dedicated trans-ATs for the MGS and LTM AT-less type I PKSs. In vivo and in vitro experiments
demonstrated that MgsB was devoid of any AT activity, despite the presence of the conserved catalytic triad of canonical ATs. Cross-
complementation experiments demonstrated that MgsH and LtmH are functionally interchangeable between the MGS and LTM
AT-less type I PKSs. This work sets the stage for future mechanistic studies of AT-less type I PKSs and efforts to engineer the MGS
and LTM AT-less type I PKS assembly lines for novel glutarimide-containing polyketides.
■INTRODUCTION
The glutarimide-containing polyketide natural products (NPs)
exhibit a range of valuable biological activities, all of which are
intricately tied to their polyketide scaffolds. Iso-migrastatin (1,
iso-MGS), lactimidomycin (2, LTM), migrastatin (MGS, 3),
and cycloheximide (4, Figure 1A) exemplify this class of
NPs.1−7 Compounds 1 and 3 exhibit potent tumor cell
migration inhibitory activity and have inspired numerous drug
discovery efforts. Analogues of 3 have served as antimetastatic
cancer drug leads that target the actin-bundling protein
fascin.3,8−11 Lactimidomycin (2) is a potent inhibitor of
eukaryotic protein translation that specifically binds to the
initiating ribosomes,5 serving as a valuable probe for global
mapping of translation initiation sites in mammalian cells.12 In
contrast, 4 inhibits eukaryotic protein translation by binding
both the initiating and elongating ribosomes.13 The bio-
synthetic machinery by which Nature assembles 1−4 thus
provides a rare glimpse into how biological activity can be fine-
tuned by evolution.
Numerous analogues of 1 and 2 have been generated to
improve potency or expand their biological profile via total
synthesis, genetic engineering of their biosynthetic machinery,
or a combination of the two (semisynthesis).3,14,15 The
structural complexity of 1 and 2 makes synthetic efforts
quite laborious.3,16 Genetic manipulation of the biosynthetic
machinery should allow for precise structural editing of the
nascent 1 or 2 scaffolds. However, to date, only peripheral
modifications have been made via semisynthesis from
congeners of 1 or 2, including their biosynthetic intermediates
(i.e., 5−7, Figure 1B).15 A detailed understanding of the
biosynthetic machinery for the polyketide scaffolds of 1 and 2
could provide new opportunities to engineer the next-
generation structural analogues.

machinery provides an excellent opportunity to investigate

acyltransferase (AT)-less type I polyketide synthase (PKS)
assembly lines. The mgs biosynthetic gene cluster (BGC) from
Streptomyces platensis NRRL 18993 and the ltm BGC from S.
amphibiosporus ATCC 53964 have each been cloned and
characterized in detail.17−21 The genetic organization of the
two BGCs is essentially identical with mgs harboring an
additional three regulatory/tailoring genes compared to ltm
(mgsA, mgsI, and mgsJ) (Figure 1B). The domain architectures
of the mgsCDEFG and ltmCDEFG gene products, which
constitute the MGS and LTM AT-less type I PKSs,
respectively, are the same except for modules 8 and 9 (Figure
S1). Accordingly, the PKS assembly lines produce nearly
identical compounds (5 from MGS and 7 from LTM, Figure
1B). However, the domain organizations of the MGS and
LTM AT-less type I PKSs do not directly correlate to the
reductive processing cycles predicted for the nascent
polyketide scaffold (i.e., noncollinearity). While collinearity is
an established archetype for canonical type I PKSs, non-
collinearity is a common feature of AT-less type I PKSs.22 The
molecular mechanisms involved in noncollinear chemistry are
poorly understood, the studies of which are typically limited to
correlations between BGC architectures and NP structures.
The MGS and LTM AT-less type I PKSs are well-suited for
the detailed mechanistic study of AT-less type I PKS assembly
line chemistry and enzymology. This was recently demon-
strated by the characterization of the unprecedented “long-
range” dehydration catalyzed by MgsF-DH10 using a
genetically amenable strain capable of producing high titers
of biosynthetic intermediates.19 Genetic systems are well-
established for S. platensis NRRL 18993 (native producer for
1),17,21 S. amphibiosporus ATCC 53964 (native producer for
2),18 and Streptomyces albus SB11001 (a recombinant producer
for 1).19,23 Manipulations of 1 and 2 biosynthesis, in both
native producers (1 and 2) or heterologous model host (for 1),
have afforded recombinant strains that overproduce 1 (up to
210 mg/L)24 and 2 (up to 100 mg/L)20 under the optimized
medium and fermentation conditions.
The biosynthetic logic for characterized AT-less type I PKSs
requires minimally one discrete trans-AT that delivers malonyl-
CoA-derived extender units to each of the acyl carrier proteins
(ACPs) within an AT-less type I PKS (Figure 1C).25,22,26 AT-

less type I PKSs that utilize multiple extender units, such as
methylmalonyl-CoA or aminomalonyl-CoA, feature multiple
trans-ATs, each of which is highly specific for the extender unit
and the cognate ACP(s).27,28 The biosynthetic model of 1 and
2 requires only malonyl-CoA extender units, hence predicting
the need of only one malonyl-CoA specific trans-AT (Figure
S1). However, by all bioinformatics analysis, the mgs and ltm
BGCs are predicted to encode for two discrete malonyl-CoA
specific trans-ATs, i.e., MgsB and MgsH for 1 and LtmB, and
LtmH for 2 (Figure 1B,D).
trans-ATs are often multidomain proteins, where the N-
terminal AT domain is fused with one or several other
domains, most of which have no obvious relevance to trans-
AT-catalyzed chemistry. The gene products of all trans-AT
genes within characterized AT-less type I PKS-containing
BGCs function as ATs regardless of their architecture.
Characterized multidomain trans-AT architectures include
AT-enoylreductase (AT-ER), AT-AT, AT-AT-ER, and AT-
ACP, while single domain trans-ATs have also been identified
(Figure 1D and Table S4). The presence of two AT-encoding
genes in both mgs and ltm with overlapping predicted substrate
specificity suggests that they serve divergent functions, which
may be associated with their non-AT domains.
In addition to a predicted AT domain, MgsB and LtmB each
contain a predicted thioesterase (TE) domain, suggesting that
each may function as a type II TE. Type II thioesterases (type
II TEs) play an editing role in the biosynthesis of both
canonical and AT-less type I PKS-derived polyketide NPs. Like
trans-ATs, the type II TE interacts in trans with multiple ACPs
within a type I PKS and catalyzes the hydrolysis of acyl-S-ACPs
resulting from aberrant chain elongation events by ketosyn-
thases (KSs) or mispriming by ATs (Figure 1C).29,30 Without
the type II TE, a single misprimed ACP would cause the
“stalling” of an entire type I PKS assembly line. Accordingly,
the inactivation of type II TE genes within canonical or AT-
less type I PKS BGCs often results in greatly reduced yields for
the biosynthesis of the encoded polyketide NPs.31−33 While
these conclusions have been experimentally validated for
numerous type II TEs in the context of canonical PKS
assembly lines, studies involving type II TEs in the context of
AT-less PKSs are uncommon. The non-AT domains within
trans-ATs have received little experimental investigation, and
their functional relevance or necessity remains poorly under-
stood. This is not due to a lack of effort but rather a lack of
model systems enabling complementary in vivo experiments
with reliable in vitro studies.34
Herein, we report the functional characterization of the
mgsB, mgsH, ltmB, and ltmH genes within the mgs and ltm
BGCs, where the MgsH and LtmH, and MgsB and LtmB
proteins are of the AT-ER and AT-TE architecture,
respectively. We establish that only MgsH and LtmH exhibit
trans-AT activity, serving as the sole dedicated trans-ATs for
the MGS and LTM AT-less type I PKSs, and that MgsB and
LtmB are type II TEs, playing editing roles for the MGS and
LTM AT-less type I PKS assembly lines, respectively. We
further demonstrate that MgsH and LtmH can functionally
cross-complement their cognate AT-less type I PKS assembly
lines, setting the stage for future studies of AT-less type I PKS
enzymology and providing new opportunities to engineer the
MGS and LTM AT-less type I PKS assembly lines for novel
glutarimide-containing polyketides.
■MATERIALS AND METHODS
Instrumentation. HPLC analysis of fermentation extracts
containing 1 was performed with an Agilent 1260 Infinity II
LC equipped with an Agilent ZORBAX SB-C18 column (250
mm × 9.4 mm, 5 μm) at ambient temperature, using mobile
phases and gradients provided with the experimental details
below. LC-MS analysis of fermentation extracts containing 2
was performed with an Agilent 1260 Infinity LC, coupled to a
6230 TOF (HRESI), equipped with an Agilent Poroshell 120
EC-C18 column (50 mm × 4.6 mm, 2.7 μm) with a constant
temperature of 40 °C, using mobile phases and gradients
provided with the experimental details below. HPLC analysis
of protein samples was performed with a Varian HPLC system
equipped with Prostar 210 pumps, a photodiode array
detector, and a Phenomenex Jupiter C-18 column (250 mm
× 4.6 mm, 5 μm, 300 Å) at ambient temperature, using the
mobile phases and gradients provided with the experimental
details below. MS analysis of proteins was performed on an
Agilent (Palo Alto) 1000 HPLC-MSD SL instrument.
Phosphor imaging was performed on an LE phosphor screen
(Amersham Pharmacia).

Inactivation of ltmB and ltmH in S. amphibiosporus
ATCC 53964. The ltmB gene was inactivated in S.
amphibiosporus ATCC 53964 by λ-RED-mediated PCR
targeting mutagenesis.43 Primers ltmB-delF and ltmB-delR
(Table S1) were used to amplify the disruption cassette from
the template plasmid pIJ773, yielding a PCR product
containing the aac(3)IV apramycin resistance gene and oriT,
flanked by DNA sequences identical to the regions
surrounding ltmB. This cassette was used to replace ltmB on
cosmid pBS15001 by homologous recombination to provide
pBS15012 (ΔltmB). The mutant cosmid was transformed into
E. coli S17-1 and conjugated into the S. amphibiosporus wild-
type strain. Exconjugants with the desired double-crossover
recombination were selected based on apramycin resistance
and kanamycin sensitivity. The genotype of the mutant was
confirmed by Southern analysis (Figure S3), yielding SB15010
(ΔltmB) (Table S3). The SB15005 (ΔltmH) strain was
previously reported.18
Inactivation of mgsB and mgsH in S. albus J1074. The
protocol was originally adopted from a previous study44 and
has been used to edit other BGCs in model Streptomyces
hosts.45 The mgs BGC-containing pBS11001 (a bacterial
artificial chromosome or BAC)23 and the pCas plasmid were
each introduced into DH5α via electroporation to yield the E.
coli cloning host, which served as the host for gene editing. The
pTargetF plasmid was used as the template for PCR using
primers sgRNA-mgsB-F or sgRNA-mgsH-F for mgsB or mgsH,
respectively, each with the reverse primer pTargetF-R. The
PCR products were digested with SpeI, and self-ligation was
performed with T4 DNA ligase, yielding the gRNA scaffold-
encoding plasmids. A 1 kb editing template with a 500 bp
upstream region and a 500 bp downstream region flanking the
target gene was generated by overlapping PCR using the
primers indicated in Table S1. The pTargetF plasmids and
corresponding 1 kb editing templates were cotransformed into
E. coli DH5α harboring both pBS11001 and pCas via
electroporation, and the resultant colonies were screened via
colony PCR using the primers indicated in Table S1. The
pTargetF and pCas plasmids were cured according to the
previously described protocol,44 and the resultant edited BACs,
pBS11032 (ΔmgsB), and pBS11033 (ΔmgsH) were isolated
and verified by Sanger sequencing using the primers indicated
in Table S1. The BACs were transformed into E. coli
ET12567/pUZ8002 and conjugated into S. albus J1074 to
yield SB11027 (ΔmgsB) and SB11028 (ΔmgsH), respectively
(Table S3).

Fermentation and Analysis of Strains Producing 1. S.
albus strains, harboring the mgs BGC and engineered variants
(Table S3), were cultivated on MS agar with antibiotics as
needed for sporulation. Fresh spores were harvested and
inoculated into baffled 250 mL flasks containing 50 mL of TSB
with antibiotics as needed for selection. After shaking at 28 °C
for 2 d, 5 mL of seed culture was added to baffled 250 mL
flasks containing 50 mL of fermentation media, consisting of
sucrose 20%, glucose 1%, bacto soytone 2%, yeast extract 1.5%,
(NH4)2SO4 0.2%, and CaCO3 0.2% (pH 7.0), supplemented
with 4% (w/v) XAD-16 resin. After shaking at 28 °C for 5 d,
the resin was isolated and washed with water, air-dried
overnight, then extracted with 20 mL of anhydrous EtOH, and
analyzed with HPLC. Compound separation was performed on
an Agilent ZORBAX SB-C18 column (250 mm × 9.4 mm, 5
μm) at a flow rate of 3 mL/min (solvent A, H2O; solvent B,
CH3CN), with UV detection at 205 nm. Samples (10 μL) were
injected and eluted with a linear gradient of 15% B, 0 min; 65%
B, 20 min; 95% B, 25 min. Under these conditions, the
retention time of 1 was 21 min. P values were calculated using
the appropriate t-test from the Analysis ToolPak in Microsoft
Excel, specifically the “t-Test: Two-Sample Assuming Equal
Variances” for Student’s t-test and the “t-Test: Two-Sample
Assuming Unequal Variances” for the Welch’s t-test.
Fermentation and Analysis of Strains Producing 2. S.
amphibiosporus wild-type and recombinant strains (Table S3)
were cultivated on ISP4 agar with antibiotics as needed for
sporulation. Fresh spores were harvested and inoculated into
baffled 250 mL flasks containing 50 mL of seed culture
medium containing antibiotics when needed. After shaking at
28 °C for 2 days, 5 mL of seed culture was added to baffled
250 mL flasks containing 50 mL of fermentation medium. The
seed and fermentation media each consisted of dextrin 2%,
pharmamedia 0.5%, soytone peptone 0.8%, yeast extract 0.2%,
and CaCO3 0.2% (pH 7.0), and the fermentation medium was
supplemented with 3% (w/v) XAD-16 adsorbent resin. After
shaking at 28 °C for 7 days, the resin was isolated and washed

Molecular Modeling and Molecular Dynamics. With-
out crystal structures of MgsB or MgsH, five homology models
of each were created using AlphaFold, and the best-scoring
model for each protein was relaxed using AMBER.48,49 The AT
regions of MgsB (Met1−Ala277) and MgsH (Met1−Ser278)
were extracted from the structures, and the resultant structures
were subjected to 50 ns molecular dynamics simulations using
YASARA.50,51 The simulations were set up using an
optimization of the hydrogen bonding network to increase
the solute stability and a pKa prediction to fine-tune the
protonation states of protein residues at the chosen pH of
7.4.52,53 Na+ and Cl−ions were added at a physiological
concentration of 0.9%, with an excess of Na+ to neutralize the
cell. After the steepest descent and simulated annealing
minimizations to remove clashes, the simulation was run for
50 ns using the AMBER14 force field for the solute, GAFF2
and AM1BCC for the ligand, and TIP3P for water.54−56 The
cutoff was 8 Å for van der Waals forces, and no cutoff was
applied to electrostatic forces (using the Particle Mesh Ewald
algorithm).57,58 The equations of motions were integrated with
a multiple-time step of 2.5 fs for bonded interactions and 5.0 fs
for nonbonded interactions at a temperature of 298 K and a
pressure of 1 atm (NPT ensemble) using algorithms described
in detail previously.59 After inspection of the solute RMSD as a
function of simulation time (Figures S5 and S6), the first 10 ns
was considered equilibration time and excluded from further
analysis. Molecular graphics and analyses were performed with
UCSF Chimera.60
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Bioinformatics Analysis Predicting MgsB and LtmB as
AT-ERs and MgsH and LtmH as AT-TEs. Bioinformatics
analysis identified the AT domains of MgsB, MgsH, LtmB, and
LtmH all as functional trans-ATs and the TE domains of both
MgsB and LtmB as functional type II TEs (Figure 1E).
Sequence alignment of the four AT domains of MgsB, MgsH,
LtmB, and LtmH with the functionally and structurally
characterized trans-AT DszD revealed the conserved Ser-Arg-
His catalytic triad (Figure S2A,B).37 Four motifs that have
been implicated in the catalytic mechanism of type I PKS ATs
were also conserved among the five sequences (Figure
S2A).61−63 AlphaFold models of the AT domains of MgsB
and MgsH were compared to the model of DszD built from a
crystal structure (PDB 3RGI),37 and the overall structures
were conserved along with the positioning of the four motifs
(Figure S2C). A sequence alignment of TE domains of MgsB
and LtmB with RifR, a functionally and structurally
characterized type II TE,38 revealed that the catalytic triad
Ser-Asp-His was conserved among the three sequences (Figure
S2D,E).
In Vivo Inactivation Revealing mgsH and ltmH to
Encode trans-ATs and mgsB and ltmB to Encode Type II
TEs. In vivo inactivation of mgsB and ltmB resulted in
significantly attenuated production of 1 and 2, while
inactivation of mgsH and ltmH abolished 1 and 2 production
entirely. For in vivo inactivation of mgsB and mgsH in
SB11001, an S. albus recombinant strain harboring a BAC of
the mgs BGC,23 in-frame deletions of mgsB and mgsH were
carried out via CRISPR/Cas-9 techniques in E. coli. The edited
BACs were transformed back into S. albus J1074, yielding
SB11027 (ΔmgsB) and SB11028 (ΔmgsH), respectively. For in
vivo inactivation of ltmB and ltmH in S. amphibiosporus ATCC
53694, in-frame deletion of ltmB was carried out using λ-RED-
mediated PCR targeting mutagenesis and confirmed by
Southern analysis (Figure S3), yielding SB15010 (ΔltmB),
with the SB15005 (ΔltmH) strain reported previously.18
Fermentation of the recombinant strains, with SB11001 (for
1) and S. amphibiosporus wild-type (for 2) as controls, were
carried out using previously optimized conditions,18 and the
resultant extracts were analyzed by LC-MS (Figure 2).
Inactivation of mgsH as in SB11028 completely abolished 1
production (Figure 2A, panel IV), and inactivation of mgsB as
in SB11027 decreased 1 production by 22-fold (Figure 2B,
panel III and Table 1). Similar results with 2 production were
obtained with the inactivation of ltmB as in SB15010 and ltmH
as in SB15005 (Figure 2C, panels III, V, and Table 1).

experiments demonstrated that mgsH was essential for 1
production, while disruption of mgsB resulted in greatly
reduced yields. These data suggested that mgsH encodes for
the sole trans-AT for the biosynthesis of 1, whereas mgsB
encodes for a functional type II TE. MgsB and MgsH were
each overproduced from E. coli, purified to homogeneity, and
assayed for their ability to catalyze self-malonylation. MgsB and
MgsH were each incubated with [2-14C]malonyl-CoA and
[2-14C]methylmalonyl-CoA followed by SDS-PAGE and
phosphor imaging (Figures 3A,B and S4). MgsB showed no
self-malonylation activity with either substrate. MgsH catalyzed
self-malonylation with malonyl-CoA as the substrate, and to a
lesser extent, also catalyzed self-malonylation with methyl-
malonyl-CoA as the substrate (Figure S4B). Self-malonylation
is required for a functional AT, and these results show that
MgsH could function as an AT, whereas MgsB could not.

Functional Characterization of MgsB as a Type II TE.
Radiolabeled acetyl- and propionyl-S-MgsG-ACP11 were

catalyzed hydrolysis. Incubation of apo-MgsG-ACP11 with Svp
and the corresponding [1-14C]acyl-CoA provided radiolabeled

MgsB functions as a type II TE with acetyl-S- and propionyl-S-

PKS assembly line.
Molecular Dynamics Simulations Reveal a Perturbed
Active Site in MgsB-AT Relative to Functionally
Characterized trans-ATs. Molecular dynamics (MD)
simulations provided a plausible rationale for the lack of
MgsB-AT catalytic activity, the validity of which remains to be
determined in future studies. The lack of MgsB-AT catalytic
activity was unexpected considering the sequence alignment
and structural modeling predictions (Figure S2A−C). By
sequence alignment, all four motifs identified in the AT regions
of MgsB and MgsH were present and nearly identical, and
AlphaFold models of both AT regions overlaid well with one
another (1.09 Å RMSD; Figure S2).48,61−63 Therefore, given
the flexible nature of ATs, 50 ns MD simulations were carried
out for the AT regions of both proteins to determine if the
missing AT activity in MgsB could be rationalized (Figure
S5A).61 No obvious differences in the catalytic Ser-His dyads
(Figure S5B) or the large and small AT subunits of MgsB and
MgsH could be observed (Figure S5C). Notably, the time-
averaged structures of MgsB-AT showed a loss of the large
subunit β-sheet, presumably due to the three missing N-
terminal residues in MgsB that make up half of the central β-
strand (Figure S6). The stability and folding of this β-sheet
would be expected to have a profound effect on self-
malonylation activities, as the sheet is directly connected to
the catalytic Ser84 and the Gln9 that has been previously
implicated in AT substrate hydrolysis.62 These predictions
warrant the investigation of AT-TE-encoding genes in the
context of uncharacterized AT-less type I PKS-containing
BGCs. An analysis of domain architectures in the Interpro
database revealed 25 predicted AT-TE sequences associated
with AT-less type I PKSs (Table S5), which are distinct from
MgsB and LtmB. The functional characterization of AT-TE
gene products in the context of these orphan BGCs will
provide further insight into the validity of the predictions made
here.

AT-less type I PKS chemistry and enzymology.46
■CONCLUSIONS
The polyketide NPs assembled by AT-less type I PKSs have
historically been a rich source of bioactive compounds and
continue to serve as inspiration for new medicines.9 However,
our fundamental understanding of how Nature evolves AT-less
type I PKS to biosynthesize the myriad of NPs remains
incomplete due to their deviations from the deceptively simple
collinearity biosynthetic logic known for type I PKSs.22
Anomalies in AT-less type I PKS enzymology include module
skipping,66 module stuttering,64 and, most markedly, modules
whose functions rely on trans-acting enzymes.67 Here, we show
that two genes within the same BGC appear to encode for
functional ATs, yet only one of the gene products performs the
predicted function. Furthermore, the complementary nature of
the mgs and ltm biosynthetic machinery was demonstrated in
this study, setting the stage for further mechanistic studies of
the structural and functional modularity of AT-less type I
PKSs.
The subtle structural differences between 1 and 2 lead to
significant differences in their biological effects and their
applications in drug discovery and fundamental chemical
biology research.9 The genetic engineering of microbial hosts
that produce analogues of 1 and 2 with scaffold modifications
is an attractive method to systematically study their structure−
activity relationships. The functional characterization of
discrete proteins that interact with the mgs and ltm PKS
assembly lines, as exemplified by this study, is essential to
future engineering efforts to produce 1 and 2 analogues.
Combinatorial biosynthesis has long been a goal of the field of
PKS engineering. Although great progress has been made for
the canonical type I PKS systems, this is far from true for the
AT-less type I PKSs.68 The mgs and ltm BGCs are highly
related, serving as fantastic model systems to engineer diversity
by applying both combinatorial and rational strategies to their
AT-less type I PKS biosynthetic machinery. The fact that the
discrete ATs for each pathway are functionally interchangeable
paves the way for detailed mechanistic studies involving the
protein−protein interactions between discrete ATs and the
ACPs throughout an AT-less type I PKS assembly line. This
foundational work also enables future campaigns to design and
engineer nonnatural analogues of these clinically relevant NPs
for further biological studies.
