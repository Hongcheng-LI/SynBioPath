ABSTRACT: Antibiotics are essential for modern medicine, but
their use drives the evolution of antimicrobial resistance (AMR)
that limits the long-term efficacy of any one drug. To keep pace
with AMR and preserve our ability to treat bacterial infections, it is
essential that we identify antibiotics with new structures and targets
that are not affected by clinical resistance. Historically, most
developmental candidates for antibiotics have come from microbial
natural products, as they feature chemical structures and biological
activities that have been honed over millions of years of evolution.
Unfortunately, as classical bioactivity screens for natural product
discovery are blind to the pharmacological properties of their hits, they often identify molecules with functional groups that limit
their utility as drugs. One prominent example is actinonin, an inhibitor of bacterial peptide deformylase (PDF) whose activity is
dependent on a hydroxamate moiety associated with toxicity in vivo. The abundance of bacterial genomes now presents an
opportunity for target-based natural product discovery, where biosynthetic pathways can be mined for molecules that possess desired
activities but lack known pharmacophores. Here, we use bioinformatics to lead a chemotype-sensitive, target-based search for natural
product inhibitors of bacterial PDF that lack the conserved metal chelating group. We describe the discovery, heterologous
expression, biosynthesis, total synthesis, and activity of the gammanonins: actinonin homologues from Gammaproteobacteria.
Moving forward, we hope this chemotype- and target-driven approach will help to expedite the discovery of new leads for antibiotic
development.
■INTRODUCTION
Antibiotics are keystone drugs that provide us an ability to
fight or prevent bacterial infections, thus enabling invasive
surgeries, immunosuppressive therapies, and other hallmarks of
modern medicine. Unfortunately, the use of antibiotics also
drives the development and dissemination of antimicrobial
resistance (AMR) that limits their viability.1 As many
antibiotics reuse chemical scaffolds or affect conserved targets
and binding sites, emergent resistance mechanisms can imperil
multiple generations of drugs. Multidrug resistant Gram-
negative bacteria now pose a particularly daunting threat, as
their outer membrane provides an intrinsic barrier that further
limits treatment options.2 The challenge of AMR highlights the
need for continued antibiotic discovery, with an emphasis on
targets and chemotypes not already impacted by clinical
resistance.3
Historically, microbial natural products have been our most
important source of antibiotics,4 as these molecules feature
privileged structures and activities that have evolved over
millions of years.5 Although bioactivity screening of microbial
extracts has produced many of the antibiotic scaffolds still used
today, such efforts have suffered from diminishing returns due
to oversampling, and can uncover toxic hits with limited
potential for development. A classic example is chloramphe-
nicol, an inhibitor of bacterial ribosomes discovered during a
bioactivity screen in 1947. Though effective at fighting
infections in humans, the para-nitrobenzene and dihaloalkane
moieties of chloramphenicol produce toxic metabolic by-
products following oral or intravenous administration, leading
to aplastic anemia that limits the use of this drug.6,7 Recent
examples include actinonin8 and matlystatin,9 hydroxamate
inhibitors of bacterial peptide deformylase (PDF; Figure 1a):
an essential enzyme that removes formate from the N-formyl-
methionine that initiates bacterial protein translation. Surpris-
ingly, resistance to actinonin is not achieved by mutations in
PDF, but through inactivation of methionyl-tRNA formyl-
transferase, yielding bacteria with significantly reduced fitness
and virulence.10 Despite the promise of PDF as an antibiotic
target, the hydroxamate present on natural inhibitors is
metabolically unstable and mutagenic in vivo.6,11 Actinonin
derivatives that have undergone clinical trials thus feature more

In the past decade, natural product discovery has undergone
a renaissance driven by microbial genome sequencing.13 Bio-
and chemo-informatic methods can now use antibiotic
biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) to guide the discovery of
new molecules from diverse bacteria.14,15 As antibiotic BGCs
often contain self-protection genes that act as resistant copies
of the associated target, molecules with specific activities can
be mined directly from genomes.16−18 This technique provides
a chance to revisit unrealized targets and find new leads with
alternative pharmacophores.
Here, we apply this target-centric mining approach to scan
BGCs for hydroxamate-free inhibitors of bacterial peptide
deformylase (PDF). This search revealed a novel BGC from
Gammaproteobacteria, encoding molecules that resemble
actinonin but lack the known hydroxamate. We report the
identification, isolation, heterologous expression, total syn-
thesis, biosynthesis, and activity of these new natural products,
the gammanonins.
■RESULTS
Profiling Candidate BGCs for PDF Inhibitors. To find
BGCs that likely encode antibiotic inhibitors of bacterial PDF,
we used the ClusterScout19 tool to search over 135,000
sequenced and annotated bacterial genomes from the
Integrated Microbial Genomes (IMG) database.20 Using
nonribosomal peptide synthetase (NRPS) domains as hooks,
we searched for BGCs with a colocalized PDF (<10 kb away).
This initial search returned 158 candidates, which could be
further refined to 73 leads by eliminating isolated domains,
BGCs for siderophores, and pathways positioned next to the
lone genomic PDF. We used CORASON21 to group leads into
21 distinct families of NRPS BGCs bearing a colocalized PDF
that could serve as a putative self-protection gene18 (Figure
1b). Among this series, 18 BGCs were found in prolific Gram
positive Actinomycetes,22 while a single candidate could be
found in Gram negative Cyanobacteria, Alphaproteobacteria,
and Gammaproteobacteria. Two BGCs (Figure S1, BGC12,
16) resembled matlystatin,9 while another (BGC10) likely
encodes the related hydroxamate-free inhibitor Sch-382583.23
Three BGCs (Figure S1, BGC1, 13, 21) matched or were
closely related to the pathway for lydiamycin,24 which
resembles Sch-382583, was recently described as a PDF
inhibitor,25 and includes a resistant PDF gene in its BGC.25 A
full series of representative BGCs recovered from this search is
available as a Supporting Data set, accompanied by an
annotated CORASON alignment (Figure S1).

The most compelling lead in our analysis was the BGC from
Gammaproteobacteria, including species of Vibrio and Photo-
rhabdus. This minimal pathway features one polyketide
synthase (PKS) module, three NRPS modules, a priming
phosphopantetheinyl transferase (PPTase), a pathway-specific
methyltransferase (MT), and critically, a PDF gene embedded
in the biosynthetic operon (Figure 1c, Table S1). The
predicted chemical product of this BGC is a polyketide-
extended tripeptidecomparable to actinonin, but without a
hydroxamate or other metal chelating group. Use of the
pathway-specific MT in NCBI BLAST searches revealed more
identical pathways in Xenorhabdus and Brenneria. This BGC
has also been linked to intragenus antibacterial activity in
Vibrio,26 suggesting it encodes an antibiotic. Given these
features, we chose to investigate this BGC in the hopes of
finding a new, hydroxamate-free inhibitor of bacterial PDF.
Investigating a Candidate BGC from Gammaproteo-
bacteria. We obtained Vibrio tubiashii DSM 19142 from the
German Collection of Microorganisms and Cell Cultures
(DSMZ) and repeated the culture and extraction conditions
previously reported to enrich for bioactivity from the Vibrio
ordalii isolate 12B09.26 Unfortunately, the reported UV signal
(300 nm) for this product was absent and a diagnostic mass
was never reported. As genetic inactivation in V. tubiashii
proved intractable, we chose to heterologously express this
pathway in the E. coli BL21(DE3) derivative BAP127 to find
BGC-associated products. The BGC was cloned into two pET
Duet plasmids, with inducible T7 promoters supporting the
expression of each assembly line gene as well as the
cotranscribed PDF and MT (Figure 1c). BAP1 E. coli with
this refactored pathway were grown in M9 minimal media,
induced, and incubated overnight at 37, 30, or 16 °C. Cultures
were centrifuged and the resulting cell pellets and supernatants
were extracted and analyzed by high-resolution liquid
chromatography-coupled mass spectrometry (HR-LCMS).
To identify metabolites associated with BGC expression, we
used Global Natural Product Social Molecular Networking
(GNPS)28 to compare our extracts of V. tubiashii and our
engineered E. coli. A series of peptide metabolites related to the
GNPS annotated leucine-valine dipeptide was shared between
V. tubiashii and BAP1 when induced at 16 °C (Figure 1d,e).
These three molecules are variably modified tripeptides,
present in very low concentrations in supernatants and cell
pellets. As an alternative, we made expression plasmids from
the matching BGC in Photorhabdus asymbiotica DSM 15149,
resulting in identical products in similarly low concentrations
(Figure S2). As heterologous expression of the V. tubiashii
BGC provided better yields than the native organism and
employed a chemically defined culture media, we pursued E.
coli as a producer for all future work.
To assess which metabolite represented the final product of
our BGC, we added stable isotopically labeled methionine and
acetate, which should reveal the involvement of the MT and
the terminating PKS module (Figure S3). While the methyl
group was seen in all metabolites, the PKS-associated acetate
was only found in 374.3 [M + H] (1), suggesting the other
molecules result from failed transmission between assembly
line enzymes. Adenylation domain substrate predictions29,30
suggested a composition of one L-leucine and two L-valine
residues (Table S2), corroborated by MS/MS of the 344.3 [M
+ H] tripeptide (2). To confirm this, we used solid phase
peptide synthesis (SPPS) to create tripeptide variants with
leucine, valine, isoleucine, and N-methyl-valine at different
positions. The synthetic tripeptide of N-methyl-L-valine-L-
valine-L-leucine was identical to the natural tripeptide (2;
Figure S4). A series of minor ions (358.3 [M + H]) were also
seen, corresponding to valine-to-leucine/isoleucine substitu-
tions by MS/MS. To resolve the structure of the complete
natural product 1, we grew, extracted, and processed 100 L of
our engineered E. coli producer strain. Combined cell pellet
and supernatant extracts were separated using size exclusion
chromatography and semipreparative LCMS to afford 7.8 mg
of 2 and 1.8 mg of 1 (Figure 2a), sufficient for structure
elucidation by NMR. As expected, 2 matches our synthetic
tripeptide, while 1 features a polyketide extended leucine diol
reminiscent of the residue statine31,32 (statinol).
Synthesis of 1. To assign the secondary alcohol in 1 we
established a total synthesis, generating the N-methylated
dipeptide by SPPS and coupling it to an appropriate isomer of
statinol. Synthesis of statinol followed an established route to
statine31,32 yielding R/S diastereomers of the Fmoc-L-statine
ethyl ester as key intermediates. NMR analysis of Mosher
esters33 was used to establish S as the dominant product (ee:
0.85), consistent with prior reports of statine synthesis.31
Following the isolation of each diastereomer, a second
reduction and deprotection yielded pure statinols. Coupling
the major isomer (L-S-statinol) with our dipeptide yielded a
product with an identical retention time and MS/MS
fragmentation pattern to 1 (Figures 2a and S5).
Resolving the Biosynthesis of 1. This BGC had several
unusual features that we wished to resolve. Most notably, α-N-
methylation is apparently achieved in trans, in contrast to
nearly34 all bacterial nonribosomal peptides which feature cis-
acting N-methyltransferase domains embedded in the NRPS.35
To investigate MT substrate preference, we made N-acetyl-
cysteamine (SNAC36) versions of each intermediate, expressed
and purified the MT, and performed methylation reactions in
vitro. LCMS indicated that the MT cannot methylate
individual amino acyl SNACs and preferentially acts on the
enzyme-bound polyketide-extended tripeptide (Figure 2b).
When the MT was removed from our expression plasmids, we
did not observe 1, 2, or unmethylated 2, but rather only
unmethylated 1 (Figure S6). The lack of signal for
unmethylated 2 suggests this tripeptide may be degraded by
the cell or that MT overexpression contributes to premature
release of 2 from the assembly line. Next, we sought to

Assessing the Activity of 1. Finally, we sought to test the
activity of 1 and the role of the BGC-associated PDF enzyme.
First, we removed the PDF from our plasmids and analyzed
culture extracts to confirm this gene is not involved in
biosynthesis. Removal did not result in the production of new
intermediates and had only a minor impact on yields of 1
(Figure S7). Surprisingly, the removal of the assumed self-
protection gene did not impact the growth of induced E. coli
(Figure S8). To test whether E. coli is intrinsically resistant to
1, we overexpressed and purified the E. coli PDF enzyme
(Figure S9). For testing, we used an in vitro end point assay to
measure the capacity of PDF to liberate a primary amine from
an N-formylated tripeptide substrate, detected by the
fluorogenic reporter molecule fluorescamine.38 We did not
observe inhibition of E. coli PDF by 1, in contrast to an
actinonin positive control (Figure S10). Given prior reports of
this BGC showing anti-Vibrio activity26 and the apparent self-
protection gene in the operon, we tested if 1 could inhibit
Vibrio PDF enzymes. We expressed and purified the BGC-
associated PDF along with house-keeping PDFs from V.
tubiashii and V. crassostreae a species with known sensitivity
to this BGC product26 (Figure S9). As with E. coli, we did not
see inhibition of PDF activity by 1 (Figure S10).
To assess whether a missing metal binding group may
explain the lack of bioactivity, we synthesized N-carboxymeth-
yl-L-valine-L-valine-L-(S)-statinol (3) and repeated our anti-
bacterial and enzyme inhibition assays. This modified product
did not show appreciable PDF inhibition (Figure S11) or
antibacterial activity (Table S3). While 3 could theoretically be
biosynthesized by using carboxy-S-adenosyl-methionine (Cx-
SAM39 as a cosubstrate in lieu of SAM, it was not observed in
extracted cultures from Vibrio or our E. coli expression system.
Overexpressing genes for Cx-SAM biosynthesis during BGC
induction also failed to yield the expected product.
Collectively, these studies suggested a bioactive product of
this BGC was absent in our native and heterologous producers.
Metabolomic Analysis of Bioactive Samples. To find
the molecule responsible for the antibacterial activity reported
from this BGC, we obtained the original producer strain (V.
ordalii 12B09) and a corresponding BGC knockout.26 Spot-on-
lawn assays using V. crassostreae as an indicator revealed a clear
zone of inhibition associated with wildtype 12B09 that was
absent with either the knockout or V. tubiashii (Figure 3a).
Extraction, HR-LCMS processing, and GNPS analysis of
12B09 cultures revealed a series of abundant molecules that
were related to 1 but were not seen in prior analyses (Figure
3b,c). Oddly, cultures of P. asymbiotica also contained these
metabolites, which were not seen when these exact genes were
expressed in E. coli (Figures S2 and S12). Based on their
masses and fragmentation patterns, these molecules varied
from 1 in having a terminal aldehyde instead of the observed
alcohol (4−5). The complete suite of observed masses could
then be explained by spontaneous dehydration of a reactive β-
hydroxy-aldehyde (6), and conversion from an aldehyde to
alcohol by cellular aldehyde reductase (7) (Figure S13).
Discovery and Description of Gammanonin. To find
the product responsible for antibacterial activity we iteratively
fractionated extracted cultures of V. ordalii 12B09 and
performed bioactivity assays against V. crassostreae (Figure
S14). Antibacterial activity was exclusively tied to a single peak
seen in HR-LCMS analysis (372.3 [M + H], 4; Figure 3c).
During isolation, this molecule slowly converted to a 354.3 [M
+ H] ion and a second 372.3 [M + H] ion that eluted earlier,
immediately after 1. Structure elucidation by 2D NMR
revealed the active molecule to be a cyclic hemiaminal,
explaining its inherent reactivity and distinct in-source
fragmentation during LCMS. Given its similarity to actinonin
and origins in Gammaproteobacteria, we named this new
molecule gammanonin (4, Figure 4).
The cyclic hemiaminal of gammanonin is reminiscent of the
tetrahydroisoquinoline alkaloids,40 where this feature acts as a
covalent, electrophilic warhead. To assess whether hemiaminal
formation is spontaneous, we synthesized the linear aldehyde
5. SPPS was initiated with Weinreb AM resin loaded with
Fmoc-L-(S)-statine, which was extended to the tripeptide and
released as an aldehyde by cleaving from the resin with lithium
aluminum hydride.41 HR-LCMS revealed the formation of the
aldehyde, corresponding to the earlier eluting 372.3 [M + H]
ion (5), along with the presumed α,β-unsaturated aldehyde (6,
354.3 [M + H]) and a dimer caused by an intermolecular aldol
side reaction. The hemiaminal 4 was a minor component,

Biological Activity of Gammanonin. Having isolated 4
by bioactivity-guided fractionation, we next wished to assess
whether it inhibited bacterial PDF. Using the same assay we
employed previously, we found gammanonin was capable of
inhibiting PDFs from E. coli, V. crassostreae, and the house-
keeping copy from V. tubiashii (Figure 5a−c). Dose response
comparisons show 4 is less potent than actinonin, and that
inhibition could be improved by preincubating PDF with 4
prior to substrate addition (Figure S16) consistent with a
covalent mechanism of action. As expected, the BGC
associated PDF (GamD) displayed a degree of resistance to
4 (Figure 5d). A phylogenetic tree of Gammaproteobacteria
PDF sequences show that GamD genes from diverse bacteria
form a distinct clade (Figure S17), in line with the expectation
that this protein is evolved to protect against gammanonin.
Such PDF comparisons also showed conserved residues that
were altered in GamD, including amino acids found near the
actinonin binding site42 (Figure S18a). In particular, a
conserved cysteine found in all non-GamD PDF sequences
from Gammaproteobacteria attracted our attention as a
potential site for gammanonin reactivity. However, mutating
this cysteine to the valine found in GamD was insufficient to
grant gammanonin resistance (Figure S18b,c).
To finish our investigation of gammanonin we used
multiwell serial dilutions to determine minimum inhibitory
concentrations (MICs) against a panel of diverse bacteria.
Unsurprisingly, our indicator strain V. crassostreae was sensitive
to gammanonin, its growth being partially inhibited by 4 μg
mL−1 and completely inhibited by 8 μg mL−1. However,
excluding marginal activity toward the closely related V.
anguillarum (16 μg mL−1) all other bacteria were surprisingly
unaffected, including a tolC-deficient E. coli (Table S3). As
Vibrio and E. coli PDFs are established to be gammanonin-
sensitive, target-independent resistance likely complicates
whole cell antibacterial activity. To assess which bacteria may
be potentially sensitive to gammanonin, we scanned bacterial
genomes for instances where GamD had been acquired as an
apparent resistance gene. Lone GamD homologues could be
found in isolates of Klebsiella, Yersinia, Acinetobacter, and other
Gammaproteobacteria (Figure S19). Revisiting our inhibition
assay using a copy of this enzyme from insensitive Streptococcus
agalactiae, we found that 4 was effectively inactive, suggesting it
may be specific for PDF from Gram negative bacteria (Figure
S20).
■DISCUSSION
Microbial natural products feature chemical structures and
biological activities that have been honed through natural
selection to provide a fitness advantage to their producer.
Unsurprisingly, these evolved molecules have served as vital
leads for pharmaceuticals.4 In this work, we present a
chemotype-sensitive approach to natural product genome
mining, with the goal of identifying antibacterial inhibitors of
peptide deformylase that lack the hydroxamate11 group found
in prior developmental leads like actinonin.8,9 Using this
approach, we successfully identified a series of BGCs for
suitable antibiotics (Figure 1b), including a reported BGC for
lydiamycin24,25 and a likely pathway for the known PDF
inhibitor Sch-382583.23 The gammanonin BGC stands out
among these hits, as it is found in several Gram negative
Gammaproteobacteria and features a simple, highly conserved
operon structure that includes a cotranscribed PDF gene
(Figure 4). The presence of the gam pathway in Vibrio species
is unusual, as this genus has only previously been known to
produce nonribosomal peptide siderophores43−45 and the
similarly widely distributed antibiotic andrimid.46 While the
gam BGC had been linked to antagonistic “superkiller” Vibrio
isolates,26 this antibacterial phenotype appears to require high
basal levels of expression, as is apparent in V. ordalii 12B09
(Figure 3a).

Despite its small size, the gam BGC contains several unusual
features. The trans-acting N-methyltransferase GamE is, to our
knowledge, the first instance of on-assembly line α-N-
methylation being performed in trans, as this is typically
achieved by cis-acting domains35 or following release from the
NRPS.34 We are similarly unaware of other examples of α-N-
methylation occurring on an elongated enzyme-bound peptide.
While we suspect that N-methylation is likely important for
recognition by PDF, this modification may also serve to limit
formation of inactive imine macrocycles following release from
PKS. On that point, the reductase (Re) domain of the GamC
PKS is similarly odd, bearing limited sequence similarity to the
many well-characterized Re domains that are abundant in
NRPS and PKS BGCs.47 Given the levels of 4 in culture
extracts relative to those from aldehyde synthesis, it is tempting
to speculate that the Re domain actively directs formation of
the hemiaminal, which later decays to equilibrium with the free
aldehyde. Proper stoichiometry of gam biosynthetic genes is
evidently essential for the formation of bioactive products, as
shown by the accumulation of 2 when overexpressing GamE,
or the contrasting metabolomic profiles from native and
heterologous expression of the P. asymbiotica pathway.
Similarly, we expect these differences in expression may
explain why the effectively silent gam BGC in V. tubiashii fails
to produce 4 under laboratory conditions, despite the
reductase sharing >93% pairwise identity with the 12B09
homologue.
Peptide deformylase is an unrealized clinical target for
antibiotics.2 This small metalloenzyme plays an essential role
in bacterial protein translation by removing formate from the
initiating N-formyl-methionine found on nascent polypeptides.
Prior developmental leads for PDF inhibitory antibiotics have
been derived from actinonin, which uses an N-terminal
hydroxamate to access and chelate the active site metal.48
Resistance to actinonin is surprisingly achieved by inactivating
mutations in methionyl-tRNA formyl-transferase, yielding slow
growing mutants with poor fitness in vivo.10 While actinonin
derivatives underwent phase II clinical trials for acute bacterial
skin and skin structure infection and displayed efficacy
comparable to linezolid, they had a less favorable safety
profile.12 These optimized actinonin derivatives already
featured the less effective but more metabolically stable
reverse-hydroxamate. Further substitution with less potent
metal binding groups results in less ineffective inhibitors.49
Such molecules are also often capable of inhibiting human
metalloenzymes,50 including the mitochondrial copy of PDF,51
presenting an additional challenge for their development.
Gammanonin (4) appears to offer a new means of inhibiting
PDF. Despite its resemblance to actinonin, 4 lacks a functional
group capable of engaging the active site metal. Bioactivity-
guided fractionation identified 4 as the sole active component
of V. ordalii 12B09 culture extracts, indicating the conspicuous
hemiaminal is essential for antibacterial activity. This func-
tional group typically acts as a covalent, electrophilic
warhead.40 As such, we expect gammanonin to inhibit PDF
by labeling a nucleophilic residue and occluding the substrate
binding pocket.52 This apparently distinct mechanism suggests
that hybrid antibiotics with metal-binding and hemiaminal
warheads could offer a new pathway to improve target
specificity and PDF inhibition. Still, while the hemiaminal
warhead of 4 was necessary for antibacterial activity, it remains
unclear how this moiety engages its target, and whether 4 is the
sole gammanonin congener capable of inhibiting PDF. Given
the inconsistent correlation we observed between PDF
inhibition and antibacterial activity, it seems likely that
gammanonin resistance can be provided by a PDF-
independent mechanism. We speculate this may be achieved
by nonspecific aldehyde reduction by house-keeping metabolic
enzymes, yielding inactive alcohol congeners such as 1 and 7.
This could also explain the abundance of inactive 1 during
heterologous expression in E. coli. Based on MICs, enzyme
assays, and resistance gene mining, it appears that gammanonin
predominantly affects Gram negative bacteria, suggesting a
degree of specificity not observed with actinonin.48 A more
comprehensive evaluation of gammanonin’s activity spectra
and mechanism is underway in the hopes of providing a new
developmental lead for antibiotic inhibitors of PDF.
