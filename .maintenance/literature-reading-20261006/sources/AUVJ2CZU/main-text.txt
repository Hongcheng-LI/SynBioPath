Genetic Manipulation of an Aminotransferase Family Gene dtlA
Activates Youssoufenes in Marine-Derived Streptomyces
youssoufiensis

ABSTRACT: Disruption of an aminotransferase family gene dtlA activated the production of a novel dimeric benzoic polyene
acids (BPAs), named youssoufene A1 (1), along with four new (2−5, youssoufenes B1−B4) and a known (6) monomeric BPA
in the marine-derived Streptomyces youssoufiensis OUC6819. The structures of 1−5 were elucidated by extensive spectroscopic
and computational approaches. Youssoufene A1 (1) exhibited notably increased growth inhibition (MIC = 12.5 μg/mL) against
multidrug resistant Enterococcus faecalis compared to monomeric structures (2−6).
M
arine microorganisms are exposed to complicated and
challenging environments and, thus, have evolved
eﬀective metabolic systems to adapt and respond to environ-
mental cues and subsequently confer competitive advantages.
Generally, secondary metabolite biosynthetic gene clusters in
Streptomyces are controlled by a complex regulatory network,
responding to various environmental stimuli;1−3 however,
many of them are silent under normal cultivation conditions,
leaving a plethora of cryptic secondary metabolites unex-
plored.4−6
In our eﬀorts to discover novel natural products from marine
Streptomyces strains by a genome mining strategy, pleiotropic
regulatory genes were set as the targets to activate cryptic gene
clusters. Genome scanning of the marine-derived Streptomyces
youssoufiensis OUC6819 revealed the presence of a degT-like
gene dtlA (KY593326), which belongs to the DegT/DnrJ/
EryC1/StrS aminotransferase family. The members of this
family are pyridoxal-phosphate-dependent aminotransferases
with various molecular functions. Expression of the degT gene
from Geobacillus stearothermophilus had impacts on multiple
physiological processes of Bacillus subtilis, including compe-
tency, ﬂagellation, sporulation, autolysin activity, as well as cell
division.7 DegT was proposed to be a transcriptional regulator
based on the presence of a putative DNA binding region,7 but
no further functional characterization has been reported since
then. In this study, the dtlA gene in the S. youssoufiensis
OUC6819 was inactivated, delightedly leading to activation of
youssoufene A1 (1), a unique dimeric benzoic polyene acid
(BPA), along with four new (2−5, youssoufenes B1−B4) and
a known (6) monomeric BPAs (Figure 1). Herein, we report
the activation, isolation, structure elucidation, and biological
evaluation of youssoufenes.
From the genome of S. youssoufiensis OUC6819, a degT-like
gene dtlA was discerned, which exhibits 55.2% similarity/
41.1% identity to DegT from G. stearothermophilus
(WP_033014895.1),7 47.4% similarity/32.5% identity to

DnrJ
from

aminotransferase StrC from Streptomyces glaucescens
(AIR96275.1).9 Further analysis indicated the DtlA homo-
logues are widely distributed in the genomes of many
actinomycete strains (Table S1). Considering the pleiotropic
phenotypes displayed by the degT genetically engineered strain
of B. subtilis, the dtlA gene was inactivated as described in the
experimental procedures. After conﬁrmation by PCR analysis
(Figure S1), the resulting ΔdtlA mutant strain was fermented
in a pilot scale (50 mL) along with the wild-type strain. The
HPLC analysis of their EtOAc extracts revealed that the ΔdtlA
mutant strain accumulated a series of new peaks (Figure 2,

With massive fermentation (20 L) of the ΔdtlA mutant

1 was isolated as a yellow amorphous solid. The molecular

HR-ESIMS data ([M + H]+ at m/z 537.3005, calcd 537.2994).

Compounds 2−6 were obtained as yellow amorphous solids,
the molecular formulas of which were established as C18H18O2
([M + H]+ at m/z 267.1383 and 267.1384, respectively, calcd
267.1385) for compounds 2 and 3 and C20H20O2 ([M + H]+ at
m/z 293.1540, 293.1538 and 293.1541, respectively, calcd
293.1542) for compounds 4−6 based on the HR-ESIMS data.
In the COSY spectrum of 2 (Figure S16), we observed a 1H
spin system consisting of eight oleﬁnic protons H-10−H-17
with a terminal methyl group H-18 (δH 1.76), indicating the
presence of a methylated octyltetraene chain. The COSY
correlations of H-5 (δH 7.73)/H-6 (δH 7.31) and H-7 (δH
7.37)/H-8 (δH 7.23) (Figure S16), together with the HMBC
correlations (Figure S17) from H-5 to C-7 (δC 129.0) and the
quaternary carbon C-9 (δC 136.2), and from H-8 to the
quaternary carbon C-4 (δC 131.8) and C-6 (δC 127.1) revealed
the existence of an 4,9-ortho-substituted benzene ring. The
oleﬁnic proton H-3 (δH 7.63), having a correlation with H-2
(δH 6.40) in COSY (Figure S16), showed HMBC correlations
to C-4, C-5 (δC 126.2), C-9 and the carboxyl carbon C-1 (δC
167.1) (Figure S17), indicating a propylene carboxylic acid
moiety substituted at C-4 of the benzene ring. The HMBC
correlations from H-10 to C-4, C-8 (δC 129.5) and C-9
suggested that the methylated octyltetraene chain was
connected to the benzene ring at C-9 (Figure S17). The
NOE correlations of H-11 (δH 6.51)/H-13 (δH 6.93), H-12
(δH 6.27)/H-14 (δH 5.84) and H-16 (δH 6.61)/H-18 (δH 1.76)
(Figure S18), together with the 3JH,H values of 3J2,3 (15.6 Hz),
3J10,11 (11.4 Hz) and 3J14,15 (11.4 Hz) (Table S3), revealed that
the methylated octyltetraene chain in 2 share the same double
bond geometries with those of 1, which were determined as 2-
E, 10-Z, 12-E, 14-Z, and 16-E, respectively. Thus, compound 2
was reasoned to be the monomeric substructure of 1.

Compound 3, a stereoisomer of 2, showed high similarity of
1H and 13C NMR data with those of 2 (Table S3), except for
the oleﬁnic protons from H-12 (δH 6.31) to H-16 (δH 6.10),
indicative of diﬀerent double bond geometries. The NOE
correlations of H-15 (δH 6.32)/H-17 (δH 5.79) and H-16/H-
18 (δH 1.75) in 3 (Figure S26), together with the 3JH,H values
of 3J2,3 (16.2 Hz), 3J10,11 (11.4 Hz), 3J12,13 (15.0 Hz), and 3J14,15
(15.0 Hz) (Table S3) determined the geometry of each double
bond in 3 as 2-E, 10-Z, 12-E, 14-E, and 16-E, respectively.
Compounds 4−6 were stereoisomers. By comparison of the
NMR data of compounds 4−6 with those of 2 and 3 (Tables
S3 and S4), we conﬁrmed that compounds 4−6 also contain
an ortho-substituted benzene ring, which was substituted by a
methylated octyltetraene chain at C-11. However, in
compounds 4−6, the propylene carboxylic acid at C-4 of 2
and 3 was replaced by a pentadiene carboxylic acid moiety,
which was determined by a diene 1H spin system of H-2−H-5
in COSY (Figures S32, S40, and S47), together with the
HMBC correlations from H-3 to C-2 and C-1 and from H-5 to
C-6 and C-11 in each compound (Figures S33, S41, and S48).
On the basis of the 1H and 13C chemical shift values (Tables
S4 and S5) together with the NOESY data assignment (Figures
S34, S42, and S49), the double bond geometries of the
methylated octyltetraene chains were determined to be 12-Z,
14-E, 16-Z, and 18-E for compounds 4 and 6 and 12-Z, 14-E,
16-E, and 18-E for compound 5. The double bond geometries
of the pentadiene carboxylic acid in 4 were determined as 2-Z
and 4-E by combination of the NOEs of H-3/H-5 (Figure
S34) as well as the 3JH,H values of 3J2,3 (11.5 Hz) and 3J4,5 (15.5
Hz) (Table S4), while in compounds 5 and 6 they were
determined to be 2-E and 4-E based on the
3JH,H values
(Tables S4 and S5). Thus, compound 6 was determined to be
the reported serpentene,10 and compounds 4 and 5 are 2-Z
and 16-E stereoisomers of serpentene, respectively.
Compound 6 has been reported to be derived from a fatty
acid and/or polyketide biosynthetic pathway based on feeding
experiments.11 Inspired by the structural similarity of
compounds 2−6 to the N-terminal cinnamoyl lipid moieties
of skyllamycins12 (Figure S51), we reasoned the youssoufene
biosynthetic gene cluster might harbor genes homologous to
the ketosynthase genes sky17−19 and 22 (Figure S52, Table
S6), which are involved in the cinnamonyl moieties formation
during skyllamycins biosynthesis.12 Therefore, they were used
to search against the genome of S. youssoufiensis OUC6819,
hitting a single polyketide synthase gene cluster (ysf).
Comparison of this cluster to those for skyllamycins12 as
well as pepticinnamins13 (Figure S51) revealed the presence of
the putative biosynthetic genes for cinnamoyl lipid (Figure
S52, Table S5), indicating ysf could be involved in the
biosynthesis of youssoufenes. To probe the function of this
cluster, pWLI813 harboring ysfA-K under the control of the
constitutive promoter PharB was constructed and introduced
into Streptomyces coelicolor M1146 as described in the
experimental procedures. The resulting heterologous expres-
sion strain M1146/pWLI813 successfully produced com-
pounds 1−6 (Figure 2, panel v), demonstrating the ysf gene
cluster is responsible for youssoufene biosynthesis. Hence, we
proposed that youssoufenes might be assembled via a pathway
similar to that of the cinnamonyl moiety in skyllamycin.12 As
outlined in Scheme 1, compounds 2−6 are proposed to be
assembled from condensation of acetyl-CoA and malonyl-CoA
to form a postulated C18-polyene 7 or a postulated C20-
polyene 8, followed by isomerization and aromatic cyclization
to respectively give compounds 2/3 or 4/5/6. Further
dimerization of 2 through an unknown mechanism generates
the unique carbon-bridged dearomatic dimeric BPA, youssou-
fene A1 (1).
In the antibacterial assay against multidrug resistant (MDR)
strains (Table S7), youssoufene A1 (1) showed notably
increased inhibitory activity (MIC = 12.5 μg/mL) against
Enterococcus faecalis CCARM 5172 compared to those of the
monomeric structures (2−6). Compounds 2 and 4 exhibited
weak inhibition with MIC value of 50 μg/mL, while
compounds 3 and 5 were inactive.
In summary, inactivation of a putative aminotransferase
family gene dtlA activated production of a novel carbon-
bridged dimeric BPA (1), along with four new (2−5) and a
known (6) serpentene-type monomeric BPAs in S. youssou-
fiensis OUC6819. The biosynthetic mechanism of youssou-
fenes as well as the underlining mechanism of activation are
worthy of further study in depth. Given their wide distribution,
the DtlA homologues might serve as eﬀective targets for
activation of cryptic gene clusters in actinomycete strains.
