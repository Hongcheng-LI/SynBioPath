inflammatory Activity from an Arctic-Derived Fungus

ABSTRACT: With the advancement of bioinformatics, the integration of genome
mining with efficient separation technology enables the discovery of a greater
number of novel bioactive compounds. The deletion of the key gene responsible for
triterpene cyclase biosynthesis in the polar strain Eutypella sp. D-1 instigated
metabolic shunting, resulting in the activation of dormant genes and the subsequent
production of detectable, new compounds. Fifteen sesquiterpenes were isolated from
the mutant strain, with eight being new compounds. The structural elucidation of
these compounds was obtained through a combination of HRESIMS, NMR
spectroscopy, and ECD calculations, revealing six distinct skeleton types. Compound
7 possessed a unique skeleton of 5/10 macrocyclic ether structure. Based on the
gene functions and newly acquired secondary metabolites, the metabolic shunting
pathway in the mutant strain was inferred. Compounds 6, 8, 11, 14, and 15
exhibited anti-inflammatory effects without cytotoxicity through the release of nitric
oxide from lipopolysaccharide-stimulated RAW264.7 cells. Notably, acorane-type sesquiterpene 8 inhibited nitric oxide production
and modulated the MAPK and NLRP3/caspase-1 signaling pathways. Compound 8 also alleviated the CuSO4-induced systemic
neurological inflammation symptoms in a transgenic fluorescent zebrafish model.
T
he polar regions have produced unique microbes due to
the harsh environmental conditions characterized by low
temperatures, limited water availability, frequent freeze−thaw
cycles, and strong winds. These extremophilic microorganisms
have shown the capacity to generate substantial quantities of
unique secondary metabolites with diverse biological activ-
ities.1−3 Eutypella sp. D-1, a fungus isolated from the Arctic
region, falls under the taxonomic classification Ascomycota,
Xylariales, Diatrypaceae, and Eutypella. Our previous research
has identified various bioactivity secondary metabolites from
Eutypella sp. D-1, with terpenes being the predominant
compounds.4−6 The diterpene libertellenone H exhibited
varying degrees of cytotoxic activity against SW-1990, HeLa,
and U251 tumor cells;7,8 the sesquiterpene eut-guaiane
exhibited significant antibacterial activity against Escherichia
coli, Bacillus subtilis, and Staphylococcus aureus.9 Subsequently, a
series of culturing strategies were employed on Eutypella sp. D-
1, leading to the identification of numerous bioactive
compounds, although the vast majority of the isolated
compounds were pimarane-type diterpenes.5,10−12 To fully
explore the potential of Eutypella sp. D-1, research on the
biosynthesis gene clusters (BGCs) responsible for these
secondary metabolites was initiated. The advancements in
gene sequencing and bioinformatics have enabled extensive
analysis and annotation of genomic information from various
strains;13,14 however, a significant number of BGCs remain
uncharacterized.15 The ongoing advancements in molecular
biology technologies, such as gene knockout, have significantly
broadened the avenues of research into secondary metabolites.
For example, by disrupting crucial genes involved in the
biosynthesis of rubratoxins, a primary product derived from a
Penicillium strain, various biosynthetic gene clusters can more
effectively vie for shared synthetic precursors, and novel
compounds with unique structures have been successfully
discovered.16
Inflammation is a broad response of the body to various
external stimuli, including injury, infection, and irritation.17,18
This complex process entails the involvement of blood vessels
and cells, triggering the release of inflammatory mediators that
activate different regulatory pathways.19,20 Uncontrolled
inflammation plays a significant role in the pathogenesis of
chronic inflammatory diseases. Presently, the commonly used
anti-inflammatory drugs are still insufficient and are associated
with obvious adverse effects.21,22 The exploration of novel anti-
inflammatory medications with minimal toxicity continues to
be a prominent area of research.

Herein, this article established the relationship between the
secondary metabolites and biosynthetic gene clusters of
Eutypella sp. D-1, and a gene related to triterpene cyclase
biosynthesis was identified. The sesquiterpene biosynthetic
gene clusters were successfully activated by gene deleting, and
multiple skeleton types of sesquiterpenes were obtained. The
anti-inflammatory activity of compounds was evaluated
through in vitro and in vivo experiments, revealing that certain
sesquiterpenes demonstrate significant anti-inflammatory effi-
cacy.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Analysis of Secondary Metabolite BGCs and Triter-
pene Cyclase Gene. The secondary metabolite BGCs of
Eutypella sp. D-1 were analyzed using the online antiSMASH
tool. A total of 22 scaffolds were identified, specifically
associated with terpenes, polyketides (T1PKS), and non-
ribosomal peptides (NRPS) biosynthesis pathways (Table S1).
A secondary metabolite BGC related to terpene biosynthesis
was identified. Within this BGC, a gene (gene B) showed 100%
similarity to the gene (occ) associated with clavaric acid
biosynthesis reported in the literature, indicating a high
homology between gene B and occ (Figures 1a and S1).23 It
was discovered that these two genes may share similar or
identical functions in the secondary metabolite biosynthesis
process. A subsequent in-depth investigation revealed that occ
encodes an oxidosqualene cyclase (OSC) from the triterpene
cyclase family, which plays a role in converting linear
2,3:22,23-dioxidosqualene into the annular precursor clavar-
inone (Figure S2).
As annotated by antiSMASH, gene B is situated within a
BGC delineated by five genes, encompassing a total length of
2491 bp, which includes an intron sequence of 297 bp. This
gene is implicated in terpene biosynthesis and is classified as a
core gene (Figure 1a). The genes flanking gene B are gene A, C,
and D, respectively, lacking annotation, suggesting the absence
of identified homologous sequences for these genes. Hence, it
is hypothesized that these genes may be inactive or not
involved in altering the structure of the parental nuclear
skeleton. The function of gene E is that of cytochrome P450,
which is not a key gene in skeleton biosynthesis. As a
consequence of the significant homology between gene B and
occ, it is inferred that gene B is also implicated in the cyclization
of triterpenes, thereby impacting the biosynthesis of triterpenes
in Eutypella sp. D-1. One of the nontargeted strategies in
biosynthesis is metabolic shunting, a method that involves
activating BGCs to redirect precursor molecules toward the
production of alternative compounds by disrupting the core
genes responsible for the synthesis of the secondary
metabolites. In previous studies, triterpenes such as ergosterol
were discovered from Eutypella sp. D-1. We arranged to use
metabolic shunting strategy to knock out the key gene (gene B)
in the biosynthesis of triterpene and activate the BGCs through
the metabolic shunting of precursors, to obtain additional
secondary metabolites with diverse structures.
Analysis of Secondary Metabolites for Wild and
Mutant Strains. The gene B deletion strain named Δterp of
Eutypella sp. D-1 was constructed by PEG-mediated
homologous recombination.24 Molecular biology screening
and validation of the transformants were conducted (Figure
S3). There was no gene B fragment in the transformant,
indicating that gene B has been successfully deleted. The
colony morphology of Δterp was observed (Figure 1b); in
comparison to the wild-type strain, the mycelium of the Δterp
mutant exhibited a transition from a fluffy to a moist and
viscous texture, accompanied by a change in color from milky
white to a distinct yellowing. The wild-type and Δterp strains
underwent small-scale fermentation (2 L) under identical

Identification of Secondary Metabolites for Δterp.
The Δterp mutant was subjected to large-scale fermentation
(20 L), and the products were isolated and identified to
determine specific changes of the secondary metabolites.

identified compounds (1−8) as illustrated in Figure 2. The
isolated sesquiterpenes encompassed six skeleton types:
bisabolane-type (1, 2, 13−15), bergamotane-type (3, 4),
acorane-type (8), eudemane-type (9−12), and two unclassified
skeleton types (5−7). Compound 7 possessed a unique
skeleton of 5/10 macrocyclic ether structure.

Proposed Metabolic Shunting Pathway of Δterp. In
previous studies, a wide range of sesquiterpene skeleton types
was not identified in the wild-type strain of Eutypella sp. D-1.
In this study, diverse sesquiterpenes were identified, indicating
that the metabolic disruption resulting from the deletion of
gene B may primarily influence the biosynthesis pathway of
sesquiterpenes. Based on the biosynthesis pathway of
triterpenes, it was speculated that there might be a metabolic
shunting pathway for sesquiterpenes in the Δterp strain (Figure
S4). First, two farnesyl pyrophosphates (FPP) will condense to
form the triterpene precursor squalene under the catalysis of
squalene synthase (SS); subsequently, 2,3-oxidosqualene will
be produced by squalene epoxidase (SE). In the typical
biosynthetic pathway, 2,3-oxidosqualene undergoes cyclization
to produce a variety of triterpene skeletons catalyzed by
various oxidosqualene cyclases. Upon knocking out gene B, the
oxidosqualene cyclases will not be produced, consequently
blocking the cyclization pathway of 2,3-oxidosqualene and
resulting in a significant buildup of 2,3-oxidosqualene.
According to the feedback mechanism, the activity of squalene
synthase or oxidosqualene cyclases will decrease when 2,3-
oxidosqualene accumulates excessively. On the contrary, a
reduction in enzyme activity will result in a notable buildup of
FPP, the precursor of sesquiterpenes. This accumulation may
induce upregulation of sesquiterpene cyclase genes, thereby
promoting the development of various skeleton structures of
sesquiterpenes in the secondary metabolites of Δterp strains.

was measured at 540 nm to detect the content of NO. The formula
for calculating the inhibition rate of NO release is as follows:
Inhibition rate = (NO content in LPS group −NO content in
compounds group)/NO content in LPS group × 100%.
Cytokine Measurement of RAW264.7 Cells by RT-qPCR.
RAW264.7 cells were cultured at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well in
12-well plates. The cells were treated with LPS (0.1 μg/mL) along
with varying concentrations of EA. Following treatment, total RNA
was extracted utilizing an RNA extraction kit, and cDNA synthesis
was carried out with an RNA reverse transcription kit. The cDNA
samples obtained were subsequently analyzed using the SYBR Green
qPCR Master Mix kit and quantified with the QuantStudio 5 Real-
Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The primer
sequences utilized are presented in Table S6.
Western Blot Analysis. Appropriate RIPA buffer containing
protease and phosphatase inhibitors was added to lysate the
RAW264.7 cells and extract the total protein. The protein
concentration in each sample was assessed by employing a protein
BCA assay kit. Protein samples were loaded onto polyacrylamide gels
and separated by electrophoresis using SDS-PAGE. Subsequently, the
proteins were transferred to a PVDF membrane and blocked with 5%
BSA for one hour. Primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4
°C, followed by a 1 h incubation of secondary antibodies at room
temperature after washing with TBST three times for 10 min each.
The membranes were then developed by using the ECL system.
Assay of Inflammatory Cells and Behavior for Zebrafish.
The transgenic zebrafish Tg (zlyz:EGFP) provided by the Engineering
Research Center of Zebrafish Models for Human Diseases and Drug
Screening of Shandong Province was selected with normal develop-
ment. The zebrafish were transferred into a 24-well plate, with 12 fish
per well. The blank control group, copper sulfate model group, and
compounds group were set up, and each concentration group was
equipped with three repeated wells. Copper sulfate was added to all
groups except the blank group after 24 h of culturing, with a
concentration of 40 μM. The migration of inflammatory cells was
observed under a fluorescence microscope after two hours of
culturing, and the number of inflammatory cells migrating to the
lateral line behind the cloaca was calculated. Around 24 zebrafish were
selected from each group and placed in a dedicated 48-well plate for
detection. The plate was placed in a Zebrabox dark box for 10 min to
acclimate the zebrafish to the environment. Subsequently, the
behavioral trajectory of the zebrafish was monitored for 20 min in a
tranquil setting, with the total distance swum and swimming duration
recorded using Zebralab.
Cytokine Measurement of Zebrafish by RT-qPCR. The
zebrafish tissues were collected and homogenized to extract RNA,
followed by cDNA synthesis carried out with an RNA reverse
transcription kit. Quantification was performed using the Real-Time
PCR System (Roche, USA). The primer sequences utilized are
presented in Table S7.
Statistical Analysis. Data were presented as mean ± standard
error of measurement (SEM). For comparisons between groups, one-
way ANOVA followed by an LSD-Fisher test was employed. Pairwise
comparisons within groups were analyzed by using the t test.
Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software
(GraphPad Software, USA). A significance level of p value of <0.05
was considered statistically significant.
