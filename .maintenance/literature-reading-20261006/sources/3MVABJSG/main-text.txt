ABSTRACT: Macrolactins (MLNs) are a class of important antimacular degeneration
and antitumor agents. Malonylated/succinylated MLNs are even more important due to
their eﬃcacy in overcoming multi-drug-resistant bacteria. However, which enzyme
catalyzes this reaction remains enigmatic. Herein, we deciphered a β-lactamase
homologue BmmI to be responsible for this step. BmmI could speciﬁcally attach C3−
C5 alkyl acid thioesters onto 7-OH of MLN A and also exhibits substrate promiscuity
toward acyl acceptors with diﬀerent scaﬀolds. The crystal structure of BmmI covalently
linked to the succinyl group and systematic mutagenesis highlighted the role of oxyanion
holelike geometry in the recognition of carboxyl-terminated acyl donors. The
engineering of this geometry expanded its substrate scope, with the R166A/G/Q
variants recognizing up to C12 alkyl acid thioester. The structure of BmmI with acyl
acceptor MLN A revealed the importance of Arg292 in the recognition of macrolide
substrates. Moreover, the mechanism of the BmmI-catalyzed acyltransfer reaction was
established, unmasking the deft role of Lys76 in governing acyl donors as well as catalysis. Our studies uncover the delicate
mechanism underlying the substrate selectivity of acyltransferases, which would guide rational enzyme engineering for drug
development.
■INTRODUCTION
Acylation is an important and widespread strategy for the
functional control of biomolecules.1 Acetylation is a well-
known post-translational modiﬁcation of proteins, while
malonylation and succinylation, harboring a terminal carboxyl
group, have been recognized only recently.2,3 Notably, for
small-molecule natural products, aliphatic acylation normally
occurs as malonylation in plants and in many cases on sugar
moieties.4,5 In contrast, microbial natural products are rarely
modiﬁed as such.
7-O-Malonyl macrolactin A (MMA, 2a) and 7-O-succinyl
macrolactin A (SMA, 3a), commonly produced by Bacillus
strains6 bear acyl moieties with a terminal carboxyl group at 7-
OH (Figure 1). Both MMA and SMA display stronger
inhibition multi-drug-resistant bacteria than against macro-
lactin A (MLN A, 1a). Of note, SMA is much more potent
than teicoplanin, which is used for the treatment of
vancomycin-resistant enterococcal infections.7,8 MLN A and
SMA are currently being evaluated in preclinical studies as
antimacular degeneration and antitumor agents.9 The back-
bone of MLNs is assembled via trans-acyltransferase (trans-
AT) PKSs to give MLN A,10 followed by glycosylation to
generate MLN B (1b) or acylation to aﬀord MMA and SMA
(Figure S1). Previously, we demonstrated that bmmGT1, a
gene outside of the MLN gene cluster in Suaeda salsa-
associated Bacillus methylotrophicus B-9987, is responsible for
the glycosylation step during the biosynthesis of MLNs and
bacillaenes (BAEs, polyunsaturated enamines).11,12 However,
the malonylation/succinylation step remains enigmatic.
Until now, the characterized tailoring acyltransferases for
natural products are mostly from plants and belong to the

However, the underlying molecular mechanisms for the

Herein, we reported that bmmI, a putative class C family β-
lactamase (AmpC) gene, is responsible for the acylation
tailoring step during MLN biosynthesis, notably favoring C3
and C4 alkyl acid thioesters. To parse out the molecular basis
of the substrate preference, we determined the crystal
structures of apo-BmmI and BmmI in complex with diﬀerent
substrates, disclosing unusual oxyanion holelike geometry to
act as the acyl donor gatekeeper. The rational engineering of
this geometry dramatically broadened the substrate scope of
BmmI. These results provide key insights into the acyl donor
selectivity determinants of acyltransferases, highlighting the
great potentials of the oxyanion holelike geometries in rational
enzymatic engineering.
■RESULTS
Inactivation and Overexpression of bmmI Demon-
strating Its Role as an Acyltransferase. The MLN gene
clusters, conservatively present among B. methylotrophicus and
B. velezensis,18 consist of nine genes, mln A−I.10 To decipher
the acylation step during MLNs biosynthesis, we ﬁrst analyzed
the ORFs within the MLN gene cluster in marine-derived B.
methylotrophicus B-9987 but found no typical acyltransferase
gene. BmmI (no. AIJ04688.1, Figure S1), a homologue of Mln
I from B. velezensis FZB42 (no. CAJ57404.1), attracted our
attention as it was predicted to belong to the β-lactamase/
transpeptidase-like superfamily (IPR012338), which is a
principal member of the β-lactam-recognizing enzymes
(BLRE) family.19 The BLRE family also has some expansions
including family VIII carboxylesterases, glutaminases, and
LACTB in higher metazoans. With limited sequence identities
(<27%), BmmI harbors the conserved SXXK motif (I) and
tyrosine of the YXN motif (II) (Figure S2), constituting the
putative catalytic triad Ser-Lys-Tyr, in which Tyr is commonly
believed to be deprotonated by Lys and then to function as the
general base to active the nucleophile Ser.20,21 Noticeably, the
third residue of motif II in BmmI is arginine instead of
asparagine (in AmpCs and DD-peptidases) or absent (in
family VIII carboxylesterases and LovD) (Figure S2),
distinguishing BmmI from these enzymes. Motif III is not
well conserved, and it is His319-Leu320-Gly321 in BmmI (Figure
S2). Further phylogenetic analysis revealed that BmmI is
evolutionarily distinct from other functionally characterized
BLRE family enzymes (Figure S3).
To investigate the biological function of bmmI in vivo, we
constructed gene inactivation strain ΔbmmI (Figure S4) as
well as gene overexpression strain B-9987/pWLI212. HPLC
monitoring of their fermentation broths showed that ΔbmmI
completely lost the ability to produce acylated MLNs, SMA
(2a), and MMA (3a), and the yield of glycosylated compound
MLN B (1b) was simultaneously improved by about 2-fold
(Figure 2a, panel (ii)) in comparison to those of the wild-type
strain (Figure 2a, panel (i)). Consistently, overexpression of
bmmI led to the increased production of 2a and 3a and
signiﬁcantly reduced the production of 1b (Figure 2a, panel
(iii)). These results suggested that bmmI is involved in the
malonylation/succinylation step during the biosynthesis of
MLNs.
Probing the Substrate Promiscuity of BmmI In Vitro.
To elucidate the exact biochemical function of BmmI, we set
out to reconstitute the proposed acyltransfer reaction in vitro.
BmmI was produced in Escherichia coli Rossetta (DE3) and
puriﬁed to near homogeneity (>95%) (Figure S5a). As shown
in Figure 2b, BmmI respectively transferred the malonyl group
and succinyl group onto 1a, generating 2a (panel (ii)) and 3a
(panel (iii)) as expected. To determine if BmmI also
recognizes the acyl group with a longer chain or void of the
terminal carboxyl group, glutaryl-CoA, acetyl-CoA, and
butyryl-CoA were further tested. Surprisingly, BmmI was
capable of recognizing all of them, albeit at much declined
catalytic eﬃciencies, resulting in corresponding acylated MLN
compounds (Figure 2b, panels iv−vi, Figures S6c, S7, and S8).
In combination with the in vivo data, BmmI is unequivocally
demonstrated to be an O-acyltransferase during the biosyn-
thesis of MLNs, with considerable ﬂexibility toward acyl
donors.
The acyl acceptor ﬂexibility of BmmI was further probed.
Antibiotics with diﬀerent scaﬀolds, including ﬁlipin III,
glycosylated piericidin A and chloramphenicol, were subjected
to biochemical assays. Delightedly, BmmI was able to transfer
malonyl group onto ﬁlipin III, which was conﬁrmed by HR-
ESI-MS (Figure S9a); glycosylated piericidin A and chlor-
amphenicol could also be eﬃciently recognized by BmmI
(Figures S9b and 9c). These results indicated that BmmI has
considerable promiscuity toward acyl acceptors.

Crystal Structure of BmmI with an Acyl Donor
Disclosed an Unusual Oxyanion Holelike Gatekeeper.
To understand the structural elements that determine the acyl-
CoA speciﬁcity, we sought to elucidate the crystal structures of
BmmI in complex with succinyl-CoA. When the wild-type
BmmI was incubated with 5 mM succinyl-CoA for 16 h and
then was subjected to crystallization, the crystals were obtained
and diﬀracted to 2.11 Å resolution (Table S3), which belong to
space group C2 and contain four monomers in an asymmetric
unit (PDB ID 6KJT). We observed positive electron density
connected to the Ser73 side chain in the active sites of the four
monomers, which are best interpreted as succinyl groups
covalently attached to this residue (Figure S14). The presence
of the covalent succinyl adduct to Ser73 was further conﬁrmed
by LC-MS/MS analysis of the obtained crystals dissolved in
water, which gave mass shifts consistent with a covalently
attached succinyl group (100 amu) (Figure S15b), consistent
with Ser73 being a nucleophile for acylation. As shown in
Figure 4a, the newly formed covalent bond between the
carbonyl group of succinyl and the Ser73 hydroxyl oxygen
atom in this high-energy transition state was stabilized by the
conventional oxyanion hole composed of the Met322 main-
chain amide and the catalytic Ser73 side-chain hydroxyl.
Noticeably, at the carboxyl end of the succinyl group, we
observed structurally similar oxyanion holelike geometry, in
which two hydrogen bond donors, the ε-amino group of Lys76
and the Nη1 of Arg166, hydrogen bond with the carboxyl
group of the succinyl adduct (Figure 4a). This hydrogen
bonding network suggests that this unique oxyanion holelike
geometry is most likely involved in carboxyl-terminated acyl
donor recognition.
To experimentally investigate the predicted function of the
oxyanion holelike geometry, we substituted Lys76 and Arg166
with amino acids with diﬀerent properties (K76A/R/D,
R166A/K/D), followed by tests of catalytic activities toward
malonyl-CoA, succinyl-CoA, and acetyl-CoA. As indicated in
Figure 4b, the substitution of Lys76 with alanine led to the
complete abolishment of malonylation and succinylation
abilities but interestingly enhanced the acetylation ability by
∼2.5-fold in comparison to that of wild-type BmmI. Variants
K76R and K76D completely lost their acyltransfer activities
(Figure 4b), which might be due to impaired deprotonation of
Tyr164 (K76D) or a steric clash obstructing the entrance of
the acyl donors (K76R). When mutating Arg166, the variants
exhibited decreased (R166A, R166 K) or abolished (R166D)
malonylation and succinylation ability, but all of the variants
showed a large increase in acetylation activity by ∼25−75-fold
(Figure 4b). The kinetic parameters were further characterized
for K76A and R166A toward acetyl-CoA. As indicated in Table
1, the kcat/Km values increased by ∼3-fold (K76A) and ∼15-
fold (R166A). The above results demonstrated that this
unusual oxyanion holelike geometry does play a role in
recognizing carboxyl-terminated acyl donors.

Furthermore, we looked into the surrounding residues of
Arg166 in the BmmI structures and found that the Nη2 of
Arg166 is anchored by Glu128 (Figures 3c and 4a). Through
further inspection of the transition-state structure (Figure
S14), we noticed that the distances from the Nη2 or Nε of
Arg166 to the carboxyl group of Glu128 and from the Nη1 of
Arg166 to the Ser73-succinyl are in a dynamic equilibrium,
indicating a certain degree of ﬂexibility of this oxyanion
holelike geometry. Therefore, we respectively replaced Glu128
with alanine and glutamine to loosen its interaction with
Arg166. As expected, the catalytic activities of both variants
toward malonyl-CoA, succinyl-CoA, and glutaryl-CoA were
enhanced by ∼1.5−2-fold in comparison to those of the wild-
type enzyme (Figure 5b). In addition, they gained the ability to
recognize hexanedioyl-CoA (Figure 5b).
Crystal Structure of BmmI with MLN A Reﬂects the
Molecular Basis for Acyltransfer Reactions. To further
unravel the molecular basis of the acyltransfer reaction, we
determined the crystal structure of BmmI in complex with acyl
acceptor MLN A. We constructed double mutant S73A/K76A
and incubated it with 2 mM MLN A for 16 h before
crystallization. The crystal structure was solved at 2.35 Å
resolution and belongs to space group P4212 (PDB ID 6KJQ,
Table S3). We observed substantial positive electron density of
MLN A in catalytic cavity. However, the electron density is
clear for the polar groups that directly interact with the protein
residues but is weak for some part of the hydrocarbon chain in
MLN A, which might be due to the ﬂexibility of the macro ring
(Figure S18). As shown in Figure 6a, the 7-OH of MLN A is
located near residue 73, indicating that this hydroxyl group
could initiate a nucleophilic attack on the covalent bond of the
Ser73-acyl intermediate during the deacylation step of the
reaction. Lys76 and His319 are located near the hydroxyl
group of Tyr164. We substituted His319 with alanine to
experimentally investigate the role of this residue in catalysis.
As shown in Figure 3d, the activities of H319A decreased 90%
toward all of the acyl donors tested, indicating that His319
plays some role in the catalysis, likely involved in the

■DISCUSSION
In this study, through genetic and biochemical studies, we
demonstrated that bmmI is responsible for transferring
carboxyl-terminated acyl groups onto 7-OH of MLN A,
remarkably displaying substrate promiscuity toward both acyl
donors and acyl acceptors. Of note, crystal structures and
systematic mutagenesis established that the oxyanion holelike
geometry composed of Lys76 and Arg166 in BmmI is an
essential structural motif for acyl donor discrimination, causing
the enzyme to prefer alkyl acid thioesters rather than common
alkyl thioesters. Accordingly, the rational engineering of this
geometry greatly expanded the acyl donor substrate scope of
BmmI, making it a potentially eﬃcient tool enzyme for
compound structural diversiﬁcation.
Oxyanion holes are usually evolved to act as a stabilizer for
negatively charged oxygens in a high-energy substrate−enzyme
transition state, which are composed of hydrogen bonds from
two or more backbone amides.24,25 As for BmmI, in addition to
the conventional oxyanion hole, another structurally similar
oxyanion holelike geometry composed of Lys76 and Arg166
side chains is adopted to guard the acyl donors (Figure 4a).
The K76A and R166D variants of BmmI completely switched
from malonyltransferase/succinyltransferase to acetyltransfer-
ase (Figure 4b), demonstrating their role in stabilizing the
terminal carboxyl group of the acyl donors. The fact that
BmmI accepts C3−C5 alkyl acid thioesters (Figure 2b)
indicates that this geometry has a certain ﬂexibility. This is
well supported by the dynamic distances between (Ser73-
succinyl)-Arg166-Glu128 observed in the transition-state
structure (Figure S14).
As the development of protein engineering and computer-
aided design, the oxyanion hole has been an important
structural propertity for enzyme engineering and design.26,27
Notably in BmmI, the ﬂexibility of the oxyanion holelike
geometry enabled further engineering to broad its substrate
spectrum. Herein, on one hand, we adopted a “coarse tuning”
strategy to engineer the geometry-constituting Arg166 into a
diﬀerent residue (lysine, alanine, glycine, and glutamine).
While the wild-type BmmI could accommodate only C3−C5
alkyl acid donors, variants R166A/G/Q are capable of
recognizing up to C12 alkyl acid thioesters (Figure 5b). The
catalytic activity of R166G toward glutaryl-CoA is greatly
improved (Figure 5b), which could be ascribed to the reduced
steric hindrance in R166G. In the case of the wild-type BmmI,
the chain lengths of the acyl donors might be mainly deﬁned
and limited by the electrostatic interactions between their
terminal carboxyl groups and the guanidinium moiety of
Arg166. Substitution of Arg166 with glycine would greatly
increase the pocket volume, enabling the accommodation of
longer-chain alkyl acid thioesters, with C5−C6 substrates
taking the optimal spatial positions in the binding pocket of
R166G. On the other hand, we employed a “ﬁne tuning”
method to modify the geometry by replacing Arg166-
anchoring residue Glu128. Variants E128A and E128Q
displayed obviously improved malonylation and succinylation
activities and earned a hexanedioylation ability (Figure 5b).
We propose, free from interaction with Glu128, the Arg166-
based oxyanion holelike geometry becomes more ﬂexible. Our
results unambiguously demonstrate that the engineering of this
geometry and its related residue(s) serves as an eﬀective
strategy to broaden the acyl donor promiscuity and improve
the catalytic eﬃciencies of the enzyme.
Interestingly, Lys76, which is a conserved deprotonation
agent for tyrosine among β-lactamase/transpeptidase-like
superfamily proteins, serves as an important character of the
oxyanion holelike geometry. Variant K76A turned into an
acetyltransferase (Figure 4b), clearly demonstrating the role of
the far-end oxyanion holelike geometry in recognizing
carboxyl-terminated acyl donors. Lys76 would also act as the
classical deprotonation agent for Tyr164, as the ε-amino
nitrogen of Lys76 hydrogen bonds with the phenolic hydroxyl
oxygen of Tyr164 in both the wild-type (Figure 3c) and
transition-state structures (Figure 4a). This raised the question

Acyltransfer is of crucial importance for the modiﬁcation of
many secondary metabolites, altering their lipophilicity,
volatility, chemical stability, and biological activity.13 For
example, the 3-O-succinyl-28-O-benzyl oleanolate derivative
presents greater cytotoxicity and apoptosis eﬀects than its
natural precursor, oleanolic acid, or its benzyl derivative.29
SMA and MMA have superior antibacterial activities compared
to that of MLN A.7,8 Our study showed that BmmI displays
broad substrate promiscuity toward not only acyl donors
(Figure 5b) but also acyl acceptors (Figure S9). The crystal
structure of BmmI in complex with MLN A brought to light
Arg292 to ﬁx the acyl acceptor via hydrogen bonding with the
keto oxygen of the ester bond in MLN A. Engineering Arg292
into alanine successfully improved the catalytic activities
toward MLN A and macrolide compound ﬁlipin III as well
(Figure 6b,c), which might be due to the decrease in steric
hindrance during catalysis. Therefore, the crystal structures of
BmmI in complex with substrates would undoubtedly guide
further enzyme engineering to exploit BmmI as a valuable
biocatalyst for compound structural diversiﬁcation.
■CONCLUSIONS
Oxyanion holes are usually evolved to act as a stabilizer for a
high-energy substrate−enzyme transition state. Herein, we
disclosed that malonyltransferase/succinyltransferase BmmI
has evolved an oxyanion holelike geometry as an acyl donor
gatekeeper. Of note, the rational engineering of this geometry
dramatically broadened the substrate scope of BmmI. Our
results contribute to understanding the trick that nature has
developed to facilitate transferring carboxyl-terminated acyl
groups. Given that acylation is of crucial importance for the
pharmaceutical properties of many bioactive natural products,
BmmI could be exploited as a valuable biocatalyst in expanding
the chemical space of compounds with medicinal potentials.
