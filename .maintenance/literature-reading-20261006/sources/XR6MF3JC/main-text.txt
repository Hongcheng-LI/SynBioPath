ABSTRACT: Enfumafungin-type antibiotics, represented by enfumafungin and fuscoatroside, belong to a distinct group of
triterpenoids derived from fungi. These compounds exhibit significant antifungal properties with ibrexafungerp, a semisynthetic
derivative of enfumafungin, recently gaining FDA’s approval as the first oral antifungal drug for treating invasive vulvar candidiasis.
Enfumafungin-type antibiotics possess a cleaved E-ring with an oxidized carboxyl group and a reduced methyl group at the break site,
suggesting unprecedented C−C bond cleavage chemistry involved in their biosynthesis. Here, we show that a 4-gene (fsoA, fsoD,
fsoE, fsoF) biosynthetic gene cluster is sufficient to yield fuscoatroside by heterologous expression in Aspergillus oryzae. Notably, FsoA
is an unheard-of terpene cyclase-glycosyltransferase fusion enzyme, affording a triterpene glycoside product that relies on enzymatic
fusion. FsoE is a P450 enzyme that catalyzes successive oxidation reactions at C19 to facilitate a C−C bond cleavage, producing an
oxidized carboxyl group and a reduced methyl group that have never been observed in known P450 enzymes. Our study thus sets the
important foundation for the manufacture of enfumafungin-type antibiotics using biosynthetic approaches.
■INTRODUCTION
Enfumafungin-type antibiotics represented by fuscoatroside
(1),1 enfumafungin,2 kolokoside A,3 and WF116054 (Figure
1A) are a unique group of fungi-derived triterpenoids. They
are characterized by an E-ring-cleaved fernane triterpene
skeleton equipped with a C3β-D-glucopyranose and a C2α-
hydroxyl group (Figure 1A). Enfumafungin-type antibiotics
display an outstanding antifungal activity by inhibiting β-(1,3)-
glucan synthase (FKS) critical for fungal membrane synthesis.5
Accordingly, a semisynthetic analogue of enfumafungin,
ibrexafungerp (BREXAFEMME, Scynexis, lnc.) (Figure 1A),
was approved in 2021 by FDA for the clinical treatment of
invasive vulvar candidiasis (caused by Candida albicans), which
affects approximately 75% of women at least once in their
lifetime.6 Ibrexafungerp is an orally active antifungal drug and
represents a new class of antifungals approved in the past 20
years.7 Compared with current first-line antifungal agents such
as echinocandins, ibrexafungerp stands out due to its oral
availability and strong activity against echinocandin-resistant
strains.8
Ibrexafungerp is a semisynthetic product from the natural
product enfumafungin,9−11 which possesses an unusually
cleaved E-ring, likely resulting from an unprecedented C−C
bond cleavage reaction. Accordingly, the biosynthesis of
enfumafungin-type antibiotics has attracted intensive attention.
Recently, Kuhnert et al.12 discovered a potential biosynthetic
gene cluster of enfumafungin from Hormonema carpetanum
ATCC 74360 (Figure S1) and observed an interesting fusion
enzyme comprised of a terpene cyclase (TC) domain and a
glycosyltransferase (GT) domain in the gene cluster by

have been reported for biosynthesis of fungal secondary
metabolites,13 which generally fall into two groups depending
on the enzymatic fusion pattern (Figure 1B). The first is the
fusion of core enzymes, such as prenyltransferase-terpene
cyclase fusion enzymes (e.g., AcOS) involved in the biosyn-

tailoring enzyme GT, which represent a new class of fusion
enzymes. However, the functionality of the TC-GT fusion

been addressed.

Baeyer−Villiger (BV) monooxygenase (VidF)21 (Figure 1C).
Nevertheless, the above oxidative cleavages of the C−C bond
generally involve the successive oxidation of both carbon
atoms at the break site, which is in sharp contrast to the
generation of an oxidized carboxyl group and a methyl group
observed in enfumafungin-type antibiotics (Figure 1C).
Kuhnert et al. proposed that the cleavage of E-ring was
initiated by a cytochrome P450-mediated BV oxidation
reaction, followed by ring-opening hydrolysis, dehydration,
and reduction12 (Figure S2). However, since none of the
putative intermediates were found in nature and no
corresponding genes were found in the biosynthetic gene
cluster, it is highly possible that an alternative C−C bond
cleavage mechanism exists.
In this work, we unambiguously resolved the function of the
TC-GT fusion enzyme and characterized the C−C bond
cleavage of the E-ring occurring in an unprecedented manner,
wherein a singular P450 enzyme orchestrates the entire
cleavage process to generate a pair of residues comprising a
carboxyl group and a methyl group and finally completely
dissected the biosynthetic pathway of the enfumafungin-type
antibiotic fuscoatroside. Our finding thus sets the stage for the
manufacture of enfumafungin-type antibiotics using biosyn-
thetic approaches.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Identification of the Fuscoatroside Biosynthetic Gene
Cluster and Heterologous Reconstitution of Its Biosyn-
thesis. Due to the unavailability of the enfumafungin-
producing strain H. carpetanum ATCC 74360, we turned to
focus on the biosynthesis of fuscoatroside, an analogue of
enfumafungin. We obtained the fuscoatroside-producing strain
Humicola fuscoatra NRRL 22980 from the Agricultural
Research Service Culture Collection (NRRL)1 and confirmed
its production of fuscoatroside (1) via high-performance liquid
chromatography-mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS) analysis and
NMR analysis after purification (Figure S3). We then
sequenced the whole genome of H. fuscoatra NRRL 22980
and identified a potential biosynthetic gene cluster for
fuscoatroside with the guidance of efu gene cluster of
enfumafungin12 (Figures 2A and S4). This gene cluster
(designated as fso cluster; Genbank accession number:
OR962264) consists of six genes, encoding one TC-GT
chimeric enzyme (fsoA), three cytochrome P450 monoox-
ygenases (fsoB/D/E), one glucose oxidase (fsoC), and one
acetyltransferase (fsoF). Among them, fsoA, fsoD, and fsoE
encoded proteins with high sequence similarity (50−56%
identity) to those encoded by efuA, efuH, and efuG in the efu
gene cluster for enfumafungin biosynthesis, suggesting that
these three genes might be involved in the generation of the
core structure of the enfumafungin-type antibiotics (Figure
S5).
To clarify the link between the fso cluster and fuscoatroside
biosynthesis and investigate the role of each gene, we first
introduced the fso cluster into A. oryzae NSAR1 (AO) strain,
which has been employed to elucidate the biosynthetic
pathways of several fungi-derived triterpenoids.22−25 The
transformant strain AO-fsoABCDEF was cultured in an
induction medium for 5 days, and the mycelia extract was
analyzed by HPLC−MS. Surprisingly, in contrast to the
control strain AO, the AO-fsoABCDEF strain generated the
final metabolite 1 (Figure 2B), suggesting that the fso cluster is
fully capable of forming 1, including the glycosylated fernane
skeleton and the unusually cleaved E-ring. Based on the
predicted functions, we hypothesized that the TC-GT chimeric
enzyme FsoA might account for C3β-D-glucopyranose, while
the oxidases FsoB/D/E/C could be responsible for the C2α-
hydroxylation and E-ring cleavage.
Since fsoB and fsoC have no homologous genes in the efu
cluster, they might not be involved in the biosynthesis of
enfumafungin-type antibiotics (Figure S5). We therefore
constructed a four-gene-harboring transformant AO-fsoADEF,
and the HPLC−MS results showed that the transformant was
sufficient to produce 1 (Figure 2B), confirming that fsoB and
fsoC are not engaged in the biosynthesis of fuscoatroside.
To further elucidate the roles of FsoA/D/E/F in the
biosynthesis of fuscoatroside, we first introduced the chimeric
gene fsoA into AO to generate the transformant AO-fsoA.
HPLC-MS analysis showed that AO-fsoA afforded two
additional products 2 and 3 compared with AO (Figure 2C).
After purification and NMR analysis, 2 was determined to be
isomotiol, harboring a fernane triterpene skeleton with a C8−
C9 double bond (Figure 2D).26 3 could not be obtained by
isolation from AO-fsoA due to the limited amount. HPLC-MS
analysis revealed that 3 is 162 Da larger than 2, indicating that
3 is a glycosylated product of 2. The structure of 3 was further
confirmed by the chemical synthesis of 3-O-glycosylated
product of 2 via the Schmidt glycosylation method (Figure
S6).27 The above results clearly indicate that the TC-GT
chimeric enzyme FsoA is responsible for forming triterpene
glycoside 3, wherein the TC domain generates triterpene
aglycone (2) and the GT domain mediates C3 glycosylation.
This finding aligns with the previous research indicating that
the heterologous expression of FsoA’ TC domain in AO yields
aglycone 2.26 The limited efficiency in generating glycosylated
product 3 also implies that 2 might not be a preferred substrate
for the GT domain.
Of the remaining three enzymes, it is likely that the two
P450 monooxygenases FsoD/E exert their activity subsequent
to FsoA. We then individually introduced fsoD and fsoE into
AO-fsoA to construct AO-fsoAD and AO-fsoAE, respectively.
HPLC-MS analysis revealed that AO-fsoAD generated a major
product 5 along with a minor product 4, which exhibited a
mass increase of 16 Da compared to compounds 3 and 2,
respectively (Figure 2C). This observation strongly suggests
that compounds 4 and 5 are monohydroxylated derivatives.
Compound 5 was isolated, and its structure was determined as
the C2α-hydroxylated product of compound 2 (Figure 2D),
while compound 4 was identified as the C2α-hydroxylated
form of compound 3 by comparison with the deacetylated
product of compound 12 obtained in a subsequent study
(Figures 2D and S7). These findings proved compelling
evidence for FsoD’s ability to catalyze hydroxylation at
position C2. Compound 5 is likely generated by FosD-
mediated hydroxylation of 2, which was confirmed by
introducing fsoD into the previously established AO-fsoA(TC)
strain,26 resulting in the production of compound 5 (Figure
S8A). Compound 4 could be generated either through FosD-
mediated hydroxylation of compound 3 or glycosylation of
compound 5 by the GT domain of FosA. However, due to the
presence of a bulky steric hindrance caused by the 3-O-glycan
in compound 3, it may render FsoD incapable of accepting it
as a substrate. On the other hand, the transformant AO-fsoAE
yielded two compounds 6 and 7. Following purification and

Subsequently, an A. oryzae strain expressing three-gene
fsoADE was constructed, which produced four additional
compounds 8−11 (Figure 2C). Compound 8 was identified as
the E-ring cleaved form of 4 through comparison with the
deacetylated compound 1 (Figures 2D and S9). NMR analyses
revealed that compounds 9−11 corresponded to the C19β-
hydroxyl, C19-carbonyl, and E-ring cleavage derivatives of
triterpene aglycone 5, respectively (Figure 2D). These findings
further validate the role of FsoE in catalyzing the oxidative C−
C bond cleavage by successive oxidation at position C19.
Compound 8 should be generated by the FsoE-mediated
oxidative cleavage of compound 4, while compounds 9−11
were likely formed by the action of FsoE on aglycon 5. To
validate this, fsoE was introduced into AO-fsoA(TC)D, resulting
in the production of compounds 9−11 (Figure S8B). Despite
these observations, the abundance of aglycon 5 in AO-fsoADE
suggests that its conversion to compounds 9−11 is not highly
efficient. On the other hand, the introduction of fsoF to AO-
fsoAD resulted in the production of acetylated glycoside 12
(Figure 2C,D). Though we did not detect the acetylated
alycone 13 in AO-fsoADF, the introduction of fsoF into AO-
fsoA(TC)D did generate a new peak with m/z = 485 [M + H]+
that was identical to compound 13 (Figure S10). These
findings indicate that FsoF catalyzes the acetylation of C2α−
OH following the action of FsoD. Interestingly, compared to
AO-fsoAE and AO-fsoADE, which retain a significant amount of
aglycone products 2 and 5 (Figure 2C), the introduction of
fsoF appears to contribute to the efficient glycosylation of
aglycone 13 to form 12 (AO-fsoADF), which subsequently
undergo oxidative C−C cleavage, yielding the final product 1
under the action of FosE (AO-fsoADEF) (Figure 2D).
Based on the above findings, we have proposed a
biosynthetic pathway for fuscoatroside (Figure 2D). This
pathway is initiated by the cyclization of 2,3(S)-oxidosqualene
through FsoA’s TC domain, leading to the formation of the
fernane skeleton 2. Subsequently, C2α-hydroxylation mediated
by FsoD results in the production of 5, which is further
acetylated by FsoF to give 13. The GT domain of FsoA may
convert 2, 5, and 13 into their corresponding glycosides 3, 4,
and 12, which then undergo oxidative cleavage under the
action of FsoE to form 6, 8, and 1, respectively. Although
hydroxylation followed by acetylation of 3 and 6 by FosD and
FosF could not be ruled out, this process is likely to occur with
difficulty due to bulky steric hindrance caused by the presence
of a glycan at C3 in these compounds. Interestingly, FsoE can
also utilize aglycones 2 and 5 as substrates to generate
compounds 7 and 9−11 despite their low efficiency.

fusion pattern by combining a skeleton-forming TC domain
with a tailoring GT domain. In order to investigate the
function of TC-GT fusion in FsoA, we separated FsoA into
FsoA(TC) and FsoA(GT) based on the sequence alignment of
TC-GT fusion enzymes (Figure S11) and expressed them
individually or together in AO. Expression of fsoA(TC) alone
yielded isomotiol 2, but further introduction of fsoA(GT) into
the fsoA(TC) expressing strain failed to give the expected
glycosylation product 3 (Figure 3A), suggesting that the
removal of the TC domain from GT hinders its ability to
continue glycosylation. To rule out the possibility of an

triterpene skeleton. Unlike most fusion enzymes, where the
fused domains can function independently, it is essential for
the GT domain to be fused with the TC domain in FsoA to
achieve its full functionality. It is noteworthy that although the
GT domain must operate in conjunction with the TC domain,
it may not only act subsequent to the TC domain but also
alongside postmodifying enzymes, indicating a potential broad
substrate promiscuity of the GT domain.
To the best of our knowledge, there have been no reports on
the inclusion of a glycosyltransferase as a constituent of a
fusion enzyme.28 Phylogenetic analysis revealed that FsoA(GT)
and EfuA(GT) fall within the clade of fungal sterol GTs
represented by UGT51, UGT51B1, and UGT51C129,30
(Figure S13). Sequence alignment indicated that FsoA(GT)
and EfuA(GT) exhibited high sequence similarity to the C-
terminal regions of fungal sterol GTs, but they lacked extensive
N-terminal regions. Previous studies have shown that the
removal of the N-terminal regions did not significantly affect
glucosyltransferase activity,30 implying limited importance for
the N-terminal segments, which could be readily degraded
during evolution (Figure S14). Therefore, it is plausible to
propose that the GT domain of TC-GT fusion enzymes may
have originated from fungal sterol GTs, where truncation
occurred at their N-terminus before they were fused to the TC
domains rather than evolving independently. Genome mining
of TC-GT chimeric genes revealed their widespread
distribution in fungal genomes, often accompanied by
homologues of FsoD, FsoE, and FsoF (Figure S15). This
suggests a conserved four-enzyme mediated formation of the
enfumafungin-type skeletons in fungi, providing insights into
the exploration of other enfumafungin-type antibiotics.
Unveiling the Unique C−C Bond Cleavage Mediated
by FsoE. C−C bond cleavage catalyzed by P450s typically
involves the oxidation of both carbon atoms at the breaking
site; however, in the biosynthesis of fuscoatroside, the FsoE-
mediated E-ring cleavage generates an oxidized carboxyl group
and a reduced methyl group, suggesting a novel mechanism for
the C−C bond cleavage. To gain further insights into the
process, we constructed the fsoE expression strain AO-fsoE and
conducted feeding experiments using its putative substrates 9
and 10. As a result, the transformant strain AO-fsoE
successfully produced compounds 10 and 11 when supple-
mented with substrate 9 (Figure 4A), suggesting that
compound 9 may first undergo oxidation to form carbonyl
intermediate 10 before undergoing E-ring cleavage to yield
compound 11. Consistently, when compound 10 was fed to
AO-fsoE, the production of compound 11 was observed
(Figure 4A). Nevertheless, the precise mechanism underlying
the C−C cleavage from carbonyl intermediate 10 to 11
remains unknown. Previously, Kuhnert et al. proposed a BV-
based mechanism that requires multiple enzymes to complete
subsequent ring-opening hydrolysis, dehydration, and reduc-
tion12 (Figure 4B, path c). In this study, we demonstrated that
a single P450 enzyme FsoE is sufficient to catalyze E-ring
cleavage. Importantly, we observed that the stereochemistry at
C21 remained unchanged before and after C−C bond
cleavage, indicating that the hydrogen atom at C21 was not
removed during the process. This differs from the BV-based
cleavage mechanism. To investigate this, isotopic labeling
experiments were conducted using sodium [2-13C, 2-2H3]
acetate.31,32 Initially, the AO transformant, which produces
compound 1, was cultivated in the presence of sodium [2-13C,
2-2H3] acetate. The labeled compound 1 was isolated for NMR
analysis. As depicted in Figures 4C and S16, the 13C NMR
signals at C-13 [δC 38.1], C-17 [δC 40.8], and C-21 [δC 48.7]
were distinctly accompanied by β-deuterium-shifted signals
(between Δδ= −0.07 and −0.09 ppm). This observation
indicates that the deuterium atoms originally attached to C-13,
C-17, and C-21 have undergone migration to C18, C21, and
C22, which is consistent with the cyclization cascade mediated
by FsoA(TC). Moreover, this finding also confirms the retention
of the hydrogen atom at C21 during the E-ring cleavage
process, supporting an alternative C−C bond cleavage
mechanism rather than a BV-mediated mechanism. Based on
these findings, we propose two potential mechanisms for the
C−C bond cleavage by FsoE (path a and path b) (Figure 4B).
Initially, FsoE catalyzes a two-step compound I-mediated
oxidation to generate the C19-gem-diol intermediate c, which
can undergo spontaneous dehydration to form the C19-
carbonyl intermediate d.33 In path a, the nucleophilic ferric-
peroxo [Cys−FeIII−OO−]−directly attacks the C19-carbonyl
group of d to form the tetrahedral peroxohemiacetal
intermediate f. The subsequent homolytic cleavage of the
active O−O bond in ferric-peroxo, accompanied by electron
transfer, results in the C19−C20 bond cleavage and formation
of intermediate g with a C19-carboxyl group and a C20 radical.
In path b, compound I [Cys−FeIV = O]•+ directly abstracts a
hydrogen atom from the C19-gem-diol intermediate c,
accompanied by electron transfer, resulting in the cleavage of
the C19−C20 bond, also forming the C20 radical intermediate
g. In both paths, the formation of the C20 radical intermediate
g occurs and should be quenched by reducing substances (H-
X) in the active site to form the end product h. The specific
nature of H-X is currently unknown, but amino acids
containing water-exchangeable protons such as tyrosine and
cysteine have been proposed as potential reducing substances
in the nonheme iron oxygenase (CarC)34 and catalase-like
heme-containing protein (CnsD).35 However, this process
would require the regeneration of enzymes. Further exper-
imental investigation is needed to understand the exact
mechanism for quenching the radical intermediate in the
enzyme’s active site. Nonetheless, FsoE represents an
unprecedented instance where a single P450 enzyme facilitates
oxidative cleavage of a C−C bond, resulting in the formation of
an oxidized carboxyl group and a reduced methyl group; such a
phenomenon has not been observed previously in P450
chemistry.33,36

P450-mediated cleavage mechanisms, FsoE employs [Cys−
FeIII−OO−]−or [Cys−FeIV = O]•+ to generate a radical
intermediate that is reduced in the active site, producing a
nonoxidized methyl group. Consistent with its unique
function, sequence similarity network (SSN) analyses revealed
that FsoE and its homologues comprised a distinct group
within P450 enzymes (Figure S18).
Search for Catalytic Residues in FsoE. To gain further
insights into the unique function of FsoE, we employed
AlphaFold243 and AlphaFill44 to construct its enzyme model
with heme covalently linked to the conserved residue C517.
Molecular docking analysis using substrate 10 by AutoDock
Vina45 revealed a hydrophobic active pocket surrounded by 8
divergent amino acid residues (R315, F148, F259, F337,
W339, F557, N344, Y143) (Figure 4D). Based on these
docking results, site-directed mutagenesis was conducted on
these residues to generate FsoE variants. Subsequently, feeding
experiments were performed to investigate their catalytic
functions (Figure 4E). The enzymatic activity of FsoE was
completely abolished by N344A and R315A mutations,
suggesting that R315 and N344 may play crucial roles in
binding to the substrate through hydrogen bonding with the
C3 hydroxyl and C19 keto groups, respectively. Furthermore,
the catalytic activities of nonpolar residues W339 and F337
mutations were also completely abolished. This suggests that
these active sites might be recruited to bind to the substrate
through a hydrophobic interaction. Interestingly, mutations of
the four residues close to the E-ring (F557A, F259A, F148A,
and Y143A) still produced the C19-carbonyl product 10.
Notably, among these mutants, F557A exhibited an even more
efficient conversion of 9 into 10 compared to wild type.
However, all four mutants lost their ability to produce the E-
ring cleaved product, suggesting that F557, F259, F148, and
Y143 played a crucial role in facilitating the oxidative C−C
bond cleavage, leading to the formation of 11. Moreover, we
substituted Y143 with F, a residue possessing a similar side
chain but lacking a phenolic hydroxyl group, resulting in the
exclusive production of 10 rather than 11. This observation
confirms the significant contribution made by the OH group
present on Y143 toward the C−C bond cleavage. These
findings significantly contribute toward our understanding of
FsoE and provide valuable insight for its development and
utilization in the C−C bond cleavage.
Structure−Activity Analysis of Enfumafungin-Type
Derivatives. During our study, we successfully isolated
fuscoatroside (1) and 13 other analogues, including 11 newly
discovered compounds. These compounds were subjected to
antifungal activity testing against C. albicans FIM709 and
Aspergillus niger R330 by the 2-fold dilution method. The
results indicated that compounds 1 and 8 exhibited significant
inhibitory effects on C. albicans FIM709 and A. niger R330
(Table S4). Further analysis of the structure−activity relation-
ship revealed that the cleaved E-ring was essential for the
antifungal activity, as observed in the comparison between
compounds 1 and 12, as well as between compounds 8 and 4,
which aligns with previous findings.46 Moreover, compound 8
demonstrated higher potency than compound 11, suggesting
that C3β-D-glucopyranose plays a critical role in its activity.
Therefore, both the E-ring cleavage enzyme FsoE and chimeric
enzyme FsoA hold the potential for the transformation of
biologically active enfumafungin-type antibiotics.
■CONCLUSIONS
In conclusion, we successfully elucidated the complete
biosynthetic pathway of the first enfumafungin-type antibiotic,
fuscoatroside. Through biosynthetic reconstitution in A.
oryzae, we have discovered 11 novel derivatives, allowing us
to explore their structure−activity relationships in depth.
Additionally, our findings have revealed an extraordinary
enzymatic fusion mechanism and a distinctive C−C bond
cleavage process that fills a significant gap in our understanding
of enfumafungin-type antibiotics’ biosynthesis.
