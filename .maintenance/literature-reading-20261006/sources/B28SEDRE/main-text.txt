Serine Hydrolase-Catalyzed Polyol Lipids are Necessary for Rodlet
Layer Formation on the Cell Wall of Entomopathogenic Fungi
Dekun Kong,# Qian He,# Dong-Mei Lin, Huaran Zhang, Lin Chen, Yanhua Fan,* Man-Cheng Tang,*
and Yi Zou*

ABSTRACT: Some key secondary metabolism genes are important for driving the infection process of entomopathogenic fungi;
however, their chemical substance basis has not been well investigated. Here, mixtures of polyol lipids are discovered, which are
synthesized through iterative chain transfer−esterification−hydrolysis cycles catalyzed by serine hydrolase during the release of
online highly reducing polyketide intermediates. Importantly, an in vivo gene knockout experiment revealed that the synthesis of
polyol lipids is necessary for rodlet layer formation on the cell wall of Beauveria bassiana. Our work uncovers an unexpected way for
the synthesis of polyol lipids and illuminates a new perspective on their part in significant physiological processes in
entomopathogenic fungi.
E
ntomopathogenic fungi (EPF) are a unique and
specialized subgroup of fungi. They can infect and kill
more than 200 species of different insects that are important
agricultural pests or act as vectors for human and animal
diseases.1,2 Therefore, compared to synthetic chemical
pesticides that can cause environmental impact, toxic effects
to nontarget organisms including humans, and insecticidal
resistance, the EPF such as Beauveria bassiana and Metarhizium
anisopliae have been successfully developed and used globally
as ecofriendly biological control agents for pest management.2,3
Taking B. bassiana as an example, the conidia of B. bassiana
first attach to the surface of the insect cuticle. After
germination, the hyphae directly penetrate the cuticle and
rapidly proliferate as hyphal bodies in the hemolymph,
eventually killing the host.4 B. bassiana then utilizes the
cadaver as a food source and produces more infective conidia
to start the next infection cycle. Throughout the entire
infection cycle, B. bassiana produces many structurally diverse
metabolites (Figure 1),5−8 which play important chemical
weapon roles in different infection processes, such as aiding
invasions, defending against insect immunity, and fighting
other fungi or bacteria to survive in the environment.9,10
However, whether there are compounds used to support the
stability, integrality, and stress resistance of conidia during the
attachment to the insect cuticle, such as those necessary for
rodlet layer formation on the cell wall of B. bassiana conidia,
needs to be answered.
With this in mind, a highly reducing polyketide synthase
(hrPKS) cluster (PKS15 cluster, Figure 2A) in B. bassiana
ARSEF 2860 caught our attention. Previous biological
experiments mainly clarified that (1) the expression of pks15
is constitutive;11,12 (2) the germination of conidia of Δpks15
mutants is decreased and the cell wall of Δpks15 mutants is
relatively smoother;13 (3) the Δpks15 mutant has a reduced
ability to cope with phagocytosis in insect hosts.13 These
results imply that the PKS15 cluster has a significant impact on
various aspects of B. bassiana, it may contribute to growth and
development, participate in conidia gemination and play an
important role in virulence formation.11−13 However, as the
essence of the B. bassiana holding the PKS15 cluster is unclear,
it is necessary to uncover the chemical substance basis of it.

We reanalyzed the gene function of the PKS15 cluster
(named the bas cluster here) and found the following (1) This
cluster is widely distributed in entomopathogenic fungi (Figure
2A), and in addition to B. bassiana, it has been found in
Akanthomyces lecanii, Cordyceps militaris, Hirsutella minneso-
tensis, and Tolypocladium capitatum. (2) The neighboring
enzyme of hrPKS (NCBI accession number XP_008594329.1,
named
BasA
here)
is
a
serine
hydrolase
(SH,
XP_008594330.1, BasB, Figure 2A). It contains an intact
“Ser-His-Asp” catalytic triad of the α/β-hydrolase fold enzymes
(Figure S2)14 and was previously annotated as a calcium-
binding domain protein.11 (3) basA and basB are highly
conserved in homologous clusters, indicating that they
constitute the minimal cassette to produce core compound(s)
in bas and similar clusters.
The intron-free basA and basB were transferred into the
yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae BJ5464-NpgA (BJ) under the
control of the ADH2 promoter.15 As shown in Figure 2B,
compared to BJ control, (1) BJ-basA did not produce any
compounds (Figure 2B, traces i) and ii) and (2) when basB
was co-transferred with basA, compounds 1−5 were produced
by BJ-basAB (Figure 2B, trace iii). These results show that
BasB serves as a key protein to collaborate with BasA.
Subsequent fermentation, purification, and structural deter-
mination by HR-MS and NMR analyses (Tables S7−S11 for
NMR data, Figures S3−S7 for HR-MS spectra, and Figures
S17−S51 for NMR spectra of 1−5) confirmed that
compounds 1−5 are structural derivatives of 3,5-dihydroxyl-
decanoic acid (6),16 where 1 is a lactone of 6 and 2−5 are the
dimeric, trimeric, tetrameric, and pentameric polyol lipids of 6,

5/OH-3, H-4β/OH-3, and H-5/H-2β, as well as H-4α/H-2α
and H-3/H-2α, indicate that the configurations of C3 and C5

consistent with (+)-(3R,5R)-3-hydroxyl-5-decanolide (Table
S14).18 Therefore, 1 is (+)-(3R,5R)-3-hydroxyl-5-decanolide,
and the absolute C3 and C5 configurations of 2−5 are assigned
to be 3R,5R. Based on these results, 3 is exophilin A,19 4 is
exophilin B1,20 2 is the demethyl ester derivative of
aureosurfactin,21 and 5 is a new compound that we have
named beauverilin.
Determining the structures of 1−5 (1) establishes the core
chemical substance basis for the PKS15 cluster in B. bassiana,
where polyol lipids are the main products; (2) indicates that
BasB acts as a trans-acting thioesterase (TE) domain for the
release of BasA-bound 3,5-dihydroxyl-decanoic precursor. As
proposed in Figure 3, BasB may catalyze multiple rounds of
chain transfer, esterification, and hydrolysis to form 2−5, and
the formation of 1 may depend on it catalyzing chain transfer
and lactonization of BasB-tethered 6. Thus, BasB differs
significantly from the previously identified trans-acting TE
domains of fungal hrPKS clusters, which typically have a single
function of hydrolysis, lactonization, or transesterification;22−26
the action of BasB represents a new release way for fungal
highly reducing polyketides.
To solidify the way in which BasA and BasB collaborate in
the synthesis of 1−5, (1) we expressed and purified BasA,
BasB, and its S131A variant (S131 in the conserved GXSXG
motif of SH enzymes is involved in chain transfer;14 see Figure
S10 for all protein gel images); (2) we prepared 6 via alkaline
hydrolysis of 1 (see Table S12 for NMR data and Figures
S52−S58 for NMR spectra of 6); (3) we synthesized the N-
acetylcysteine thioester 6-SNAC from 6 as a starting material
(Figure 2C, Table S13 for NMR data, and Figures S59−S64
for NMR spectra of 6-SNAC),27 which is the substrate mimic
of BasA-bound 6. Several biochemical assays were performed:
(1) when BasA was incubated with acetyl-CoA, malonyl-CoA,
and cofactor NADPH, no products were observed (Figure 2D,
traces i) and ii); (2) in BasA and BasB coupled assays, 1−5
were detected (Figure 2D, trace iii); (3) in BasA and BasB-
S131A coupled assays, production of 1−5 was abolished
(Figure 2D, trace iv); (4) when BasB was incubated with 6 or
6-SNAC, 1−5 were not formed (Figure 2D, traces v and vi).
These results confirm that the generation of 1−5 is an online
assembly process and their formation depends on the
cooperation of BasA and BasB. This cooperative mode is
commonly found in many hrPKS clusters containing trans-
acting enzymes.24,25 However, BasB is unusual; it catalyzes the
chain transfer from BasA to BasB forming BasB-tethered 6 and
determines the fate of this monomer via different chemical
conversions (Figure 3). For instance, (1) BasB directly
catalyzes the lactonization to yield 1; (2) for the generation
of 2−5, BasB first catalyzes the esterification between BasB-
tethered 6 and BasA-bound 6, and then catalyzes chain transfer
and hydrolysis to form 2. Repeating this catalytic cycle results
in the formation of 3, 4, and 5. Notably, BasB is a strictly
regioselective enzyme, as it utilizes C5-OH (rather than C3-
OH) to perform nucleophilic attack, thereby forming the ester
bonds of 1−5.

we constructed the ΔbasA mutant (Figure S11) and observed
the possible metabolic and phenotypic changes in B. bassiana

conidia. Surprisingly, 1−5 were not detected in the extracts of
B. bassiana wild-type (WT) conidia; however, when the cell
wall isolates of B. bassiana WT conidia were treated by alkaline
hydrolysis,28 6 could be observed (Figure S12). These results
suggest that the polyol lipids may be linked to the cell wall or
further modified in unknown ways in the conidia stage of B.
bassiana, making them difficult to detect directly. It may
resemble sporopollenin in plants;29 however, the actual
mechanism still needs to be investigated in the future. In
addition, the ΔbasA mutant exhibits enhanced hydrophilicity
and forms small aggregates that rapidly settled in aqueous
solutions (Figure S13), indicating that the cell wall surface
structure of the conidia may be altered in the ΔbasA mutant.
There is a rodlet layer on the cell wall of B. bassiana conidia,
which plays important roles in conidial hydrophobicity, thus
affecting fungal adhesion toward insect hosts and viru-
lence.30,31 Deletion of hydrophobin genes such as hyd1 and
hyd2 severely affects the formation of the rodlet layer, and the
conidia exhibit a bald phenotype.32 To probe the effect of basA
deletion on conidia, the cell wall surface morphology of the
ΔbasA mutant was observed by scanning electron microscopy
(SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). As
shown in Figure 4A, SEM analysis revealed that the ΔbasA
mutant displayed bald conidia, without rodlet bundles on the
cell wall.13 In addition, TEM imaging showed that the cell
envelope of the ΔbasA mutant exhibited a fluffy appearance
due to the exposure of hydrophilic components such as β-
glucan in the cell wall (Figure 4B). Notably, the phenotype of
the ΔbasA mutant is consistent with that of strains lacking
hydrophobins.30,33 However, qRT-PCR analysis revealed that
the two hydrophobin genes hyd1 and hyd232 in the ΔbasA
mutant exhibited similar expression levels to those found in B.
bassiana WT (Figure S14). Thus, these results confirm that the
elimination of polyol lipid synthesis in the ΔbasA mutant
hinders the formation of the rodlet layer and leads to a bald
phenotype. Considering that the expression of hydrophobin
genes was not affected in the ΔbasA mutant, we hypothesize
that the synthesis of polyol lipids may be involved in the
assembly of hydrophobins.
Topical inoculation and intrahemocoel injection of Galleria
mellonella larvae were used to test the fungal infection ability.
Topical inoculation is like what happens in nature. Intra-
hemocoel injection can bypass cuticle adhesion and
penetration; thus it is often combined with topical inoculation
to evaluate fungal changes that occur on the insect cuticle.34
After topical inoculation, the mortality rate for the ΔbasA
mutant decreased by nearly 40% at 8 days compared to that of
B. bassiana WT (Figure 4C). The calculated mean lethal time
(LT50) values were as follows (in days): WT, 5.3 ± 0.1; ΔbasA,
6.7 ± 0.4 (Figure S15). However, when the conidia were
injected into the G. mellonella hemocoel cavity, no significant
difference in mortality rate was observed between the B.
bassiana WT and ΔbasA mutant (Figure 4D), showing similar
LT50 values (in days): WT, 2.7 ± 0.1; ΔbasA, 2.8 ± 0.2 (Figure
S15). These results confirm that the reduced fungal virulence
of the ΔbasA mutant comes from the fact that the process of
conidial attachment to the insect cuticle is affected. Therefore,
the loss of polyol lipid formation ability of the ΔbasA mutant
greatly affects the formation of the rodlet layer, thereby
reducing the ability of conidia to attach to the surface of insect
cuticle and ultimately lowering its insecticidal virulence (Figure
S16).
In summary, we clarified the core chemical substance basis
of the PKS15 cluster in B. bassiana ARSEF 2860, which mainly
is mixtures of polyol lipids. Their synthesis greatly depends on
the unique function of serine hydrolase in catalyzing iterative
chain transfer−esterification−hydrolysis cycles during the
release process of online highly reducing polyketide
intermediates. In addition, an in vivo gene knockout experi-
ment reveals that the synthesis of polyol lipids is necessary for
rodlet layer formation on the cell wall of B. bassiana. Our work
not only uncovers an unexpected way for the synthesis of
polyol lipid family natural products but also illuminates a new
perspective on these compounds taking part in significant
physiological processes in entomopathogenic fungi.
