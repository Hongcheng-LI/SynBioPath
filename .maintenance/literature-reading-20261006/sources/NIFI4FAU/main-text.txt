ABSTRACT: Single-module nonribosomal peptide synthetases
(NRPSs) have nonclassical domain compositions and play versatile
roles in the biosynthesis of natural products. AstC, a prototypical
single-module NRPS-like D-alanyltransferase with the architecture
of adenylation-thiolation-thioesterase (A-T-TE) tridomain, was
identified as the catalyst in the D-alanylation process during the
post-polyketide synthase (PKS) modifications of ansatrienin
biosynthesis. In this study, the function of the TE domain of
AstC was elucidated in intermolecular esterification, and its
substrate promiscuity was revealed for both acyl donors and
polyketide acceptors. Through genome mining, a newly charac-
terized AstC homolog was identified, SmAstC, and it was shown
that SmAstC exhibited the highest catalytic activity for
D-
alanylation of N-desmethyl-4,5-desepoxymaytansinol (DDM), a
precursor of the antitumor agent ansamitocins, obtained from Actinosynnema pretiosum HGF052. Harnessing the broad substrate
selectivity of the TE domains and the spontaneous hydrolysis propensity of the D-alanylated products, the post-PKS modification of
ansamitocin biosynthesis was reprogrammed through introducing the gene encoding SmAstC into A. pretiosum HGF052. The
ansamitocin biosynthetic pathway in this engineered strain was switched toward the production of maytansinol that was a critical
intermediate of maytansine-derived antibody-drug conjugates. The SmAstC-catalyzed D-alanylation of DDM interrupted the
stringent post-PKS modification steps within the original biosynthesis of ansamitocins, which produced 3-O-D-alanyl maytansinol
that readily underwent spontaneous hydrolysis to afford maytansinol. The innovative application of the single-module NRPS-like D-
alanyltransferases combined with rational strain engineering has surmounted the longtime hurdle in converting DDM to maytansinol
in vivo, enabling the biomanufacturing of maytansinol without precedent.

■INTRODUCTION
Nonribosomal peptide synthetases (NRPSs) serve as multi-
modular assembly lines that catalyze the biosynthesis of diverse
peptide natural products with potent biological activities.1 As a
subset of the NRPS superfamily, single-module NRPS-like
enzymes lack the typical multimodule architectures and chain
elongation capabilities, but they possess unique biological
functions due to their diverse domain compositions. Essentially,
adenylation (A) and thiolation (T) domains are commonplace
in single-module NRPS-like enzymes, while alternative domains,
such as condensation (C),2 reductase (R),3 thioesterase (TE),4
epimerase (E),5 and oxidase (Ox),6 further diversify their
domain repertoires. Among these, A-T-TE tridomain NRPS-like
enzymes have garnered recognition for their involvement in the
formation of quinones7,8 or lactones9−11 through the dimeriza-
tion of α-keto carboxylic acids, exemplified by AtrA,12 TdiA,13
and ApvA14 in fungi as well as EchA in Streptomyces.15 In
particular, AstC, a unique A-T-TE tridomain NRPS-like enzyme
discovered in Streptomyces sp. XZQH13,16 stands apart from its
counterparts with analogous domain compositions (Figure
1A).17,18
Our previous study revealed that AstC is an O-D-
alanyltransferase, orchestrating the specific loading of a D-alanyl
group onto the 11-OH in ansatrienins.19 This unique
functionality hinted at the extraordinary functional diversity
inherent in its structural domains, particularly in terms of its
selectivity for D-alanine and D-alanylation toward the polyketide.

In the functions of typical NRPSs, the incorporation of a D-
configured amino acid rarely occurred through the direct
activation by the A domains but relied on epimerization
mediated by the E domains. Additionally, while the catalysis of
intermolecular esterification by the TE domains was observed in
the post-NRPS modifications of depsipeptides, such an
occurrence was not documented in post-polyketide synthase
(PKS) modifications catalyzed by NRPS-like enzymes com-
posed of A-T-TE.20,21 Therefore, these unique properties of D-
alanyltransferases inspired our further research endeavors.

post-PKS modifications greatly elevate bioactive potential but
incur distinctive challenges in the synthesis of target
compounds. An illustration of such biosynthetic complexity

shunt metabolite of ansamitocin biosynthesis in Actinosynnema
pretiosum. Maytansinol served as the direct precursor for
maytansine-derived antibody-drug conjugates and a useful
probe for studying microtubule dynamics.22−24 The current
process for generating maytansinol predominantly relied on
reductive hydrolysis to remove the 3-O-acyl groups of
ansamitocins, which underwent harsh conditions and resulted
in the inevitable formation of side products.25 The 3-O-acyl
moieties in ansamitocins were incorporated by the 3-O-
acyltransferase, namely Asm19. Asm19 catalyzed promiscuous
3-O-acylations of the biosynthetic precursor N-desmethyl-4,5-
desepoxymaytansinol (DDM).26 The sequential nature of 3-O-
acylation occurring prior to the 4,5-epoxidation and amide N-
methylation rendered direct biosynthesis of maytansinol
unattainable despite the exhaustive efforts of manipulating
ansamitocin biosynthesis over the past two decades.27
During examining the activity of the single-module NRPS-like
D-alanyltransferase AstC in vitro, the
D-alanylated product
displayed a tendency to revert to the substrate state via
spontaneous hydrolysis. The stable presence of the D-alanyl
group within ansatrienins may be facilitated by the subsequent
N-cyclohexanecarboxyl modification.19 This fortuitous discov-
ery led us to contemplate the potential of harnessing the
distinctive catalytic property of D-alanyltransferases for the
biosynthesis of maytansinol. The reprogrammed pathway for
maytansinol was based on the biosynthesis of ansamitocins in A.
pretiosum, which differed from maytansinol only in the 3-O-acyl
esters that were challenging to remove chemically. Instead of the
enzyme Asm19 responsible for 3-O-acylations, the newly
characterized NRPS-like
D-alanyltransferase SmAstC was
employed in this pathway, substituting a D-alanyl moiety for
the acyl ester on the same hydroxyl group. With the D-alanylated
intermediate, the following epoxidation and N-methylation
steps catalyzed by Asm11 and Asm10, respectively, still took
place. The hydrolytic lability of the 3-O-D-alanyl ester ultimately
led to the formation of the free alcohol maytansinol.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Intermolecular Esterification Catalyzed by the TE
Domain of the D-Alanyltransferase AstC. Previously, the
role of AstC was revealed in transferring D-alanine to the 11-OH
of ansatrienins. However, the precise functionalities of its
domains remain elusive, particularly the role of the TE domain
in catalyzing intermolecular esterification. Herein, the TE
domain of AstC (AstC TE) was purified, and its activity was
examined by utilizing D-alanyl N-acetylcysteamine thioester (D-
alanyl SNAC) as an acyl donor (the primers, plasmids, and
strains used in this study are listed in Tables S1, S2, and S3,
respectively).28 The incubation of AstC TE with D-alanyl SNAC
and trienomycinol (1) successfully resulted in the formation of
11-O-D-alanyl trienomycinol (2), consistent with the result of
the enzymatic assay performed with full-length AstC (Figure
1B). However, the D-alanyl product 2 readily reverted to 1
through spontaneous hydrolysis (Figure S1).
The phylogenetic analysis highlighted AstC TE as a distinctive
catalyst for the intermolecular esterification between the
aminoacyl donor and the polyketide backbone (Figure 1C).
Two other TEs (Sln9 TE and FrsA TE), positioned within the
same clade as AstC, were derived from comparable single-
module NRPSs featuring the C-A-T-TE architecture that was
responsible for the intermolecular esterification of modified
amino acids and depsipeptides.20,21 The sequence alignment of
AstC TE with representative type I TEs revealed a conserved
GXSXG motif but diverged in the composition of catalytic
triads. While the canonical Ser-Asp-His triad was typically
conserved among the TE domains of NRPSs, AstC TE featured
a catalytic triad consisting of Ser-Asn-His. This departure from
the conventional acidic residue aligned with similar enzyme
profiles found within partial single-module NRPS-like enzymes
characterized by the A-T-TE domain composition (Figure
S2).29,30 To assess the impact of this substitution on catalytic
activity, the N117 residue of AstC TE was mutated. The N117D
mutant of AstC TE exhibited a 5-fold decrease in activity, while
the N117A mutant completely abolished the activity (Figure
1D). This distinctive structural feature observed in AstC TE
would distinguish it from other TEs and facilitate the
identification of related homologous enzymes.
Substrate Scope of AstC TE Domain. Given that AstC TE
was demonstrated to independently catalyze the intermolecular
esterification between the acyl donor and acceptor, we
embarked on exploring the substrate scope of AstC TE with
diverse acyl SNACs and ansamycins with distinct backbones.
Previously, the attachment of several
D-amino acids was
achieved with full-length AstC, but the attachment of L-amino
acids failed.19 Herein, several
L-aminoacyl SNACs were
chemically synthesized and utilized as substrates for the
enzymatic assays of the standalone AstC TE. The loading of L-
alanyl, L-methionyl, L-leucyl, and L-phenylalanyl moieties that
were previously unutilized by the full-length protein was
accomplished by the standalone AstC TE (Figure 2A). Further,
the attachment of N-substituted amino acids, e.g., N-methyl-L-
alanyl moiety, was carried out through utilizing the correspond-
ing SNAC derivatives. Despite the challenges in loading fatty
acyl moieties, such as butyryl, AstC TE showcased limited
catalytic activity toward acetyl SNAC as detected through ESI-
HRMS analysis (Figure S3). These results demonstrated that
AstC TE did exhibit broad compatibility with diverse acyl
donors and suggested that the stringent preference of the full-
length AstC for D-amino acids with small side chains would be
attributed to its A domain.
On the basis of suboptimal acyl donors provided in the SNAC
form, the full-length AstC was subsequently utilized to screen
the compatibility toward a range of ansamycins as acyl acceptors.
The detection of D-alanylated products by ESI-HRMS analysis
demonstrated that AstC was able to D-alanylize two distinct
types of pentaketide ansamycins and two octaketide ansamycins,
exhibiting high promiscuity toward acyl acceptors (Figures 2B
and S5). In particular, regarding that DDM (3) was a 4,5-
desepoxyl intermediate in the biosynthesis of ansamitocins,
modest catalytic activity was observed for the formation of 3-O-
D-alanyl DDM (4) from 3, but no activity was detected toward
maytansinol (5), which showed a similar catalytic logic to that of
the 3-O-acyltransferase Asm19 in ansamitocin biosynthesis, i.e.,
3-O-acylation preceded 4,5-epoxidation.18
Discovery of Homologous D-Alanyltransferases and
Structural Basis of Specific D-Alanylation. As observed in
the SSN network, AstC was not an isolated entity but belonged
to a cluster of homologous enzymes, including AnsC and MycC,
which were previously identified in other ansatrienin bio-
synthetic pathways but remained uncharacterized (Figure S6).
In this concern, a phylogenetic tree of these AstC homologs was
constructed using the maximum likelihood method. Six
homologs, namely SyAstC, SmAstC, AnsC, MycC, SlAstC,
and KbAstC (Figure 2C), from different branches were selected
as natural variants to identify potential candidates with

In the assembly of NRPSs, the incorporation of D-amino acids
typically relied on the epimerization domain. However, the
absence of E domains in A-T-TE tridomain D-alanyltransferases
suggested the direct D-alanine activation by A domains. To
elucidate the configuration preference, crystallization screening
was initiated for A domains of these six D-alanyltransferases,
respectively, which successfully afforded the cocrystal structure
featuring the A domain of SyAstC complexed with 5′-O-[N-(D-
alanyl) sulfamoyl] adenosine (D-Ala-SA),31 a mimic of D-alanyl
adenylate intermediate (Figure 2D). A cavity formed by residues
F211, D212, G304, V310, and W311 was postulated to be the
binding site for the D-alanyl moiety. The D-alanyl amino group
formed a hydrogen bond with the carboxyl group of D212, the
carbonyl group of G304, and the main chain of V310,
respectively. This arrangement was entirely consistent with
that observed in D-alanyl carrier protein ligase DltA (PDB ID:
3DHV).32
However, the C269 residue in DltA, which was considered to
be a key determinant governing the preference for the D-
enantiomer, was replaced by A280 in the SyAstC A domain
correspondingly. Intriguingly, the side chain of W311 occupied a
space similar to that of C269 in DltA (Figure S9B), and its indole
group was situated 3.4 Å from the α-carbon of the D-alanyl
moiety. This suggested that W311 governed the enantiomer
preference, which was supported by the increased activity
toward L-alanine observed in the W311A mutant (Figure S10A).
Additionally, the distinct conformations of ligands in the two
chains of the SyAstC A domain may be ascribed to the shift of
the Asub domain and variations guided by F211 (Figure S10B
and S10C). This structural elucidation provided a novel example
to understand the structural basis of configuration selectivity
within A domains and offered structural support for the
preference of AstC-like D-alanyltransferases for D-alanine.
The 3-O-D-Alanylation of DDM Catalyzed by SmAstC.
Using the optimal substrate D-alanine as the acyl donor, the six
selected D-alanyltransferases (SyAstC, SmAstC, AnsC, MycC,
SlAstC, and KbAstC) displayed noteworthy disparities in their
proficiency to catalyze the conversion of DDM (3) to 3-O-D-
alanyl DDM (4). Among these, SmAstC exhibited the highest
catalytic activity toward 3, showing an 8-fold increase compared
to that of AstC in vitro (Figure 3A).
SmAstC, a homolog with 89.6% sequence identity to AstC,
was identified from a putative ansatrienin biosynthetic gene
cluster in Streptomyces sp. MMG1121 (Figure S6). Detailed
substrate specificity kinetic assays demonstrated that SmAstC
exhibited a pronounced preference for D-alanine as its acyl
donor, with significantly lower catalytic efficiency observed for
D-serine, glycine, and L-alanine (Figure S11). Additionaly, this
observation suggested that the accumulation of the D-alanylated
product did not persist over time but gradually diminished. To
gain further insights into this phenomenon, expanded in vitro
reactions were conducted, yielding 3.5 mg of 4 (NMR
spectroscopic data in Table S8). The stability assessment of 4
across a spectrum of pH values revealed its spontaneous
hydrolysis propensity, notably accelerated under alkaline
conditions (Figures 3C and S12). This spontaneous hydrolysis
was tentatively suggested to occur via the neighboring-group
participation of the D-alanine amino group.33
The kinetic parameters of SmAstC were measured during the
initial velocity phase under the optimized enzymatic conditions
(Figure S13), showing the conversion with the observed Km of
8.8 ± 0.9 μM and kcat of 11.9 ± 0.5 min−1 for D-alanine (Figure
3D). The apparent Km and kcat values for 3 were 0.24 ± 0.03 mM
and 0.28 ± 0.02 min−1, while the apparent Km and kcat values for
1 were 44 ± 5 μM and 24.0 ± 0.8 min−1 (Figure 3D),
respectively. These characterizations revealed significant

Introduction of the smastC Gene into Strain HGF052
Led to the Biosynthesis of Maytansinol. To assess the
activity of SmAstC toward DDM (3) in vivo, the smastC gene
under the kasO* promoter was integrated in the ΦC31
integration site on the chromosome of the HGF052 strain (a
gift from Heinz G. Floss laboratory at University of Washington,
Seattle). The resulted strain HGFS01 exhibited a remarkable
variation in the metabolite profile, prominently accumulating
maytansinol (5) (Figure 4A, NMR spectroscopic data in Table
S9). Originally, disruption of the 3-O-acyltransferase gene
asm19 in HGF052 hindered the effective catalysis of down-
stream modifying enzymes on DDM, so only a trace amount of
maytansinol was detected.26 In the HGFS01 strain, the
production of maytansinol would be attributed to the 3-O-D-
alanylation of DDM catalyzed by SmAstC, facilitating the
subsequent 4,5-epoxidation and N-methylation reactions
catalyzed by Asm11 and Asm10, respectively, which was
evidenced by the fact that 3-O-D-alanyl DDM (4) was not
detected.
The analysis of the EIC peaks of the fermentation products of
the HGFS01 strain unveiled the presence of distinct signals
corresponding to D-alanylated products, e.g., N-desmethyl-3-O-
D-alanyl maytansinol (6) and 3-O-D-alanyl maytansinol (7)
(NMR spectroscopic data in Table S10), and their correspond-
ing hydrolysis products N-desmethyl maytansinol (8) and
maytansinol (5) (Figure 4A). The undetected signal of 4 could
be notably observed in the fermentation of the HGFS03 strain,
wherein the smastC gene was overexpressed in the asm11-
disrupted mutant HGFS02 (Figure S14). Further, compounds 3
(10 mg) and 5 (17.6 mg) were isolated from a 5-L solid ferment
of HGFS01 to confirm the chemical structures. These results
confirmed the role of SmAstC in vivo and the biosynthetic

Rational Design of SmAstC TE Domain. Our previous
study revealed that overexpression of the pathway-specific
positive transcriptional regulator gene, asm18, effectively
increased the production of DDM (3).37 However, over-
expression of the smastC gene in the asm18-overexpressing
mutant HGFS04 failed to substantially enhance the production
of maytansinol compared to that of the HGFS01 strain (Figure
S18). This result suggested that the high levels of DDM were not
efficiently converted to maytansinol, indicating that the D-
alanylation of DDM catalyzed by SmAstC was a rate-limiting
step in this biosynthetic process. Therefore, it was unmet to
improve the catalytic efficiency of SmAstC toward DDM
through rational design methods, particularly focusing on the
SmAstC TE domain (SmAstC TE) (Figure 5A). Despite
tremendous efforts, it was refractory to obtain protein crystals of
the SmAstC TE domain or cocrystals with its substrates. A
structural model predicted by AlphaFold2 was used for the
following rational design.38 The structural alignment of SmAstC
TE and the holo-EntF T-TE domain elucidated the arrangement
of the acyl donor channel in SmAstC TE and the corresponding
binding pocket for the acyl acceptors (Figure S19).39,40
Subsequently, covalent docking simulations were performed
using the Cov_Dox server, employing the predicted catalytic
intermediate and SmAstC TE, to simulate the transition state of
D-alanylation (Figures 5B and S20).41,42
To evaluate the impact of DDM-associated residues within
the substrate-binding pocket, alanine-scanning mutagenesis was
performed on the residues within 4 Å of the ligand (Figure S21).
Mutations of W89 and F91 to alanine in the conserved GXSXG
motif led to a drastic reduction in enzymatic activities. The
residues L26, N119, I140, V212, and E239 were identified as

suggested that the steric influence exerted by K125 appears to be
the primary factor influencing the observed activity changes.
The K125G mutation of the M2 mutant ultimately resulted in a
3.2-fold increase in the production of 4 in vitro. This mutant,
namely SmAstCM, was characterized for its kinetic parameters
toward DDM, revealing Km and kcat values of 0.16 ± 0.02 mM
and 0.54 ± 0.02 min−1, respectively (Figure 5E). The calculated
kcat/Km value of SmAstCM is 2.8-fold higher than that of
SmAstC, indicating an improved substrate specificity toward
DDM.
Yield Improvement of Maytansinol. To probe the in vivo
catalytic activity of the SmAstCM mutant, HGFS06 was
constructed through the overexpression of the smastCM gene
in HGFS04. The yield of maytansinol in HGFS06 was found to
be 2.4-fold higher than that in HGFS05 (Figure 6A),
demonstrating the efficacy of rational design to enhance the
catalytic efficiency of SmAstC toward DDM. The accumulation
of 8 underscored the potential limitations of the overall
conversion of DDM to maytansinol posed by subsequent
modification steps (Figure 6A). Further overexpression of
asm11 and asm10 genes assisted the conversion of 8 to
maytansinol but failed to result in significant improvement of
maytansinol production, probably due to affecting the overall
metabolic state of this strain. On the basis of the docking
domains (DDs) of 6-deoxyerythronolide B synthase (DEBS),
the mimic DEBS enzyme assembly line strategy was recently
developed to achieve the ordered assembly of key cascade
enzymes, enhancing their collaborative catalytic efficiency.45
Consequently, this strategy was employed to assemble
SmAstCM, Asm11, and Asm10, aimed at reducing the
accumulation of intermediate metabolites and accelerating the
conversion toward maytansinol (Figures 6B and S22). The
resulted HGFS08 strain exhibited a significant increase in
maytansinol production through solid-state fermentation,
reaching 27 ± 1 mg/L (Figures 6C and S23).
To validate the usefulness of the engineered strain for scale-up
fermentation, a 3-L fermentation with a lab-scale bioreactor was
conducted to assess the performance of HGFS08. Utilizing LA
media suitable for liquid fermentation of A. pretiosum,46
maytansinol was obtained with the yield of 25 ± 2 mg/L after
10-day fermentation, and 32.8 mg of maytansinol was isolated
together with 6.0 mg of 3-O-D-alanyl maytansinol (7). The
bioreactor fermentation of HGFS08 resulted in a comparable
maytansinol yield to solid-state fermentation and enabled the
retention of previously inaccessible intermediate 7, attributed to
the shortened fermentation period and maintenance of a neutral
pH environment (Figures S24 and S25). The structural
characterization and stability assessment of 3-O-D-alanyl
maytansinol completed the final stage of the maytansinol
biosynthesis pathway. Concisely, the biosynthetic pathway of
maytansinol was optimized in A. pretiosum and achieved by
unprecedented biomanufacturing of maytansinol.
