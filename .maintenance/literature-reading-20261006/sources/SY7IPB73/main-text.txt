ABSTRACT: Mangrove derived actinomycetes are a rich reservoir
of bioactive natural products and play important roles in
pharmaceutical chemistry. In a screen of actinomycetes from
mangrove rhizosphere sedimental environments, the isolated strain
Streptomyces sp. SCSIO 40068 displayed strong antibacterial activity.
Further fractionation of the extract yielded four new compounds
kebanmycins A−D (1−4) and two known analogues FD-594 (5)
and the aglycon (6). The structures of 1−6 were determined based
on extensive spectroscopic data and single-crystal X-ray diffraction
analysis. 1−3 featured a fused pyranonaphthaxanthene as an integral
part of a 6/6/6/6/6/6 polycyclic motif, and showed bioactivity
against a series of Gram-positive bacteria and cytotoxicity to several
human tumor cells. In addition, the kebanmycins biosynthetic gene cluster (keb) was identified in Streptomyces sp. SCSIO 40068,
and KebMT2 was biochemically characterized as a tailoring sugar-O-methyltransferase, leading to a proposed biosynthetic route to
1−6. This study paves the way to further investigate 1 as a potential lead compound.
T
he mangrove ecosystem, characterized by fluctuating
physicochemical conditions and moderate saline environ-
ments, harbors diverse microorganisms, which are potential
sources of pharmaceutically relevant natural products,1−3 such
as polycyclic xanthone-type natural products (PXTNPs).4
PXTNPs featured a highly oxygenated, curved hexacyclic
core.4 Over the past decades, this family of natural products
has been expanded by natural isolation or genetic engineering
of the related biosynthetic pathways, leading to the discovery
of xantholipin,5 turbinmicin,6 simaomicin α,7 lysolipin I,8 FD-
594,9 cervinomycins,10 fluoxanthones11 and calixanthomycin
A.12 Most PXTNPs contain a lactam ring; only five reported
PXTNPs harbor a lactone ring (Figure S1).9,12−17 The
majority of PXTNPs exhibit cytotoxic and antimicrobial
activities.18 For instance, calixanthomycin A is toxic to HCT-
116 cancer cells;12 xantholipin is cytotoxic to HL60 and KB
tumor cell lines;19 cervinomycin B3 demonstrates antibacterial
activity against a wide range of Gram-positive bacteria;10 both
FD-594 and BE-13793X display moderate cytotoxicity against
tumor cell lines and bactericidal activity against Gram-positive
bacteria.9,14 Additionally, turbinmicin exhibits broad-spectrum
activity against human fungal pathogens, both in vitro and in
vivo, possibly by targeting Sec14p.6 The intricate structures and
promising bioactivities of PXTNPs have spurred great interest
among chemists and biochemists. For example, various
research groups have successfully synthesized FD-59420,21
and its homologues.17,22
In our efforts to explore bioactive natural products from
marine-derived microorganisms, a mangrove rhizosphere
sediment-derived Actinomycete Streptomyces sp. SCSIO
4006823 was subjected to chemical investigation, which led
to the isolation of four new polycyclic xanthones, kebanmycins
A−D (1−4), along with the known FD-594 (5) and its aglycon
(6) (Figure 1). 1 showed antibacterial activities against
methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) strains
and cytotoxic activities against several tumor cell lines. The
biosynthetic pathway of 1−6 was proposed upon the
identification of the biosynthetic gene cluster (keb BGC) of
kebanmycins and the in vitro functional characterization of the
methyltransferase KebMT2.

Compound 5 was assigned as FD-594 by comparison of its
HRESIMS and NMR data with literature data (Table S1,
Figure S4).13,14 The structure of 5 was further validated by X-
ray crystallographic analysis (Figure 1, CCDC 2309293,
TableS2) using Cu Kα radiation to unambiguously assign its
absolute configuration as 3R,6S,7S, β-D-oleandrose, and β-D-
olivose, upon the Flack parameter value 0.00(4) and the Hooft
value 0.01(3) (Table S3). Compound 6 was assigned to be the
FD-594 aglycon by comparison of its HRESIMS and NMR
data with those reported for the chemically synthesized one
(Table S1, Figure S5).20
Kebanmycin A (1) was obtained as a yellow powder with a
molecular formula of C47H56O19, established by HRESIMS
(Figure S6). The 1H and 13C NMR data of 1 and 5 (Figures S6
and S4) were almost identical, except that 1 lacked the 7-OH
group in 5. This assignment was supported by the observed
COSY correlation of H-6/H2-7 in 1 (Table 1, Figure 2). The
relative configurations of the oleandrose and olivose units were
assigned by the coupling constants and NOESY correlations
(Table 1). The large vicinal coupling constant of 3JH‑3′/H‑4′ (8.9
Hz), 3JH‑4′/H‑5′ (8.9 Hz); 3JH‑3′′/H‑4′′ (8.9 Hz), 3JH‑4′′/H‑5′′ (8.9
Hz); 3JH‑3′′′/H‑4′′′ (8.6 Hz), 3JH‑4′′′/H‑5′′′ (8.6 Hz) indicated that
H-3′/H-4′/H-5′, H-3′′/H-4′′/H-5′′ and H-3′′′/H-4′′′/H-5′′′
should be in axial orientations, which were confirmed by
NOESY correlations of Hax-2′/H-4′, Hax-2′′/H-4′′, Hax-2′′′/
H-4′′′. The NOESY correlations of H-1′/H-3′, H-1′′/H-3′′,
and H-1′′′/H-3′′′ indicated that H-1′, H-1′′ and H-1′′′ were
in the α-position. Considering the biosynthetic relationship of
1 and 5, the absolute configuration of 1 was tentatively
assigned as 3R,6S with β-D-oleandrose and β-D-olivose.
Kebanmycin B (2) was obtained as a yellow powder. The
molecular formula of 2 was assigned as C46H54O19 by
HRESIMS (Figure S7). The 1H and 13C NMR data of 2 and
5 (Figures S7 and S4) were highly similar. Compound 2 was
found to differ from 5 only by the absence of a methoxy group
at 3′′′−OH, which was supported by the COSY correlation of
3′′′−OH/H-3′′′, and HMBC correlations from 3″′−OH to C-
2′′′/C-3′′′ and from H-1′′′ to C-3′′′. Consequently, the
structure of 2 was elucidated (Figure 1) and tentatively
assigned to have the same configuration as 5, deducing from
similar experimental ECD curves of 5 and 2 (Figure S8).
Kebanmycin C (3) was obtained as a yellow powder with a
molecular formula of C34H34O14 established by HRESIMS
(Figure S9). A comparative analysis of the 1H and 13C NMR
data of 3 and 5 indicated that both shared the same aglycon
moiety. However, they differed in the number of sugar units
attached. Only one anomeric methine (δH 5.15/δC 98.7) was
observed in 3, suggesting only one sugar unit in 3.
Subsequently, a β-olivose was assigned in 3 by detailed
analysis of 1D and 2D NMR data, and was linked at 12-OH
based on the HMBC from H-1′ to C-12. Thus, the planar
structure of 3 was established. The absolute configuration of 3
was tentatively assigned as 3R,6S,7S and β-D-olivose upon
being considered as a biosynthetic precursor of 5 and similar
experimental ECD curves of 3 and 5 (Figure S8).

Kebanmycin D (4) was obtained as an orange powder. The
molecular formula of 4 was assigned as C28H22O9 by
HRESIMS (Figure S10). The 1H and 13C NMR data of 4
lacked specific resonances typically attributed to sugar moieties
(Figure S10, Table 2), suggesting no sugar units in 4.
Additionally, 4 was found to be different from 5 in rings A,
C, D, E, and F. Rather than the lactone ring A in 5, compound
4 had an opened A ring with a carbonyl group at C-3 (δC
197.0) and a carboxyl group at C-1 (δC 172.3), which was
supported by COSY correlations of H3-19/H2-18/H2-17,
HMBC correlations from H-17 to C-3. The presence of a
pair of methylenes (CH2−6/CH2−7) in 4 was indicated by
COSY correlations of H2-6/H2-7, and the HMBC correlations
H2-6 to C-7a/C-15b, H2-7 to C-8/C-15a, which were different
from the vicinal diols in ring C of 5. The methoxy at C-8 and
hydroxy group at C-15 in ring D of 5 were absent in 4; in
contrast, compound 4 was assigned to have 8-OH and 15-CH
as confirmed by the HMBC correlations from 8-OH to C-8/C-
8a and H-15 to C-7a/C-8a. The HMBC correlations from 8-
OH/10-OH to C-9 (190.1) and H-13 to C-14 (182.0)
revealed that ring E in 4 was a p-benzoquinone, instead of the
pyranone in 5. The presence of meta-coupled aromatic protons
and HMBC correlations from H-11 to C-10/C-11/C-12 and
from H-13 to C-9a/C-13a indicated a change from the 1,2,3,4-
substituted benzene ring (ring F) in 5 to a 1,2,3,5-substituted
benzene ring in 4. Thus, the structure of 4 was determined as
shown in Figure 1.

(MRSA). However, these compounds were nearly ineffective
against the Gram-negative bacteria Vibrio alginolyticus 13214
and Acinetobacter baumannii ATCC 19606 (Table 3). 1
displayed strong antibacterial activity against MRSA, generally
superior to the positive control vancomycin. Notably, 1

AntiSMASH (version 7.0)24 analysis of the genome
sequence of Streptomyces sp. SCSIO 40068 identified the
KBM biosynthesis gene cluster (keb BGC), which is highly
similar to the pnx BGC for FD594 in Streptomyces sp. TA-
0256.25 The proteins encoded by the keb BGC share 50−89%
identity with the those corresponding proteins in the pnx BGC
(Table S3). The keb cluster contains 40 open reading frames
(orfs) and encodes three type II polyketide synthases, 13
oxygenases/reductases, three regulators/transporters, three
cyclases, three unknown proteins, and 15 other tailoring
enzymes (Figure 3, Table S4).
The feeding experiments with 13C-labeled sodium acetate
revealed that xantholipin,19 lysolipin I,26 and FD-59413 were
derived from a type II polyketide pathway. A flavin-dependent
monooxygenase XanO4 was characterized to catalyze the
formation of the core xanthone ring in the xantholipin
pathway.27 Three O-methyltransferases, XanM1-M3, were
shown to introduce methyl groups in a substrate-specific and
regiospecific manner.28 The glycosyltransferase PnxGT2 and
the methyltransferase PnxMT2 were demonstrated to perform
the final steps in the biosynthesis of FD-594.25 However, the
detailed biochemical function of PnxMT2 remain unclear due
to the lack of a structurally characterized substrate.25

butyryl- acyl carrier protein (ACP), was proposed to be formed
via an intriguing hybrid fatty acid synthase (FAS) and

KebV, the acyltransferase KebJ) and FAS enzymes (a
ketoreductuse, a dehydratase and an enoylreductase) recruited
from endogenous fatty acid metabolism (Figure 3).25 Similar
strategies to generate an unusual long chain starter unit were
also hypothesized in the biosynthesis of benastatins,29
alnumycin,30 R1128,31 and galbonolide.32 Then, the butyryl-
ACP could be condensed with 12 malonyl-CoA extender units
by the minimal type-II PKS enzymes (KebABC) and the
cyclases (KebDKL) to give a pentangular polyketide backbone
M1. The polyketide precursor M1 could undergo oxidative
modifications and thioester-mediated hydrolysis to yield M2. A
reduction of the C6/C7 double bond and C-12 hydroxylation
in M2 would generate kebanmycin D (4) as a shunt product.
Three cytochrome P450s (KebO2, KebO3 and KebO5) and a
lactonase KebO1 are proposed to convert M2 to M3 (Figure
3). The FAD-binding monooxygenases KebO6 and KebO4
would then catalyze the formation of the xanthone ring in the
FD-594 aglycon (6) from M3 (Figure 3), via a series of
reactions including an epoxidation, a Baeyer−Villiger oxida-
tion, and an oxidative decarboxylation, in a similar way to the
XanO4 and FlsO1 mediated xanthone formation.11,27 The
flavoenzyme XanO4 was biochemically characterized to
convert an anthraquinone precursor to the xanthone ring via
multiple oxidations without the help of other coenzymes.27
Similarly, the flavin dependent enzyme FlsO1 in the fluostatin
biosynthetic pathway was shown to mediate the unexpected
formation of fluoxanthone A, a xanthone-containing com-
pound, via multistep oxidations including a hydroxylation, an
epoxidation and a Baeyer−Villiger oxidation.11 The O-
methyltransferase KebMT1 (Table S4) is likely responsible
for introducing the O-methyl group at 8-OH of 6. The two
glycosyltransferases KebGT1 and KebGT2 are proposed to
attach two olivoses and an oleandrose to the FD-594 aglycon
to yield 3 and 2, followed by the KebMT2-catalyzed sugar O-
methylation to give FD-594 (5). Finally, the dehydroxylation

In summary, four novel PXTNPs kebanmycinsA-D (1-4)
were isolated from a mangrove-derived Streptomyces sp. SCSIO
40068. kebanmycin A (1) exhibited superior anti-MRSA and
antitumor activity compared to FAD594 (5), even better than
the positive controls vancomycin and adriamycin. This finding
highlights that the absence of the 7-OH is significant for
enhancing the antibacterial activity. Therefore, kebanmycin A
(1) displays significant anti-MRSA efficiency and thus holds
potential as a promising candidate for further anti-MRSA drug
development. Finally, we successfully identified the BGC of
kebanmycins by in vitro biochemical characterization of the
methyltransferase KebMT2 and propose a plausible biosyn-
thetic scheme of 1-6.

Fermentation, Extraction and Isolation. The strain SCSIO
40068 was cultivated on 38# (yeast extract 4.0 g L−1, glucose 4.0 g

medium (the same as the seed medium). The fermentation (a total of
16 L) was carried out at 28 °C, 200 rpm for 7 days. The supernatants
and mycelia were separated by centrifugation at 3900 rpm for 10 min.

further extracted 8 times with 3 L of butanone. The butanone extracts

X-ray Crystallographic Analysis. Single crystals of FD-594 (5)
were obtained in butanone. The crystal data was collected on an
XtaLAB AFC12 (RINC): Kappa single diffractometer with Cu Kα
radiation (λ = 1.54184 Å). The crystal was kept at 100.00(10) K
during the data collection. Using Olex2, the crystal structure was
solved by the ShelXT structure solution program with Intrinsic
Phasing and refined with the ShelXL refinement package using Least
Squares minimization. The Solvent Mask in Olex2 has been applied to
process the disordered solvent; 78 electrons were found in a volume
of 310 Å3 void per unit cell. Crystallographic data have been
deposited in the Cambridge Crystallographic Data Center with the
deposition number CCDC 2309293. The crystal data of 5 see Table
S3 and the melting point of 5 is 198.7−202.4 °C.
Antimicrobial Assays. Antimicrobial activities were measured
against eight indicator strains, including S. aureus ATCC 29213,
Bacillus subtilis 1064, methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) shhs-A1,
MRSA 1862, MRSA 669, MRSA 991, V. alginolyticus ATCC 13214
and A. baumannii 19606 by the broth microdilution method. The
indicator strains were grown for 12 h on a rotary shaker at 37 °C.
Cultures were diluted with sterilized medium to achieve an optical
absorbance of 0.04−0.06 at 600 nm and then diluted 1000-fold before
being added into 96-well micro titer plates. Two replicates of each
compound were tested in dilution series ranging from 64 to 0.125 μg
mL−1. The lowest concentrations that completely inhibited the visible
growth of the tested strains were recorded after 16 h of cultivation. In
this assay, vancomycin and ciprofloxacin were used as positive
controls.
Expression and Purification of KebMT2. DNA fragments
carrying kebMT2 were PCR amplified from the genomic DNA of
Streptomyces sp. SCSIO 40068. Then the purified PCR product was
subcloned into pET28a to yield the plasmids pCSG5242 (for
KebMT2 expression) (Table S5, Table S6). The overexpression of
kebMT2 was carried out in E. coli BL21(DE3)/pCSG5242 in LB
media containing 50 μg/mL kanamycin at 16 °C for 20 h by the
induction of 0.1 mM IPTG (isopropylthio-β-galactoside). Purification
of (His)6-tagged KebMT2 was conducted using Ni-NTA affinity
chromatography according to the manufacturer’s manual (Novagen,
USA). The purified KebMT2 was desalted on a PD-10 column (GE
Healthcare, USA) and stored in the storage buffer (10% glycerol, 1
mM DTT, 50 mM Tris-Cl, 100 mM NaCl, pH 8.0) at −80 °C. The
concentration of purified enzyme was determined using a NanoDrop
UV−vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific).
In Vitro Enzyme Assays. The in vitro enzyme reaction of
KebMT2 was conducted in 100 μL phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH
7.0) comprising 200 μM KBM B (2), 2 mM SAM, 5 μM
KebMT2,with or without 2 mM MgCl2. The reactions were incubated
for 2h at 30 °C and then were quenched by mixing with 100 μL of ice-
cold MeOH. HPLC analysis of the enzyme reactions was carried out
on the Agilent 1260 Infinity series instrument (Agilent Technologies
Inc., USA) using a reversed phase column Luna C18 (5 μm, 150 × 4.6
mm, Phenomenex) with UV detection at 304 nm under the following
program: solvent system (solvent A, 10% MeCN in water
supplemented with 0.1% formic acid; solvent B, 90% MeCN in
water); 5% B to 80% B (0−20 min), 100% B (21−24 min), 100% B
to 5% B (24−25 min), 5% B (25−30 min); flow rate at 1 mL/min.
