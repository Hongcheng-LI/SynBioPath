ABSTRACT: Azetidine is a strained four-membered N-heterocycle that has
important applications in medicinal chemistry and organic synthesis. Despite its
relevance to human health and agriculture, azetidine biosynthesis remains largely
unexplored. Herein, the DUF6202 family enzyme PolF is shown to utilize a diiron
center and O2 to catalyze sequential desaturation and azetidination of L-isoleucine
to yield the ethylidene azetidine moiety during the biosynthesis of polyoxin.
Crystallographic analysis of PolF in complex with iron (or manganese) and L-
isoleucine (or an alkene intermediate) reveals that it is a heme oxygenase-like
diiron oxidase with a new iron-binding motif. Trapping an O2-bound intermediate
in crystallo along with a distinctive iron-to-protein ratio observed under anoxic and
aerobic environments indicates that binding of the second iron occurs in an O2-
dependent manner. Mössbauer and stopped-flow optical absorption spectroscopy together with deuterium kinetic isotope effect
measurements demonstrate that a μ-peroxodiiron(III) species is likely responsible for C−H bond activation. Mass Spectrometry
analysis reveals that the decay of the μ-peroxodiiron(III) species is accompanied by the formation of a desaturated intermediate.
Bioinformatic analysis identifies ca. 200 PolF homologues indicative of new biosynthetic pathways for azetidine-containing natural
products.
■INTRODUCTION
Azetidines are four-membered nitrogen heterocycles with
significant ring strain (ca. 25 kcal/mol),1,2 making them both
chemically intriguing and synthetically challenging. Despite
their utility in pharmaceutical development (Figure S1A) and
an intense focus on their chemical synthesis,2−4 the biosyn-
thesis of azetidine rings remains poorly understood (Figure
S1B). At present, the only experimentally characterized
examples of azetidine biosynthesis are the azetidomonamides
and vioprolide A, where AzeJ and VioH catalyze intramolecular
4-exo-tet cyclization of S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) yielding
azetidine-2-carboxylic acid with the release of methylthioade-
nosine (MTA) (Figure 1A).5−7
Polyoxins are a family of nucleoside antibiotics that have
been used as fungicides since the 1960s due to their potent
inhibition of fungal chitin synthase (Figures 1B and S2).9−12
These compounds exhibit strong efficacy against phytopatho-
genic fungi including Pyricularia oryzae, Cochliobolus miyabea-
nus, Botrytis cinerea, and Alternaria kikuchiana and have been
used as agricultural fungicides.13,14 Polyoxins A, F, H, and K
are composed of three main building blocks: a nucleoside
skeleton, polyoximic acid (POIA) that contains an ethylidene
azetidine moiety, and carbamoylpolyoxamic acid (CPOAA).
While the biosynthetic pathways for the nucleoside skeleton
and CPOAA have been previously characterized,8,15−19 the
enzymatic steps responsible for installation of the ethylidene
azetidine moiety in POIA remain unclear. Early feeding studies
show that the carbon skeleton of POIA originates from L-
isoleucine,20 indicating that the ethylidene azetidine moiety
requires formation of both C−N and CC bonds (Figure
1B). In addition, the polyoxin biosynthetic gene cluster pol
includes the three genes polC/E/F that have been hypothe-
sized to participate in POIA biosynthesis.3,8
Herein, PolF alone is established to be responsible for
catalyzing the formation of POIA from L-isoleucine. PolF is a
member of the domain of unknown function DUF6202 family
of proteins and was previously annotated as a molybdopterin-
dependent oxidoreductase;8 however, X-ray crystallographic
analysis of PolF in complex with cofactor and substrate
demonstrates that it belongs to the family of heme oxygenase-
like diiron oxidases (HDO) but employs a new iron-binding
motif. Biochemical assay further shows that PolF utilizes
molecular oxygen as an oxidant and catalyzes desaturation,
followed by azetidination. Although the four-electron oxidation
of substrate is redox balanced by the reduction of O2 to H2O,

PolC, PolE, and PolF were heterologously overexpressed in
Escherichia coli in order to determine their enzymatic activities.
Initial attempts to express PolC and PolE in BL21 (DE3)
resulted in insoluble inclusion bodies; however, soluble
proteins were successfully obtained by fusing them to
maltose-binding protein (Figure S5). Structural modeling of
PolC revealed a glycine substitution at position 120 (Gly120)
in place of a conserved aspartate/glutamate residue critical for
iron coordination (Figure S6);21 therefore, G120D and G120E
mutants were also prepared for comparison. When L-isoleucine
was incubated with PolC, PolE, and PolF, followed by
derivatization with dansyl chloride, a new compound was
detected with a mass-to-charge ratio matching that of
dansylated POIA (1-Dns) (Figure 2A). The same result was
obtained whether PolF was incubated with L-isoleucine alone
or in combinations with PolC and PolF, demonstrating that
PolF alone is sufficient for the formation of POIA. Neither
PolC, PolC-G120D, PolC-G120E nor PolE alone produced
any new product peak upon incubation with
L-isoleucine
(Figure S7).
To identify cofactors required for the reaction, PolF was
incubated with L-isoleucine and ascorbate in the presence of
either Fe(II) or Mn(II) versus their absence. Product was
observed only in reactions containing Fe(II), and no activity
was detected with Mn(II) or without Fe(II) (Figure 2B). The
requirement of O2 was also confirmed by performing the
reaction anaerobically, which resulted in no product formation.
In addition to ascorbate, other reductants including dithio-
threitol, sodium dithionite, and β-mercaptoethanol also
enhanced reactivity, whereas the reaction only yielded trace
levels of 1-Dns when a reductant was not included in the assay
(Figure S8). These results suggest that Fe(II) and O2 are
required, while various reductants can facilitate the reaction.
Moreover, several minor products were also detected. Based on
their m/z values, these products are proposed to be
desaturation or cyclization products and a hydroxylation
product (Figure S9).
A large-scale enzymatic reaction was performed to character-
ize the identity of the reaction product. The reaction was
performed by incubating a solution containing 0.2 mM PolF, 1
mM ferrous ammonium sulfate, 5 mM L-ascorbate, and 2 mM
substrate at room temperature for 16 h with agitation at 80
rpm and exposure to air. After the reaction, dansyl
derivatization and preparative high-performance liquid chro-
matography (HPLC) were conducted.
1H and
13C NMR
analysis of the purified, dansylated product (1-Dns) led to the
identification of six additional carbon centers (in addition to
the dansylation groups) with the following distinct features:
one carbonyl (δC 168.9), one olefin (δC 124.3 and 123.7; δH
5.45), two nitrogen-linked carbons (δC 68.5 and 57.4; δH 5.26,
4.45, and 4.30), and one methyl group (δC 12.8; δH 1.53)
(Figures S29 and 30). The key (Z)-ethylidene azetidine moiety
was demonstrated by a suite of two-dimensional (2D) NMR
experiments (Figures 2E and S31−34, Table S2). COSY
established the spin system from C5 to C6. HMBC analysis
demonstrated that this system is connected to C3 as indicated
by interactions between the H6 and H5 (δH 1.53 and 5.45)
resonances with C3 as well as C2 and C4 (δC 124.3, 68.5, and
57.4). The 1H−13C long-range coupling between H4 and C2
together with the downfield chemical shift of the methylene
group at C4 confirmed the connectivity of the azetidine ring.
The Z (cis) stereochemistry of the ethylidene group was
assigned based on the interaction between H2 and H6 (δH
5.26 and 1.53) observed by NOESY (Figure S34).
Substrate Specificity. To delineate the substrate scope of
PolF, the enzyme was incubated with various amino acids.
PolF also recognizes D-isoleucine despite reduced turnover,
generating two peaks (1b-Dns and 1c-Dns) with the same m/z
as that of 1-Dns that likely correspond to stereoisomers of D-
ethylidene azetidine with the Z and E double bond
configuration (Figure 2D). Additionally, PolF catalyzes the
oxidation of L- and D-valine to form desaturated azetidine
products 2a-Dns and 2b-Dns, which showed the same
retention time, m/z value, and fragmentation pattern. A
large-scale enzymatic reaction confirmed that the chemical
structure of 2a-Dns contains an azetidine ring with an
exocyclic terminal double bond (Figures 2E and S35−39,
Table S3). A preliminary competition experiment utilizing L-
isoleucine and L-valine with PolF indicated that L-isoleucine is
the better substrate; however, its value of kcat/KM appears to be
no more than 5-fold greater than that of L-valine (Supporting
Information and Figure S10). No detectable azetidine products
were observed when L-leucine, L-alanine, or L-glycine were
supplied as substrates. These findings highlight the relatively
stringent substrate selectivity of PolF and indicate that both C3
substitution (e.g., the ethyl and methyl groups in isoleucine and
valine) and the stereochemistry at C3 (S-configuration in L-
isoleucine) are critical for effective catalysis.
Oxygen-Dependent Iron-Binding Stoichiometry. The
iron-binding stoichiometry of PolF was determined in order to
investigate how PolF enables alkene azetidine formation, which
is a transformation not previously observed among HDO
enzymes and other metalloenzymes. When PolF was overex-
pressed in E. coli grown in either Terrific Broth or minimal
medium, the purified protein contained only trace levels of iron
(Figure S11). Supplementing ferrous salts increased iron
content only marginally, indicating that PolF has weak intrinsic
iron-binding affinity. Given that HDO enzymes typically
employ two Fe(II) ions per protein,29,30 PolF was incubated
with 2−10 equiv of Fe(II) and unbound metal was removed
using size-exclusion chromatography. Analysis of protein and
iron concentrations revealed an iron-to-protein stoichiometry
of ca. 1:1, suggesting that PolF binds only one Fe(II) ion under
anaerobic conditions. Preincubation of PolF with the substrate
prior to iron reconstitution did not alter this stoichiometry. A
similar phenomenon has been observed in the case of the
HDO enzyme UndA, which was reported to bind 0.82 iron per
protein monomer under N2,31 but was later confirmed as a
diiron enzyme upon exposure to O2.32 This suggests that O2
may be required for the assembly of the catalytic diiron
complex. Indeed, when the iron-binding of PolF was measured
under aerobic conditions, the iron-to-protein stoichiometry
increased to 2:1 (Figure S11).

Most HDO enzymes possess a conserved E-H-E-H-(D/E)-
H motif that coordinates the dinuclear metal center.28,30,34−36
This motif is a hallmark of the HDO superfamily and is critical
for binding the diiron cofactor; however, PolF features a
notable deviation from this canonical motif. While the initial
glutamate residue in this motif is retained in PolF (Glu114), it
does not coordinate iron and is instead replaced by an
aspartate residue (Asp104) located outside the otherwise
conserved binding motif, resulting in a D-(E)-H-E-H-E-H
motif. Sequence alignment with other HDO enzymes
demonstrates that this aspartate residue is not conserved
again, highlighting a unique adaptation in PolF (Figure 3D).
Alanine scanning of all proposed metal-coordinating
residues produced mutants that were expressed as soluble
proteins with the exception of H221A (Figure S5). Only the
E114A mutant remained active, while all other mutants were
inactive (Figure 3E). This finding indicates that the initial

In the active site, unequivocal difference electron density
corresponding to L-isoleucine was observed in all six chains.
The amino group and carboxylate oxygen of
L-isoleucine
coordinate Fe1 in a bidentate fashion with Fe−O and Fe−N
distances of 2.3 and 2.4 Å, respectively (Figure 3C). The
carboxylate group is further stabilized by hydrogen bonding
interactions with Trp58 and Thr62 (Figure S15). Three
hydrophobic amino acids (i.e., Phe56, Val179, and Ala183)
form a cavity that accommodates the side chain of L-isoleucine
(Figure S15). A similar substrate binding mode has been
reported for the HDO enzyme AetD, where 5,7-dibromo-L-
tryptophan binds in a comparable manner during nitrile group
formation.33
F0−Fc electron difference density corresponding to a ligand
coordinated to the Fe1 center was observed in the active sites
of chains B, C, and F, each of which contains two irons. The
electron density in these chains was best modeled as molecular
oxygen, as it is significantly larger than that of water and does
not match other components present in the crystallization
medium (Figure S16). The Fe1−O and Fe2−O distances in
chain B and C are 2.4 and 2.7 Å, respectively (Figure 3C),
while both Fe−O distances in chain F are approximately 2.5 Å
(Figure S17). The presence of O2 in chains B, C, and F is
presumably due to diffusion of the O2 into the crystal during
loop handling under aerobic conditions. Analyzing the crystals
using single-crystal ultraviolet−visible (UV−vis) spectroscopy
did not show the characteristic features of a peroxo-Fe2(III/

likely represents a state prior to peroxo-Fe2(III/III) formation.
A crystal structure of PolF (PolF·Mn·D-6) was also
determined at 2.1 Å resolution by cocrystallization of PolF
with manganese and D-6 (Figure 3F), which is commercially
available (Arctom Scientific LLC) and shown in the section
below to be a reaction intermediate when D-valine is the
substrate (Figure S24). Efforts to obtain cocrystals with D-
valine or L-valine were unsuccessful. This manganese-bound
structure is highly similar to the iron-bound form and also
exhibits six PolF molecules in the asymmetric unit (Figures 3F
and S18, Table S5). In each chain, electron density indicative
of only a single metal ion at the Fe1 site was observed (Figure
3F). Clear electron density for D-6 reveals two distinct binding
modes with the metal: a bidentate coordination that is similar
to the structure complexed with Fe(II) and L-isoleucine, and a
distinct monodentate coordination where only the carboxyl
group coordinates to the metal (Figures 3F and S19). A
hydrogen bond interaction was observed between the amino
groups of D-6 and Glu221. The stronger electron density
observed for the alkene moiety relative to the methyl group
supports a trans orientation between the amine and the
terminal double bond (Figure S20).
Transient Kinetics and Spectroscopic Analysis.
Stopped-flow optical absorption spectroscopy studies were
conducted in order to investigate the reaction mechanism.
Rapid mixing of the Fe(II)-reconstituted enzyme (Fe(II)2-
PolF) with O2-saturated buffer led to a gradual accumulation
of a feature in the wavelength region of 300−400 nm (Figure
S21), which most likely originated from oxidation of the diiron
center. In the presence of L-isoleucine, however, a transient
blue species emerged with an absorption band at λmax = 605
nm that reached a maximum at ca. 7 s and fully decayed by
about 300 s. This optical feature is characteristic of a μ-1,2-
peroxo-Fe(III)2 intermediate previously observed in several
other HDO enzymes (Figure 4A).26,28,32 When the experiment
was repeated with d10-L-isoleucine, the same blue species was
observed with an identical formation rate as
L-isoleucine;
however, its decay was perturbed (Figure 4B). By using a
three-step kinetic model (see details in the SI), the decay rate
constants were determined to be 0.013 and 0.006 s−1 for L-
isoleucine and d10-L-leucine revealing a deuterium kinetic
isotope effect of ca. 2. Furthermore, the same blue species was

To corroborate the findings from stopped-flow optical
absorption spectroscopy, freeze-quench experiments were
conducted by quenching the reaction in liquid ethane at
selected time intervals. Mössbauer analysis revealed that a new
Mössbauer quadrupole doublet with isomer shift (δ) of 0.59
mm/s and quadrupole splitting (|ΔEQ|) of 1.27 mm/s emerged
after rapid mixing of Fe(II)2-PolF with an O2-saturated buffer
in the presence of d10-L-isoleucine. The parameters of this new
species are typical of the μ-1,2-peroxo-Fe(III)2 intermediates
characterized in other HDO enzymes.26,28,29,32 It accumulated
to 45 and 65% of total iron at 0.5 and 10 s. At 30 and 300 s, it
decayed to 52 and 40%, respectively (Figures 4D and S47,
Tables S6 and S7). Additionally, a mononuclear ferric species
slowly accumulated and reached ca. 50% of the total iron in the
sample in 300 s (Table S7). This ferric species is likely due to
the disintegration of the diiron center via decay of the μ-1,2-
peroxo-Fe(III)2 intermediate. The disintegration of the diiron
center into mononuclear ferric species during the enzymatic
reactions is a hallmark of HDO enzymes. In summary, while
rapid interconversion of μ-1,2-peroxo-Fe(III)2 with other
reactive species (e.g., high-valent diiron intermediates) cannot
be excluded, the present data establish the substrate- and O2-
dependent formation of a peroxo-diferric intermediate.
Furthermore, the kinetic isotope effect implies that this
intermediate is involved in C−H bond cleavage.
Identification of a PolF Reaction Intermediate. The
conversion of the diiron center from Fe(II)2 to μ-1,2-peroxo-
Fe(III)2, and subsequently to ferric iron, indicates net
oxidation of the diiron center during the reaction. While this
aligns with two-electron oxidations observed with other HDO
enzymes,26,33,37,38 the reaction catalyzed by PolF involves a
four-electron oxidation of the substrate. To elucidate the
reaction pathway, chemical-quench experiments were per-
formed with L-isoleucine as the substrate and samples were
analyzed by LC-MS. In samples quenched at 300 s, three peaks
(p1−p3) corresponding to m/z −2 and one peak (p4) with
m/z + 16 relative to L-isoleucine were observed, along with a
small amount of 1 (Figure S22). This observation suggested
the possible involvement of intermediate products in a two-
step reaction. These peaks were barely detectable in samples
quenched for 0.5 s. Compared to the sample quenched at 300
s, p2 reached approximately 18−26% in the samples quenched
at 30 and 100 s (Figures 5A and S22B).
The increase in p1−p3 correlates with the decrease of the μ-
1,2-peroxo-Fe(III)2 intermediate consistent with its role as a
key reaction intermediate. In the presence of L-valine at 300 s,
two peaks were detected with an m/z of +16 versus substrate
and an additional two peaks had an m/z of −2 versus substrate
(Figure 5C). The former two products were assigned as the
hydroxylated species
L-3 and
L-4 by comparison with
commercial standards of L-3, L-4, and L-5 (Arctom Scientific
LLC). Of the latter two dehydrogenation products, one was
assigned as
L-6 by comparison with a synthetic standard
prepared according to a published procedure and confirmed by
comparison of its spectroscopic data with previous reports
(Figures S45 and 46).39,40 The second dehydrogenation
product (7) is proposed to be a stereoisomer of 5 due to a
different retention time compared to 5 and 6. Compound 7
may be produced during the derivatization. Structural
assignment of product L-3 was also validated by large-scale
enzymatic reaction and subsequent NMR characterization
following isolation (Figures S40−44 and Table S4).
To determine which of the C3-hydroxylation, C4-hydrox-
ylation, azetidine, or desaturation products is an on-pathway
intermediate, L-3, L-4, L-5, and L-6 were incubated individually
with PolF, and only L-6 was converted to the final product 2a
(Figure 5D). In addition, D-valine and compound D-6, which is
a stereoisomer of
L-6, can also be converted to the
corresponding alkene azetidine product 2b, which is the

and Fe(II) (Figures S23−25). These results collectively reveal

■CONCLUSIONS
PolF catalyzes sequential desaturation and azetidination to
yield polyoximic acid, thereby resolving the missing link in
polyoxin biosynthesis. Structural and biochemical analyses
reveal that PolF, which was previously annotated as a
molybdopterin-dependent oxidoreductase,8 is a heme oxygen-
ase-like diiron oxidase. Unlike other HDO enzymes, PolF
employs a distinctive metal-binding motif, D-(E)-H-E-H-E-H,
instead of the canonical E-H-E-H-E-H motif. Differences in
iron-to-protein stoichiometry under anaerobic and aerobic
conditions along with in crystallo trapping of a plausible O2-
bound state in the protein active site suggest a unique cofactor
assembly sequence: (a) Fe(II) binds in site 1, (b) substrate
binds to Fe1, and (c) O2 coordinates to Fe1 upon which the
second iron binds to site 2 (Figure 6). The distinctive
sequence features and cofactor assembly PolF led to the
bioinformatic identification of 198 homologues in the
DUF6202 family. Most homologues are colocalized with
nonribosomal peptide synthetase and polyketide synthase
biosynthetic gene clusters, which suggests their involvement
in uncharacterized olefin azetidine-containing natural products.
Transient enzyme kinetics experiments demonstrate that
substrate binding is required for μ-1,2-peroxo-Fe(III)2
intermediate formation, which is a phenomenon that has also
been observed with BesC and AetD.26,33 Mössbauer analysis of
freeze-quenched samples reveals a conversion of the diiron
center from Fe(II)2 to μ-1,2-peroxo-Fe(III)2, which is followed
by its slow decay. This progression aligns with the formation of
a desaturated intermediate product, as confirmed by LC-MS
analysis of the corresponding chemically quenched samples
and experiments with the putative intermediates. Together,
these findings imply that PolF catalysis involves two sequential
reactions, namely, desaturation followed by azetidination.
Biochemical assays, protein structures, transient kinetics, and
spectroscopic analyses further reveal that the substrate binding
pocket can accommodate both L/D-isoleucine and L/D-valine,
while
L-leucine,
L-alanine, and
L-glycine do not yield any
azetidine product. This suggests a strict requirement for a
tertiary carbon at the C3 position of the substrate. The
presence of electron density for the alkene moiety of D-6 in the
enzyme active site suggests that the azetidination may occur
between the methyl and amine group of the olefin intermediate
(Figure S20).
The results collectively support the mechanistic proposal
outlined in Figure 6. Upon substrate binding at the Fe1 site,
the addition of O2 facilitates Fe2 site assembly. A small but
measurable deuterium isotope effect suggests that hydrogen
atom transfer is at least partially rate-limiting on decay of the
μ-1,2-peroxo-Fe(III)2 complex, which leads to desaturation of
the isoleucine or valine substrate and formation of the Fe(III)2
state of the enzyme. Subsequent reduction by an external
reductant presumably regenerates the active Fe(II)2 state prior
to another round of catalysis again involving the μ-1,2-peroxo-
Fe(III)2 species upon O2 binding followed by closure of the
azetidine ring in the desaturated intermediate. This reaction
