ABSTRACT: Taxol is a potent drug used in various cancer treatments. Its complex structure has prompted extensive research into
its biosynthesis. However, certain critical steps, such as the formation of the oxetane ring, which is essential for its activity, have
remained unclear. Previous proposals suggested that oxetane formation follows the acetylation of taxadien-5α-ol. Here, we proposed
that the oxetane ring is formed by cytochrome P450-mediated oxidation events that occur prior to C5 acetylation. To test this
hypothesis, we analyzed the genomic and transcriptomic information for Taxus species to identify cytochrome P450 candidates and
employed two independent systems, yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae) and plant (Nicotiana benthamiana), for their characterization.
We revealed that a single enzyme, CYP725A4, catalyzes two successive epoxidation events, leading to the formation of the oxetane
ring. We further showed that both taxa-4(5)-11(12)-diene (endotaxadiene) and taxa-4(20)-11(12)-diene (exotaxadiene) are
precursors to the key intermediate, taxologenic oxetane, indicating the potential existence of multiple routes in the Taxol pathway.
Thus, we unveiled a long-elusive step in Taxol biosynthesis.
■INTRODUCTION
Paclitaxel, also known as Taxol, is a potent anticancer drug
currently used in the clinic for the treatment of melanoma,
ovarian, breast, bladder, prostate, and esophageal cancers.1
Taxol was initially isolated from the Pacific yew tree (Taxus
brevifolia) in 19712 and approved for use in chemotherapy by
the FDA in 1992. Although Taxol plays a vital role in current
clinical practice, its production remains expensive and
unsustainable. Presently, Taxol is semisynthesized from 10-
deacetyl-baccatin III (10-DAB)3 or directly extracted from
Taxus cell cultures,4 both of which are costly methods calling
for the development of more affordable alternatives.
Production of Taxol in heterologous microbial hosts could
reduce production costs, increase availability, and enable the
synthesis of Taxol derivatives with improved pharmacological
properties. However, incomplete knowledge of the biosyn-
thetic pathway of Taxol has hindered progress in this direction.
It has been suggested that Taxol biosynthesis involves at least
19 different steps,5 including a series of oxidation and
acetylation events on the taxadiene scaffold, followed by
successive side chain modifications at C2 and C13 positions
(Figure 1A). However, despite extensive efforts to understand
Taxol biosynthesis,6,7 several steps have remained unclear. One
key step is the formation of the characteristic oxetane ring,
which is one of the four structural features regarded to be
essential for its biological activity.8
Taxadiene synthase (TXS) initiates the Taxol pathway by
converting diterpenoid precursor geranylgeranyl diphosphate
(GGPP) to taxa-4(5),11(12)-diene.9,10 The next step is
proposed to be the hydroxylation of taxadiene to taxadien-
5α-ol. This was supported by the identification of labeled
taxanes in T. brevifolia stem discs where deuterium-labeled
taxadien-5α-ol was infused.11 This hydroxylation reaction is
catalyzed by cytochrome P450 enzyme taxadiene-5α-hydrox-

series of CYP-catalyzed oxidations were proposed to occur at
different positions of the 5α-acetyl-taxadiene skeleton,

the oxidation events that occur on the taxadiene structure, the
formation of the oxetane ring encompassing C5, C4, and C20

have been proposed for the multiple-conversion steps that lead
to the oxetane moiety in Taxol (hypothesized pathways 1−3,
Figure 1B).11,13−15 In all of these propositions, enzyme-
mediated epoxide and ester rearrangements were speculated to
be responsible for oxetane ring cyclization. To date, none of
these hypotheses have been conclusively validated either
experimentally or computationally.13,16
All of the current hypotheses consider a taxane structure
with a 5-acetyl moiety (such as 5α-acetyl-taxadiene) as the
starting point for oxetane formation. However, two observa-
tions made us question this assumption. First, two taxanes
bearing an unacetylated oxetane ring moiety, 1β-dehydroxy-
4α-deacetylbaccatin IV and 4-deacetylbaccatin IV, were
identified in two different Taxus plants (Figure 1C).17−19
Second, a study showed that a promiscuous acetyltransferase,
10β-O-acetyltransferase (10βAT), was able to catalyze the
acetylation of oxetane ring containing taxanes deacetylated at
C4 (Figure 1D).20 These findings suggest that the oxetane ring
structure can be synthesized independently in plants without
acetylation and that the 4-hydroxyl group of the oxetane
structure is amenable to acetylation by other Taxus enzymes.
Thus, we considered that acetylation of the C5 hydroxyl group
may not be essential for the formation of the oxetane ring and
that an alternative mechanism involving the oxidation of C5,
C4, or C20 may be at play (Figure 1E).
Here, we report how we tested this proposal by analyzing
the function of cytochrome P450 candidates identified in the
available genomic and transcriptomic information for Taxus
species using two independent heterologous systems, the
unicellular yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae and the model plant
Nicotiana benthamiana (tobacco). By localizing taxadiene
synthesis to the yeast peroxisome or to the cytosol of tobacco
leaf cells, we aimed to enhance substrate supply for
determining the downstream enzymes in the pathway. The
results led to the finding that a single enzyme, CYP725A4,
catalyzes two consequent epoxidation events, leading to the
formation of the oxetane ring. We further show that both taxa-
4(5)-11(12)-diene (endotaxadiene) and taxa-4(20)-11(12)-
diene (exotaxadiene) can lead to the formation of this key
intermediate in a plant system, indicating the potential
existence of multiple routes in Taxol biosynthesis. Thus, we

■RESULTS
Peroxisomal Engineering Enhances the Production
of Taxadiene in Yeast. Our main hypothesis is that oxetane
ring formation occurs prior to the acetylation of taxadiene-5α-
ol, presumably catalyzed by a second CYP interacting with the
product of CYP725A4. To test this hypothesis, we set out to
identify candidate CYPs for this second reaction by mining the
transcriptomic and genomic information available for Taxus
cuspidata, Taxus wallichiana, and Taxus chinensis.21−23 We
identified a total of 26 candidates, which included CYP genes
located in close proximity to the TXS gene in the published
genomes of T. wallichiana21 and T. chinensis22 (because it has
been shown that genes involved in the same biosynthetic
pathway in certain cases are colocalized on the same
chromosome in plants), as well as CYPs found to be
upregulated upon jasmonate treatment of T. chinensis cell
lines (because jasmonate treatment has been shown to result in
elevated levels of Taxol biosynthesis-related transcripts).23 The
identified candidates are listed in Table S1. To functionally
characterize the selected CYPs, we initially used heterologous
expression in S. cerevisiae (baker’s yeast). Yeast holds
advantages for the production of plant terpenoids such as
fast growth, eukaryotic cellular architecture, and a relative lack
of background biosynthetic reactions.24−26 The availability of
cell organelles, such as the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and
peroxisomes, not only provides a suitable environment for the
function of plant pathway enzymes but also offers possibilities
for pathway optimization and compartmentalization via
appropriate metabolic engineering strategies. Although the
production of oxidized taxanes in yeast has been achieved in
several previous studies, the amount of taxadiene-5α-ol has
been considerably low.21,22,27,28 Thus, to be able to identify
enzymes likely involved in the formation of the oxetane ring,
we first aimed to optimize taxadiene production in yeast.
We started by establishing the synthesis of taxadiene in the
yeast cytosol. TXS contains a chloroplast targeting signal at its
N-terminal part, and it has been shown that removal of this
signal augments taxadiene accumulation in yeast.28,29 There-
fore, a truncated form of TXS, lacking the first 60 amino acids,
was used in this study. Furthermore, fusion of TXS with
maltose binding protein (MBP) has been shown to enhance its
solubility in yeast, improving taxadiene production.29 Thus, we
first introduced an N-terminally truncated and MBP-fused
version of TXS, namely, t60TXS-MBP, into the chromosome
of the yeast strain AM141, which is specifically engineered for
optimized diterpenoid production.30 To ensure a sufficient
pool of the precursor GGPP, we also introduced a mutant
version of yeast farnesyldiphosphate synthase Erg20p (Erg20p-
(F96C))31 into AM141, together with a destabilizing A02-B05
hairpin at the 3′UTR of the ERG9 gene,32 to downregulate
Erg9p levels and reduce competition for isoprenoid building
blocks by the native sterol biosynthesis pathway (resulting in
strain AM144-1). AM144-1 produced 4.5 mg/L taxadiene
(Figure 2A). To further improve production, we constructed a
heterozygous deletion of ERG3 (erg3/ERG3), as this was
previously shown to improve biosynthesis of the diterpene
sclareol in yeast.33 This resulted in a 2.2-fold improvement in
taxadiene levels, achieving 9.8 mg/L (Figure 2A; strain
AM151-1). Subsequently, we introduced a heterozygous
deletion of DOS2 (dos2/DOS2), which has also been shown
to improve diterpenoid synthesis,33 but the resulting strain
(AM153-1) was less efficient in taxadiene production in
comparison to AM151-1 (Figure 2A). Finally, we introduced
two more genetic modifications that were previously reported
to enhance diterpene production in yeast, a homozygous
deletion of ROX1 (rox1/rox1) and a heterozygous deletion of
YOR292c (YOR292c/yor292c). However, these interventions
were also unable to improve taxadiene production compared
with AM151-1 (Figure 2A; strain AM158-1).
These results led us to seek an alternative strategy to further
improve taxadiene production. Peroxisomal compartmentaliza-
tion of terpene biosynthetic enzymes has been reported to
facilitate monoterpene and sesquiterpene biosynthesis in
yeast,34,35 possibly due to the metabolic channeling that is
achieved by the colocalization of pathway enzymes. Fur-
thermore, compartmentalization may avoid potential undesir-
able crosstalk between heterologous and native pathways or
reduce the accumulation of intermediates that can be toxic in
the yeast cytosol. Thus, we added a peroxisomal targeting
signal (SKL)34 to the C terminus of t60TXS-MBP and also
introduced a peroxisome-targeted Erg20p(F96C)-SKL to
enable GGPP synthesis in the peroxisome of AM158. This
approach resulted in a considerable increase in taxadiene

Biosynthesis of Dioxygenated Taxanes in Yeast.
Having established efficient taxadiene production in yeast, we
used the developed platform to study the selected CYP
candidates. For this, we introduced each of the 26 candidate
CYPs alone or in combination with T. cuspidata CYP725A4
(AY289209.2) and investigated their ability to produce taxane
compounds with a molecular mass indicative of the presence of
an oxetane ring. A cytochrome P450 reductase (CPR) from T.
cuspidata (AY571340.1) was coexpressed to provide the
necessary redox partner for the CYP function. Analysis of
the resulting yeast strains using ultraperformance liquid
chromatography combined with high-resolution mass spec-
trometry (UPLC-HRMS) revealed that three of the candidates
were able to produce compounds with a molecular signature of
305.2475 (C20H32O2), corresponding to a taxadiene molecule
bearing two hydroxyl groups or an oxetane structure. However,
all three of these CYPs catalyzed the double oxidation of the
taxadiene skeleton alone, without the need for a second CYP
to be present. Notably, we observed that CYP725A4 was able
to catalyze different double oxygenation reactions, leading to
four different products (compounds 1−4; Figure 3), indicating
that the oxidizing capacity of CYP725A4 may have been
underestimated. Furthermore, we observed that CYP725A2
was able to also produce compounds 2 and 4, while
CYP725A54 produced a different dioxygenated product,
compound 5 (Figure 3). The combination of CYP725A2
with CYP725A4 or CYP725A54 with CYP725A4 did not
produce any additional compounds with a tentative C20H32O2
formula (Figure 3).
Biosynthesis of Dioxygenated Taxanes in Tobacco.
To validate the obtained results and confirm that the observed
products were not due to an intricacy of the yeast expression
system, we sought to reconstruct the same biosynthetic steps in
a plant system, tobacco. Agrobacterium-mediated transient
expression in tobacco is an efficient and reliable tool for
studying plant biosynthetic enzymes as tobacco leaves provide
a suitable plant molecular environment for their functional
expression. This system has previously enabled the elucidation
and reconstitution of partial or complete pathways of several
plant-specialized metabolites such as strychnine,36 celastrol,24
ginkgolides,26 and limonoids.37
Recently, an engineering strategy based on rerouting GGPP
and taxadiene biosynthesis from the chloroplast to the cytosol
of tobacco resulted in up to 10-fold improvement of taxadiene
production compared to chloroplast-localized production.38
Aiming to obtain improved amounts of oxidized taxanes, we
employed a similar approach to relocalize TXS into the
tobacco cytosol by removing its N-terminal plastid signal
peptide, while coexpressing two heterologous genes previously
shown to boost GGPP precursor production: a truncated form
of the yeast 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-coenzyme A reductase
(SctHMGR) and Synechococcus sp. GGPP synthase
(SpGGPPS7).26

CYP725A4 Is a Bifunctional Enzyme that Catalyzes
Oxetane Ring Formation. As all five detected products have
the hypothesized C20H32O2 formula, our results suggested that
one of these three CYPs may be responsible for the synthesis
of the oxetane structure of Taxol. To investigate this
possibility, we set out to determine the structures of the five
dioxygenated compounds. To this end, we infiltrated 210
tobacco plants with the corresponding genes and isolated
compounds 1−5 and carried out NMR analysis of the pure
metabolites.
First, we elucidated the structure of the single product of
CYP725A54, compound 5, which was identified as 11,12-
secotaxane (5, Figure 4B). Thus, CYP725A54, whose function
was previously unknown, is likely able to attack the double
bond system between C11 and C12 by successive oxidations
that result in the opening of the ring. Subsequently, we
elucidated the structures of the two products of CYP725A2,
compounds 2 and 4. These were identified as 10-hydroxy-

Subsequently, we elucidated the structures of the multiple
oxidation products of taxadiene by CYP725A4. In addition to
2 and 4, we identified the structure of compound 1 as 18-
hydroxy-OCT (1). The location of the hydroxy group in
compound 1 was established based on the HMBC correlations
from H-18 (δH 3.37) to C-11 (δC 64.0), C-12 (δC 81.2), and
C-13 (δC 29.6) (Figure S1). All the three compounds, 1, 2, and
4, harbor a second oxidation on the previously characterized
mono-oxygenated taxanes OCT and iso-OCT, which have
been reported to be aberrant products of CYP725A4 with
taxadiene.40,41 Even though 1, 2, and 4 are unlikely to be the
intermediates of the Taxol biosynthesis pathway, their
structures support the notion that the oxidation capacity of
CYP725A4 has been substantially underestimated.
Finally, comprehensive 1D and 2D NMR analysis of the
remaining product of CYP725A4, compound 3, identified it as
4-hydroxy-5,20-epoxy-taxane (Figures 4B and S1). Notably,
compound 3, which we name here taxologenic oxetane, has the
characteristic oxetane ring structure of Taxol at C-4, C-5, and
C-20. This was established based on the HMBC correlations
from H-20 (δH 3.42, 3.36) to C-3 (δC 40.2) and the downfield-
shifted quaternary C-4 (δC 87.8) and tertiary C-5 (δC 74.6)
(Figure S1). This finding supports our initial hypothesis that
oxetane ring formation is independent of taxadiene-5α-ol
acetylation and instead proceeds via a CYP725A4-mediated
reaction involving C5, C4, and C20 of taxadiene.
Branched Biosynthesis of the Oxetane Ring from
Endo- and Exotaxadiene. TXS produces endotaxadiene (6,
Figure 5A) as the main product but also synthesizes minor
amounts (∼5%) of its isomer, exotaxadiene (7, Figure 5A).42
Previous in vitro work using microsomes isolated from yeast
expressing CYP725A4 found that both endotaxadiene (6) and
exotaxadiene (7) can lead to the formation of taxadien-5α-ol,42
suggesting that the Taxol biosynthetic pathway might be
branched. As taxologenic oxetane (3) appears to be the next
intermediate in the Taxol pathway, we also investigated the
substrates and mechanisms leading to this core structure. To
this end, we purified both taxadiene isomers from 98 tobacco
plants expressing TXS (Figure 5A), infiltrated them separately
into tobacco leaves expressing CYP725A4 and examined the
production of taxologenic oxetane (3) (Figure 5B). We found
that CYP725A4 converted either of the two substrates to 3,
indicating that the oxetane ring structure can be formed from
different routes. However, the dead-end product OCT (9), and
the dioxygenated OCT and iso-OCT analogues 1 and 4 were
only produced in endotaxadiene (6)-, but not exotaxadiene
(7)-fed tobacco leaves (Figures 5C and S5). In addition, we
also detected a new trioxygenated taxane, compound 16, in
CYP725A4-expressing tobacco leaves after feeding endotax-
adiene (6), which was subsequently identified as 5,10-
dihydroxyl-OCT by NMR, likely to be the downstream
product of 2 (Figure S6). Since OCT, iso-OCT, and their
analogues have not yet been reported to be present in the
extracts of Taxus plants,17 our tobacco experiments suggest
that exotaxadiene (7) may be the preferred substrate for the
formation of taxologenic oxetane by CYP725A4 in Taxus. This
finding is consistent with the binding parameters determined
by Jennewein and Croteau for the interaction of CYP725A4
with the two substrates,42 which revealed an almost 2-fold
preference of CYP725A4 for exotaxadiene (7) (the equilibrium
binding constant (Ks) was 3−5 μM for exotaxadiene and 5−8
μM for endotaxadiene).42 Thus, although exotaxadiene (7) is
produced in lower amounts by TXS, considering that Taxol is
only one of the numerous taxadiene skeleton-derived
molecules synthesized in Taxus plants, its favorable kinetic
parameters may make it a considerable contributor to the
biosynthesis of those taxanes that bear an oxetane structure.
By analyzing the possible biosynthetic routes, here we
suggest that the enzymatic steps leading to the oxetane
structure involve two subsequent epoxidation reactions
catalyzed by CYP725A4. For endotaxadiene (6), the first
epoxidation results in the unstable epoxide intermediate, 8,
which is subsequently converted to OCT (9) and iso-OCT
(10) via structural rearrangement, or to taxadien-5α-ol (11)
via epoxide ring-opening.43 Thus, the formation of OCT and
iso-OCT by CYP725A4 is likely due to the nonselective
breakdown of an epoxide intermediate rather than a non-
selective oxygenation event by CYP725A4 itself.41 The second
oxidation on taxadien-5α-ol (11) gives rise to intermediate 12
containing an epoxide ring involving C4 and C20 that is readily
attacked by the C5 hydroxyl group, leading to the formation of
the oxetane ring in 3. For exotaxadiene (7), we propose that
the most likely mechanism involves a similar biosynthetic
routine, leading to 3 through two epoxide intermediates (13
and 14), as shown in Figure 5D (bottom). A similar cyclization
reaction triggered by the intramolecular attack of an alcohol on
an epoxide has been reported to be responsible for the
formation of five-, six-, and eight-membered O-heterocycles in
the biosynthesis of many other natural products, such as
linalool oxides44 and bicyclomycin derivatives.45 The proposed
epoxide formation-based mechanism is supported by the
finding that both chemical epoxidation and CYP725A4-
mediated catalysis of taxadiene lead to a similar product
profile.43,46
■DISCUSSION
The pharmaceutical activity and structural complexity of Taxol
have attracted much attention for its biosynthesis. Despite the
successful production of taxadiene scaffold in various
heterologous hosts,29,47−49 and the discovery of a number of
cytochrome P450 enzymes, including 2α-,50 5α-,11,42 7β-,51
10β-,52 13α-,53 and 14β-54 hydroxylases, which are capable of
chemically modifying the taxane skeleton, progress elucidating
Taxol biosynthesis has been stalled for decades.
Since its discovery in 1996,11 CYP725A4 has long been
purported as the first functionalizing enzyme of the taxadiene

Here, we challenged the established notion that taxadien-5α-
ol acetylation is an essential step of the Taxol pathway and
investigated an alternative mechanism for oxetane ring
formation that is based on successive CYP-mediated oxidation
events. To investigate this hypothesis, we first applied
systematic engineering in two independent heterologous
biosynthesis systems, yeast and tobacco, in order to improve
taxadiene production and facilitate the study of the next
biosynthetic steps. By compartmentalizing taxadiene biosyn-
thesis in the peroxisome of yeast or relocalizing it to the
cytosol of tobacco, we were able to increase the substrate
supply considerably and facilitate the detection, isolation, and
structural characterization of downstream pathway products.
This improvement unveiled the previously unappreciated
consequent oxidation events mediated by CYP725A4, leading
to the formation of the characteristic oxetane ring structure in
Taxol. Our findings place oxetane ring formation among the
early steps of the Taxol pathway and call for reinvestigating the
activity and regiospecificity of CYP enzymes previously
identified to act on other positions of the taxane skeleton.
Moreover, our results suggest that the order of biosynthetic
steps occurring downstream of oxetane ring formation may
need to be reconsidered. Specifically, based on current
knowledge,7 it is likely that oxetane ring formation is the
first event, followed by acetylation of the hydroxy group at C4
either by TAT or by a yet unidentified acetyltransferase.
Subsequent oxidation and acetylation events then lead to the
key intermediate baccatin III. The function of enzymes
reported to catalyze these subsequent oxidation reactions has
only been established using surrogate substrates (taxusin in the
case of 2α- and 7β-hydroxylases and taxadiene-5α-ol and 5α-
acetyl-taxadiene in the case of 10β- and 13α-hydroxy-
lases)50−53 that lack the oxetane moiety. Therefore, the
activity and specificity of these enzymes for oxetane-bearing
taxanes need to be validated, complemented by searches for
new enzymes that can oxidize these substrates. While this
manuscript was under review, two cytochrome P450 enzymes
and a 2-oxoglutarate/Fe(II)-dependent dioxygenase enzyme
that catalyze the oxidation of taxusin at the 1β and 9α positions
were reported.55 In the same publication, another 2-
oxoglutarate/Fe(II)-dependent dioxygenase was reported as a
tentative 4,20-taxane epoxidase based on its ability to catalyze
the 4,20-epoxidation of taxusin.55 However, the structures of
the three products formed by this enzyme when supplied with
taxadiene could not be characterized by NMR. Furthermore, in
vitro characterization of the activity of this enzyme was not
possible because neither yeast nor bacterial expression was able
to provide an active enzyme. In addition, reconstruction of
baccatin III biosynthesis in tobacco required both CYP725A4
and the tentative 4,20-epoxidase to be present. Although
further investigation is required to decisively characterize the
activity of this enzyme, it is likely that it has a synergistic action
with CYP725A4.
Here, we show that both endo- and exotaxadiene can lead to
the formation of this key intermediate, suggesting that a
multiple, matrix-like pathway56−58 could exist in Taxol
biosynthesis. Based on the findings of previous studies showing
that exotaxadiene is a favorable substrate for CYP725A4, our
results suggest that despite being a minor product of TXS,
exotaxadiene may play a substantial contributing role in the
biosynthesis of oxetane-bearing taxanes. Furthermore, the
possibility that a yet unidentified isomerase may be involved in
the conversion of endo- to exotaxadiene cannot be excluded. In
addition, it is not unlikely that other enzymes or cofactors,
such as cytochrome b5s and membrane-steroid-binding
proteins, aid the specific biosynthetic reactions in Taxus
plants. Thus, future studies placing efforts in these directions
are also warranted. Future studies should also involve
downregulation or knockout of genes in Taxus cell cultures
to establish the exact role of each gene in the pathway and
confirm the order of biosynthetic events. Eventually,
reconstruction of the pathway in a heterologous system such
as yeast or in vitro systems using isolated enzymes can further
confirm the function of each enzyme and provide a means for
the biotechnological production of Taxol or derivatives.
The oxetane ring moiety has been observed in very few
natural products.13 Despite the fact that many of these
compounds have been reported to have remarkable bio-
activities, little is known about their biosynthesis. Here, by
showing that the oxetane ring in Taxol can be catalyzed by an
enzymatic two-step epoxidation, followed by a cyclization
reaction initiated by the intramolecular attack of a hydroxyl
group on the resulting epoxide ring, we provide a framework
for future studies of the biosynthesis of similar structures.
In conclusion, our findings shed new light on the
biosynthesis of a highly valuable and complex plant-specialized
metabolite and invite further chemical, biochemical, and
physiological studies of its biosynthesis.
