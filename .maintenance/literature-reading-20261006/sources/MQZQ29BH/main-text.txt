INTRODUCTION

    ungal  infections  represent a growing  public  health burden,  contributing  to
    significant morbidity and mortality worldwide. More than one billion individualsF
globally are estimated to be affected by fungal diseases, ranging from superficial local
infections to more severe, life-threatening invasive disease, with recent global burden
analyses estimating ~6.5 million serious invasive fungal infections annually, resulting
in ~3.8 million deaths, of which ~2.5 million are directly attributable to fungal disease
(1, 2). Within the United States, invasive fungal infections are of particular concern
due to the expansion of immunocompromised populations at elevated risk, including
neonates in intensive care units, people living with HIV/AIDS, cancer patients undergo­
ing chemotherapy, and organ transplant recipients on immunosuppressants (3, 4). This
threat is compounded by the increased prevalence of isolates resistant to the limited
arsenal of systemically acting antifungal agents clinically available (5). Currently, only
four classes of antifungals, azoles, echinocandins, polyenes, and flucytosine (a pyrimidine
analog), are approved for the treatment of invasive fungal infections, with the last
first-in-class addition to this repertoire, caspofungin, introduced over two decades ago
(6). While existing drugs demonstrate efficacy against their respective indications, they
are collectively associated with multiple limitations that compromise their ability to
address the escalating challenge posed by invasive fungal diseases.
   In 2022, the World Health Organization released the first Fungal Priority Pathogens
List (FPPL) calling for more research and drug development directed toward fungal
strains causing life-threatening invasive infections (7). The  list categorizes 19 fungal
pathogens into 3 priority groups (critical, high, and medium) based on several crite­
ria including their infection-associated mortality, incidence, and levels of antifungal
resistance. The critical priority group includes four species that collectively contribute
to over one million deaths annually: Candida albicans, Candidozyma auris, Aspergillus
fumigatus, and Cryptococcus neoformans (7, 8). C. albicans  is a commensal microbe
regularly present in human mucosa but can become pathogenic and cause invasive
infection in critically ill or immunocompromised individuals. In this population, mortality
rates for invasive candidiasis caused by C. albicans range from 20% to 50% despite
the availability of antifungal treatments (9). C. auris infection can also result in life-threat­
ening invasive candidiasis affecting vulnerable patient populations, with an associated
mortality rate of 30%–72% (10, 11). C. auris has attracted increased clinical attention
because of its high outbreak potential and intrinsic resistance to most clinically available
antifungals, resulting in limited treatment options (12, 13). A. fumigatus is a ubiqui­
tous environmental mold that can lead to invasive aspergillosis, primarily affecting the
respiratory system of immunocompromised hosts. The rise of drug-resistant A. fumigatus
infections is particularly concerning, with mortality rates reported between 47% and
88% (7, 14, 15). Cryptococcosis carries a high mortality rate of 41%–61% for patients with
HIV infection and 8%–20% among HIV-negative individuals, exacerbated by diagnostic
challenges and limited access to effective therapies in low-resource settings (16, 17).
C. neoformans species complex is a soil fungal pathogen that causes life-threatening
meningoencephalitis following inhalation, especially in those with HIV/AIDS (18). In
contrast, the Cryptococcus gattii species complex (CGSC), which includes five pathogenic
species (19), exhibits distinct host predilections, with C. bacillisporus, C. decagattii, and
C. tetragattii predominantly infecting HIV-positive or immunocompromised individuals,

whereas C. gattii sensu stricto and C. deuterogattii are more frequently associated with

   In response to the threat posed by these pathogens, the WHO recommends a
dual-pronged approach to future antifungal development focused on (i) the discovery
of innovative fungal targets leading to first-in-class drug candidates showing reduced
cross-resistance to existing antifungals and (ii) the improvement of existing agents in
ways that enhance their efficacy, reduce their toxicity, and lower the risk of developing
resistance (7). Despite the formidable challenges associated with meeting these goals,
the past decade has seen the emergence of several innovative strategies leading to the
discovery of promising antifungal drug candidates in various phases of development
(21). This review sheds light on one such strategy presently in preclinical stages, focused
on targeting pantothenate utilization and Coenzyme A biosynthesis pathway, and the
downstream biosynthesis of Acetyl-CoA in fungi (hereafter referred to as PCA pathway)
(22).
  Coenzyme A (CoA), a metabolic cofactor present in all living organisms, is synthesized
from pantothenic acid (vitamin B5) and plays an essential role in numerous cellular
processes, including glycolysis, fatty acid metabolism, and the tricarboxylic acid (TCA)
cycle (23). Early studies in yeast revealed that the enzymes responsible for the synthesis
of CoA from pantothenic acid are indispensable for fungal viability and display key
structural and functional differences from their human counterparts (24–26). Pantothen­
ate kinase (PanK), which catalyzes the first and rate-limiting step of CoA biosynthesis, has
emerged as a highly validated antimicrobial target. Genetic ablation of CAB1, the gene
encoding PanK, is lethal in yeast, and pharmacological studies by Chiu and colleagues
showed that inhibition of Cab1 with the pantothenate analog α-PanAm effectively
suppresses fungal growth, providing the first direct evidence that CoA biosynthesis is
druggable for antifungal therapy (25). In 2022, the first high-resolution crystal structure
of a fungal PanK enzyme was resolved, offering a valuable tool for structure-guided
discovery of additional kinase inhibitors (26). Subsequent investigations established that
disruption of CoA metabolism downstream of PanK sensitizes yeast to existing antifungal
drugs, offering a unique strategy to enhance antifungal efficacy, mitigate resistance, and
potentially reduce dose-limiting toxicity (25, 27). Together, these findings position CoA
biosynthesis as an attractive target for next-generation antifungal drug development.

CURRENT ANTIFUNGAL THERAPIES AND ASSOCIATED CHALLENGES

Currently, only four major classes of antifungal drugs are clinically approved for the
treatment of systemic invasive fungal infections: polyenes, pyrimidine analogs, azoles,
and echinocandins (Fig. 1). Although these agents are effective within their respective
indications, they are collectively associated with multiple limitations that undermine
their capacity to fully combat the increasing burden posed by invasive fungal diseases
(Table 1) (28).

Polyenes

Polyenes were the first class of antifungals to be clinically introduced, beginning with
the approval of the polyene amphotericin B deoxycholate in the 1950s. Amphoteri­
cin B is notable for its high fungicidal potency and broad-spectrum activity against
several classes of fungal pathogens, including resistant strains of Aspergillus and Candida
(29, 30). These properties arise from its ability to directly bind and sequester ergo­
sterol, a major lipid component of fungal cell membranes that is essential for main­
taining membrane fluidity and integrity (31). This interaction promotes the formation
of extramembranous aggregates that extract ergosterol from the membrane, disrupt­
ing membrane integrity and function (Fig. 1) (31). Ergosterol complexation perturbs
membrane organization and triggers oxidative and metabolic stress, further amplifying
its fungicidal effects (32–34). Owing to this multifaceted, sterol-targeted mechanism,
amphotericin B remains the most potent and broad-spectrum antifungal agent in clinical
use, with acquired resistance remaining exceedingly rare (35).

   Despite its high potency, clinical use of amphotericin B has been largely limited
to severe systemic infections (such as cryptococcal meningitis) due to its associated
host  toxicity  (28). This  toxicity most often manifests as renal dysfunction arising
from interactions between amphotericin B and cholesterol, the human counterpart of
ergosterol that is enriched in the plasma membranes of kidney cells (36). In the 1990s,
the development and clinical approval of lipid formulations (e.g., liposomal amphotericin
B, AmBisome) significantly reduced the nephrotoxicity associated with amphotericin B
treatment without altering its fungicidal activity (28). Nonetheless, more widespread
use of amphotericin B remains limited by its requirement for intravenous administration
and the existence of intrinsically resistant fungal strains with low ergosterol content
(37). Several efforts during the past decade have focused on addressing these limita­
tions through the discovery of new polyenes or amphotericin B derivatives for treating
systemic fungal infection (38). This has resulted in a number of such compounds in
various phases of development that exhibit reduced host toxicity, greater solubility
(enabling oral administration), and an ability to target multiple fungal membrane lipids
to combat primary and acquired resistance (39–41).

Pyrimidine analogs

Following amphotericin B, flucytosine was the next first-in-class antifungal drug to
be clinically approved in the 1970s. Flucytosine  is a pyrimidine analog of cytosine
that is converted by fungal cytosine deaminase to the toxic compound 5-fluorouracil.
5-Fluorouracil competes with uracil for incorporation into nascent RNA, leading to a
direct disruption of global RNA synthesis and indirect impairment of protein synthe­
sis (Fig. 1) (42). 5-Fluorouracil can further be metabolized into 5-fluorodeoxyuridine
monophosphate (FdUMP), which potently inhibits thymidylate synthase, a key enzyme
in DNA synthesis (Fig. 1) (42). Notably, since the human genome does not encode a
cytosine deaminase capable of converting flucytosine, the drug’s toxic effects are largely
restricted to fungi when it is administered at a pharmacological dose (43). However, the

Azoles

Azoles emerged as the next major class of antifungals in the 1980s and quickly became
first-line treatments for fungal infections due to their improved safety and oral availabil­
ity (45, 46). The antifungal activity of azoles is primarily mediated by direct inhibition
of lanosterol 14-α-demethylase (Erg11), a fungal enzyme responsible for the conversion
of lanosterol to ergosterol (Fig. 1) (47). By inhibiting Erg11, azoles effectively disrupt
ergosterol synthesis, resulting in accumulation of toxic sterol intermediates, ergosterol
depletion, and loss of membrane integrity. Successive rounds of drug development have
yielded newer-generation azoles marked by expanded antifungal spectra, improved oral
availability, and reduced host toxicity (48). These advancements have allowed azoles
to be used not only for therapeutic treatment in the general population but also for
prophylactic purposes in immunocompromised patients (49).
   The major limitations of azoles are that all (except voriconazole) exhibit fungistatic
rather than fungicidal activity against Candida and Cryptococcus species, increasing the
risk of acquired resistance from long-term use (50). Moreover, the overuse of azoles in
agricultural settings as pesticides has contributed to the emergence of azole-resistant
strains of C. auris and A. fumigatus (5). Investigations into the genetic underpinnings of
azole resistance have revealed various mutations in the ERG11 gene that increase its
stability or expression as key causal factors (47, 51). Use of azoles is also limited by their
high risk of drug–drug interactions mediated by effects on cytochrome P450 enzymes,
which can preclude their use in patients receiving certain medications (52).

Echinocandins

Introduced in the 2000s, echinocandins represent the  last major class of antifun­
gal agents to be approved. Clinically available echinocandins include caspofungin,
micafungin, and anidulafungin, with the more recent addition of rezafungin in 2023
(53, 54). These antifungals inhibit β-1,3-D-glucan synthase, a fungal glucosyltransfer­
ase encoded by the FKS1 and FKS2 genes, that is essential for the synthesis of the

polysaccharide β-glucan, a key structural component of the fungal cell wall (Fig. 1) (53).
Because the cell wall is a structure absent in human cells, echinocandins notably exhibit

   Since  their  approval, echinocandins have  gradually become a cornerstone  of
antifungal therapy due to their fungicidal activity against azole-resistant fungi, including
most Candida and Aspergillus species (55). Despite success in this context, clinical cases
of echinocandin resistance have been steadily rising, particularly among certain Candida
species where acquired mutations in the FKS1 and FKS2 genes prevent drug binding (56).
Other limitations of echinocandins include their lack of efficacy against Mucormycetes,
Cryptococcus spp., or Fusarium species (57, 58) and the requirement for intravenous
administration (53).

LIMITATIONS OF CLINICALLY APPROVED ANTIFUNGAL AGENTS

Despite the availability of these four approved drug classes, mortality rates range from
20% to 90% for invasive Candida, C. auris, Cryptococcus, and Aspergillus infections,
highlighting the urgent need for a new generation of systemically active drugs (7). One
of the most significant limitations of current antifungal therapies is their narrow target
profile, as nearly all act to disrupt ergosterol levels in the cell membrane, β-glucan levels
in the cell wall or nucleic acid metabolism. This paucity of targets is concerning due to
the high risk of cross-resistance associated with drugs that act on the same pathways
(59). However, new drug targets have proven difficult to identify because fungi are
eukaryotic organisms that share many molecular components with humans. As a result,
many candidate compounds that demonstrate promising antifungal activity in vitro have
failed to be clinically viable in vivo due to associated host toxicity (60).
   Beyond the need for drugs with novel targets and favorable safety profiles, there is
also a growing emphasis on new drugs exhibiting more rapid fungicidal activity (28).
Many of the most widely used antifungals are only fungistatic, inhibiting fungal growth
but ultimately relying on the immune system to clear the infection (61). This limitation
is of special concern in immunocompromised patients with weak immune systems, a
population that comprises a growing proportion of those with invasive systemic mycoses
in both the developed and developing worlds (62).
   Furthermore, the overuse of a limited set of antifungals has contributed to the
emergence of multidrug-resistant fungal strains, such as C. auris and A. fumigatus (12,
63). Genetic studies of these resistant strains have revealed mutations in genes that
regulate xenobiotic efflux and increase drug detoxification capacity, as well as mutations
in mismatch repair genes (such as MSH1) that facilitate the accumulation of additional
pro-survival mutations (28, 47, 64). These findings highlight the need for antifungal
strategies that target resistance-associated pathways to increase the efficacy and durable
lifetime of both existing and future therapies.

EMERGING ANTIFUNGAL AGENTS IN DEVELOPMENT

Over the past decade, extensive research efforts have yielded several new antifungal
agents aimed at overcoming the limitations of existing therapies. Some of these
compounds, including ibrexafungerp (approved in 2021), oteseconazole (2022), and
rezafungin (2023), have received FDA approval, while others such as fosmanogepix,
olorofim, and ATI-2307 are advancing through clinical evaluation (65). These new agents
include both next-generation derivatives that improve the pharmacological and safety
profiles of established drug classes, as well as entirely new chemical entities that act
through previously unexploited molecular mechanisms (65, 66). Below, we summarize
key examples of these emerging antifungal agents currently in clinical or advanced
preclinical development.

Cell wall-targeting agents

Rezafungin and ibrexafungerp represent next-generation glucan synthase inhibitors that
build upon the echinocandin scaffold. Rezafungin, a long-acting echinocandin with a

half-life exceeding 100 h, enables once-weekly intravenous dosing and exhibits potent
activity against Candida spp. as well as C. auris (67). Ibrexafungerp, a triterpenoid
β-1,3-D-glucan synthase inhibitor, retains efficacy against echinocandin-resistant isolates
and is orally bioavailable, positioning it as the first non-echinocandin glucan synthase
inhibitor approved for systemic use (68). Both represent significant pharmacokinetic and
spectrum improvements over older agents (67).

GPI anchor biosynthesis inhibitors

Fosmanogepix (APX001), a first-in-class Gwt1 enzyme inhibitor, targets the glycosylphos­
phatidylinositol (GPI) anchor biosynthesis pathway, which is essential for fungal cell
wall integrity and virulence (69, 70). By disrupting anchoring of mannoproteins to the
cell surface, Fosmanogepix exerts broad fungicidal activity against Candida spp., C.
auris, Aspergillus, and some Mucorales, with limited cross-resistance relative to azoles
or echinocandins. Its high oral bioavailability (>90%) and CNS penetration make it a
strong candidate for treating disseminated and CNS infections (71).

Dihydroorotate dehydrogenase inhibitors

Olorofim (F901318) inhibits fungal dihydroorotate dehydrogenase in the pyrimidine
biosynthesis pathway  (72).  It displays potent  activity against Aspergillus spp. and
difficult molds (Lomentospora, Scedosporium) though it lacks efficacy against Candida
and Mucorales (73). As the first representative of this mechanism, olorofim provides a
complementary option for mold infections that are resistant to azoles and polyenes.

Novel azoles and polyenes

Oteseconazole (VT-1161) and opelconazole (PC945) exemplify improved azole deriv­
atives optimized for safety and tissue distribution (74, 75). Oteseconazole, recently
FDA-approved for recurrent vulvovaginal candidiasis, shows enhanced selectivity for
fungal Erg11p and minimal cytochrome P450 inhibition  (74,  76). Opelconazole  is
formulated for inhalation, enabling localized delivery against pulmonary Aspergillus
infection (75). In parallel, oral formulations of amphotericin B (CAmB) aim to overcome
the toxicity and IV dependence of polyene therapy (77).

Mitochondrial respiration inhibitors

ATI-2307 disrupts fungal mitochondrial function, resulting in loss of mitochondrial
membrane potential and inhibition of ATP synthesis (78). Though preclinical, it demon­
strates potent activity against Candida spp. and select molds (79).
   Currently approved and investigational antifungal agents, including those highligh­
ted in this review, target well-defined pathways involved in cell wall synthesis, ergosterol
biosynthesis, nucleotide metabolism, or mitochondrial respiration (65, 66). In contrast,
the coenzyme A (CoA) metabolic pathway has only recently been identified as a potential
antifungal target, opening an unexplored avenue for therapeutic development (25–
27, 80). This pathway represents a distinct metabolic vulnerability central to CoA and
acetyl-CoA biosynthesis. Targeting this pathway, therefore, offers a fundamentally new
strategy for antifungal drug development and a promising approach to address the
growing challenge of drug-resistant fungal infections. In this review, we highlight CoA
metabolism as a promising antifungal target and discuss its potential to overcome
current therapeutic limitations.

COENZYME A METABOLISM IN FUNGI
The universal CoA biosynthesis pathway

CoA is a metabolic cofactor present in all living organisms, where it plays an essential
role in cellular metabolism by facilitating diverse biochemical processes (23). The diverse
products of CoA metabolism play a direct role in several biological processes, including

June 2026 Volume 39   Issue 2                                                                                                      10.1128/cmr.00307-25 7


[PDF physical page 8]
Review                                                                                                                                                      Clinical Microbiology Reviews


cell growth, cell death, signal transduction, protein modification, and regulation of gene
expression (23). CoA participates in protein CoAlation, covalently modifying cysteine
residues to protect against oxidative stress and modulate enzyme activity (81). CoA
also acts as an allosteric regulator of metabolic enzymes and maintains metabolic
homeostasis by balancing free vs acylated CoA pools across cellular compartments
(23). Through  its reactive thiol group, CoA forms thioesters with a variety of acyl
groups, generating derivatives with specialized functions: malonyl-CoA drives fatty acid
synthesis, succinyl-CoA links carbon metabolism to the TCA cycle and heme biosynthesis,
and fatty acyl-CoAs provide substrates for lipid assembly and fatty acid β-oxidation.
Among these, acetyl-CoA represents the most central and versatile intermediate.  It
donates two-carbon units for ergosterol and fatty acid biosynthesis, provides the acetyl
donor for protein acetylation and epigenetic regulation, fuels energy production via
TCA cycle, and contributes vacuolar function and organelle integrity (Fig. 2). By linking
nutrient availability with biosynthetic output and stress adaptation, acetyl-CoA functions
as a major regulator of fungal growth and virulence (22, 82).
   Given the fundamental role of CoA and acetyl-CoA in cellular metabolism,  it  is
not surprising that its biosynthetic pathway is conserved across organisms including
bacteria, archaea, and eukaryotes, reflecting the universal requirement for CoA in central
metabolism (Fig. 3) (22, 83–85). The universal precursor for CoA biosynthesis is panto­
thenic acid (vitamin B5). In most bacteria and eukaryotes, pantothenic acid is phosphory­
lated by pantothenate kinase (PanK) to form 4′-phosphopantothenate (PPan). In contrast,
many archaea and some symbiotic bacteria bypass pantothenate and instead generate
PPan via an alternative route in which pantoate is first phosphorylated to 4-phosphopan­
toate and subsequently converted to PPan (84). In the second step of CoA biosynthesis,
phosphopantothenoylcysteine synthetase (PPCS) catalyzes the condensation of PPan
with cysteine, yielding 4′-phosphopantothenoylcysteine (PPan-Cys). The cysteine moiety
of this intermediate is then decarboxylated by phosphopantothenoylcysteine decarbox­
ylase (PPCDC), producing 4′-phosphopantetheine (PPanSH). Next, phosphopantetheine
adenylyltransferase (PPAT) transfers an adenylyl group to PPanSH, producing dephos­
pho-CoA. Finally, dephospho-CoA kinase (DPCK) phosphorylates the 3′-hydroxyl of the
ribose in dephospho-CoA to form the fully functional CoA molecule (Fig. 3). While the
core architecture of CoA biosynthesis is conserved, the individual enzymes and their
modes of regulation vary substantially between microorganisms and the host, providing
opportunities for selective antimicrobial targeting (22).

The salvage and de novo synthesis pathways for pantothenate

Although the CoA biosynthetic pathway is generally conserved across eukaryotes, key
differences exist between fungi and humans that have informed drug development
efforts. Most notably, while both humans and fungi can salvage pantothenic acid from
their extracellular environment via transporter-mediated uptake, fungi also possess
the unique ability to synthesize pantothenic acid de novo via a noncanonical route
that generates β-alanine from polyamine catabolism (Fig. 3) (86, 87). Instead of using
aspartate decarboxylase (as in many bacteria), yeast degrades the polyamine spermine
through the action of an amine oxidase (encoded by FMS1 in S. cerevisiae), produc­
ing spermidine and 3-aminopropanal (86, 88); the latter is subsequently oxidized to
β-alanine by aldehyde dehydrogenases (encoded by ALD2 and ALD3 in S. cerevisiae)
(87). The produced β-alanine is subsequently condensed with pantoate by pantothenate
synthase (encoded by PAN6 in S. cerevisiae) to form pantothenic acid, which then feeds
into CoA biosynthesis. Because  all four enzymes of the polyamine pathway (orni­
thine decarboxylase, S-adenosylmethionine decarboxylase, spermidine synthase, and
spermine synthase encoded by SPE1, SPE2, SPE3, and SPE4, respectively, in S. cerevisiae)
are required to generate spermine, perturbation of polyamine metabolism disrupts
β-alanine, pantothenate, and ultimately CoA production. In yeast, the pantothenate
salvage pathway specifically refers to the transporter-mediated uptake of extracellular

Pantothenate phosphorylation in fungi

The first step of CoA biosynthesis, phosphorylation of pantothenate by PanK, has been
extensively studied to reveal key differences between fungi and humans that present
opportunities for antifungal drug targeting (26, 90). Genome analyses and functional
studies in Saccharomyces cerevisiae demonstrated that yeast cells express a single PANK
(CAB1) gene encoding PanK, and that this gene is essential for viability (91). In con­
trast, the human genome encodes three PANK genes, PANK1, PANK2, and PANK3, which
produce enzymatically active PanK isoforms that differ primarily in their subcellular
localization and tissue-specific distribution (85, 92). Similar to yeast PanK, human PanK1
and PanK3 are cytosolic and presumed essential for life (no individuals with deleterious
mutations in PANK1 or PANK3 have been observed). Human PanK2 is uniquely localized
to the mitochondrial intermembrane space, and several pathogenic variants, including
loss-of-function and hypomorphic mutations, in the PANK2 gene are associated with
the progressive neurodegenerative disorder known as PKAN (Pantothenate Kinase-Asso­
ciated Neurodegeneration) (93). These differences suggest that humans have built-in
redundancy in the CoA biosynthesis pathway through encoding multiple PanK isoforms,
whereas fungi rely on a single, essential PanK enzyme, creating an ideal opportunity for
selective drug targeting.
   Similar patterns hold across other pathogenic  fungi. For example,  C. albicans,
Nakaseomyces glabrata, C. auris, A. fumigatus, C. neoformans, and Histoplasma capsulatum
each harbor a single-copy PanK gene (26, 80). However, genetic validation of PanK
essentiality in fungal pathogens has thus far been demonstrated only in C. albicans and
A. fumigatus (26, 80). This combination of limited redundancy and emerging genetic
validation underscores the broad potential of PanK as an antifungal target.
   The most well-characterized fungal PanK  is encoded by the CAB1 (Coenzyme A
Biosynthesis) gene in S. cerevisiae. Since CAB1 deletion is lethal, its function has largely
been studied through the expression of hypomorphic variants in yeast (94). The first
hypomorphic cab1 mutant was discovered in a chemical mutagenesis screen designed
to identify mutant strains with defects in fatty acid biosynthesis (24, 95). This yielded a
temperature-sensitive strain (cab1ts), inviable at 37°C but viable at lower temperatures
(24, 95). Sequencing and gene complementation studies revealed that the thermosen­
sitive phenotype in the cab1ts mutant was caused by a glycine-to-serine mutation at
amino acid position 351 (G351S) in CAB1 (24, 96). Biochemical characterization of this
mutant revealed that it retained less than 10% of wild-type PanK activity and exhibited
a growth defect on non-fermentable carbon sources (an indicator of impaired mitochon­
drial function) (26, 96).
  More recently, Gihaz et al. (26) reported the first crystal structure of a fungal PanK
enzyme (that of S. cerevisiae Cab1p) at a resolution of 1.8 Å (26). Structural analysis
revealed that, like human PanKs, Cab1p is a homodimer with each monomer comprised
of a parallel β-sheet flanked by α-helices to form an α/β Rossmann-like fold typical
of kinases  (Fig. 4A and B) (26, 97, 98). The enzyme’s active site  is buried at the
interface between the two monomers, where pantothenate and ATP bind to distinct
but adjacent pockets lined by catalytic residues Asp24, Glu105, Ser158, and Tyr220,
corresponding to Asp45, Glu138, Ser189, and Tyr254 in human PanK3 (Fig. 4C) (26, 90).
These residues define a conserved catalytic geometry essential for efficient phosphate
transfer. Although the catalytic cores are nearly identical, the surrounding nucleotide-
and pantothenate-binding pockets differ significantly in amino-acid composition and
physicochemical character (26). Comparative sequence analysis of Cab1p and human

PANTOTHENATE KINASE AS A TARGET FOR ANTIFUNGAL DRUG DEVELOP­
MENT

The CoA biosynthesis pathway remains an underexplored yet attractive pathway for
antifungal drug development due to its essential role in fungal metabolism and the
key differences between human and fungal enzymes. To date, most research aimed at
identifying novel drug candidates targeting CoA biosynthesis has focused on developing
small-molecule inhibitors of fungal PanK (26, 80).

Pantothenate analogs

Given the essential role of enzymes involved in pantothenate utilization in the viability
and pathogenicity of several human pathogens, structural analogs of pantothenate have
long been explored as potential antimicrobial agents (102, 103). Pantothenate analogs

as a particularly potent and stable pantothenate analog with broad antimicrobial
potential (Fig. 6) (104, 105). The antimicrobial activity of α-PanAm was first demonstrated
in the human malaria parasite Plasmodium falciparum (104). P. falciparum (as well as
several other intraerythrocytic parasites) encodes two PanK homologs, PanK1 and PanK2,
with PanK1 harboring a bona fide catalytic “DIIG” motif not found in PanK2 (106). Genetic
studies in P. yoelii indicated that the expression of both proteins is required for survival
and suggested that the two proteins might form a heterodimer (106). With the exception
of one report (which showed activity of PanK1 in vitro, although the measured Km was
roughly two orders of magnitude greater than physiologically relevant levels) (107),
efforts by several research teams have failed to demonstrate activity of the two parasite
enzymes in vitro using recombinant proteins expressed in E. coli or insect cells (96,
108, 109). Cell-based analyses in P. falciparum employing transgenic parasites expressing
tagged PanK1 and PanK2 provided evidence that the two enzymes form a heterodimer
and that pantothenate phosphorylation activity is observed exclusively in the assembled
complex following purification from parasite extracts (110).
   In addition to competitively inhibiting pantothenate binding to the PfPanK com­
plex, de  Villiers et  al. (111) showed that α-PanAm  is phosphorylated by PfPanK,
resulting in reduced production of phosphopantothenate (111). Moreover, the phos­
phorylated α-PanAm intermediate is further processed by downstream enzymes in the
CoA biosynthetic pathway, ultimately giving rise to nonfunctional CoA analogs. These
nonfunctional CoA derivatives are believed to interfere with CoA-dependent processes
critical to survival, such as fatty acid metabolism and the TCA cycle (111).
    Parallel investigations into the effect of α-PanAm on fungi by Chiu et al. (96) showed
that treatment of S. cerevisiae resulted in a dose-dependent inhibition of yeast growth
(96). The MIC₅₀ of α-PanAm increased significantly when yeast was supplemented with
higher concentrations of pantothenate, suggesting that its inhibitory effect is due to
interference with pantothenate utilization (possibly through inhibition of Cab1p activity).
Consistent with this finding, the hypomorphic cab1G351S mutant exhibited dramatically
higher sensitivity to α-PanAm compared to wild-type yeast (96). Enzyme kinetic and
mass spectrometric studies confirmed that α-PanAm directly competes with pantothen­
ate for phosphorylation by Cab1p (96). Although most efforts currently underway to
develop pantothenamides as antimicrobials are focused on malaria (104, 105, 111), these
findings provide strong evidence to support their development to treat fungal infections.
To our knowledge, the antifungal activity of other pantothenamide derivatives currently
in development as antimalarial drugs has not yet been reported.

TARGETING CoA METABOLISM TO MODULATE SUSCEPTIBILITY TO EXISTING
ANTIFUNGALS

Building on efforts to directly inhibit CoA biosynthesis, broader investigations into
fungal CoA metabolism have highlighted its potential as a modulator of drug sensitivity.
There is growing evidence that perturbation of CoA metabolism, either through genetic
or pharmacological means, can significantly enhance yeast susceptibility to clinically
approved antifungals that target ergosterol biosynthesis (27). By modulating fungal
susceptibility, drugs targeting CoA metabolism represent promising tools for enhanc­
ing the potency of existing antifungal agents and combating the emerging threat of
multidrug resistance.

Pantothenate utilization as a modulator of ergosterol biosynthesis

Evidence of a link between CoA metabolism and drug susceptibility  first emerged
from early genetic screens that discovered the fungal FEN2 gene (encoding a pan­
tothenate transporter) in the context of antifungal resistance (89). FEN2 was so named
because loss-of-function mutations in this gene conferred resistance to fenpropimorph,
a morpholine-derived antifungal that inhibits sterol-14-reductase and sterol-8,7-isomer­
ase, key enzymes in the ergosterol synthesis pathway (115). Further characterization of
fen2 mutant yeast revealed decreased intracellular pantothenate and reduced ergo­
sterol levels (89, 115). The mechanistic link between CoA biosynthesis and ergosterol
biosynthesis was hypothesized to be mediated by CoA derivatives, specifically acetyl-
CoA (the initial substrate of the ergosterol synthesis pathway) (89). In this context,
sensitivity to fenpropimorph was hypothesized to depend on the intracellular availability
of pantothenate and CoA to form the relevant CoA thioesters involved in fungal lipid
metabolism. This suggests that interfering with pantothenate uptake or CoA biosynthe­
sis can indirectly modulate the efficacy of antifungal drugs targeting sterol metabolism.
   Such early studies laid the groundwork for later investigations more directly exploring
the role of CoA metabolism in modulating susceptibility to ergosterol-targeting drugs.
Chiu et al. (25) identified PanK activity as a key regulator of ergosterol biosynthesis
and antifungal susceptibility in S. cerevisiae (25). They observed that yeast expressing

Vacuolar and mitochondrial function as key mediators of drug susceptibility

The precise mechanism by which reduced CoA biosynthesis activity leads to increased
drug susceptibility has only recently been elucidated. Recent studies showed that
yeast expressing various hypomorphic cab1 variants (cab1G351S, cab1N290I, or cab1S158A)
displayed enhanced susceptibility not only to antifungals targeting ergosterol biosyn­
thesis (amphotericin  B, terbinafine, fluconazole) but also to compounds targeting
unrelated pathways (caspofungin, hygromycin B, cycloheximide) and to metal stress
(excess copper and iron) (27). Mechanistic analyses revealed that, compared to their
wild-type counterparts, these PanK-deficient yeast mutants had enlarged vacuoles,
diminished vacuolar detoxification capacity, abnormal mitochondrial morphology, and
elevated levels of reactive oxygen species (27). Taken together, these findings support a
model whereby defective CoA biosynthesis leads to altered vacuolar detoxification and
mitochondrial function, which, in turn, enhances fungal sensitivity to a wide range of
agents (Fig. 7). Further evidence for this model came from experiments using a yeast
mutant strain expressing the acetyl-CoA synthetase ACS2 under a tet-off (doxycycline-
repressible) promoter, as well as wild-type yeast treated with the pantazine PZ-2891
or the celecoxib derivative AR-12, which were found to inhibit acetyl-CoA synthetase
(27). These enzymes function immediately downstream of CoA biosynthesis to convert
CoA into acetyl-CoA (Fig. 7). In these studies, reduced expression or inhibition of ACS
activity led to increased yeast sensitivity to multiple clinically approved antifungals (27).
These results suggest that disruption of acetyl-CoA production alone is sufficient to
confer broad-spectrum antifungal susceptibility. Consistent with these findings, PZ-2891
significantly potentiated the efficacy of amphotericin B, caspofungin, and terbinafine in
C. albicans and of caspofungin in A. fumigatus (27). Since acetyl-CoA acts as a central
metabolic node essential for multiple processes (including ergosterol biosynthesis and
membrane maintenance), we propose that defective acetyl-CoA production may be the
key mediator linking CoA metabolism to vacuolar and mitochondrial dysfunction that
ultimately drives increased drug susceptibility.
   Collectively, these findings suggest that drug candidates targeting CoA metabolism
and acetyl-CoA biosynthesis (the PCA pathway) could serve as potentiators of clinically
approved antifungals when used in combination. By increasing fungal susceptibility,
such candidates may improve the efficacy and safety profiles of existing antifungals by
reducing the dosage or treatment duration necessary to eliminate infection. Further­
more, since increased drug detoxification is a well-established mechanism underlying
multidrug resistance, the ability of CoA-targeting drugs to impair vacuolar function
suggests that they may help counteract the development of antifungal resistance (47).

FUTURE DIRECTIONS AND CONCLUSION

Current therapies for invasive fungal infections face significant limitations, including
a narrow range of drug targets, suboptimal fungicidal activity, and risk of host toxic­
ity. Although there is an urgent need for novel antifungal agents, drug development
has been challenged by the high degree of conservation between fungal and human
biology as well as the increasing prevalence of multidrug-resistant yeast strains. Taken
together, these considerations position the CoA biosynthesis pathway as a compelling
drug target due to its essential role in fungal metabolism and the presence of key
differences between yeast and human enzymatic machinery (22). Considerable progress
has been made in targeting the pathway’s first step, pantothenate phosphorylation
by PanK. Structural analogs of pantothenate and non-substrate inhibitors such as
pyrimidinone-triazoles that compete for the PanK substrate binding sites have demon­
strated particularly promising antifungal effects (26, 96). Moreover, general disruption
of CoA metabolism has been shown to sensitize fungi to a wide range of clinically
approved antifungals and other xenobiotics, underscoring the potential for CoA-target­
ing strategies to enhance the therapeutic efficacy and safety of existing treatments
while also mitigating the development of resistance (25, 27). Moving forward, medic­
inal chemistry optimization will be essential to address pharmacological challenges,
improving compound potency, oral bioavailability, and activity against clinically relevant
wild-type fungal strains where efflux and nutrient competition reduce drug effective
ness, while minimizing host  off-target  effects. Whether a CoA targeting inhibitor
advances as a standalone drug or as a potentiator will depend on the extent of
enzyme inhibition that can be achieved. Near-complete inhibition would be required
for standalone efficacy, whereas compounds that do not fully block enzyme function
can still serve effectively as potentiators to restore susceptibility to existing antifungal
agents. Early pharmacokinetic and toxicity studies will be critical for identifying which

The application of genetic engineering and advanced genomic tools will be critical
for dissecting the functional significance of these enzymes within fungal physiology,
helping to uncover the precise mechanisms by which compounds against these targets
exert their antifungal  effects.  Collectively, such  efforts  will pave the way  for the
development of next-generation antifungal agents equipped to combat the growing
public health burden posed by invasive fungal infections.
