   In response to the threat posed by these pathogens, the WHO recommends a
dual-pronged approach to future antifungal development focused on (i) the discovery
of innovative fungal targets leading to first-in-class drug candidates showing reduced
cross-resistance to existing antifungals and (ii) the improvement of existing agents in
ways that enhance their efficacy, reduce their toxicity, and lower the risk of developing
resistance (7). Despite the formidable challenges associated with meeting these goals,
the past decade has seen the emergence of several innovative strategies leading to the
discovery of promising antifungal drug candidates in various phases of development
(21). This review sheds light on one such strategy presently in preclinical stages, focused
on targeting pantothenate utilization and Coenzyme A biosynthesis pathway, and the
downstream biosynthesis of Acetyl-CoA in fungi (hereafter referred to as PCA pathway)
(22).
  Coenzyme A (CoA), a metabolic cofactor present in all living organisms, is synthesized
from pantothenic acid (vitamin B5) and plays an essential role in numerous cellular
processes, including glycolysis, fatty acid metabolism, and the tricarboxylic acid (TCA)
cycle (23). Early studies in yeast revealed that the enzymes responsible for the synthesis
of CoA from pantothenic acid are indispensable for fungal viability and display key
structural and functional differences from their human counterparts (24–26). Pantothen­
ate kinase (PanK), which catalyzes the first and rate-limiting step of CoA biosynthesis, has
emerged as a highly validated antimicrobial target. Genetic ablation of CAB1, the gene
encoding PanK, is lethal in yeast, and pharmacological studies by Chiu and colleagues
showed that inhibition of Cab1 with the pantothenate analog α-PanAm effectively
suppresses fungal growth, providing the first direct evidence that CoA biosynthesis is
druggable for antifungal therapy (25). In 2022, the first high-resolution crystal structure
of a fungal PanK enzyme was resolved, offering a valuable tool for structure-guided
discovery of additional kinase inhibitors (26). Subsequent investigations established that
disruption of CoA metabolism downstream of PanK sensitizes yeast to existing antifungal
drugs, offering a unique strategy to enhance antifungal efficacy, mitigate resistance, and
potentially reduce dose-limiting toxicity (25, 27). Together, these findings position CoA
biosynthesis as an attractive target for next-generation antifungal drug development.

CURRENT ANTIFUNGAL THERAPIES AND ASSOCIATED CHALLENGES

Currently, only four major classes of antifungal drugs are clinically approved for the
treatment of systemic invasive fungal infections: polyenes, pyrimidine analogs, azoles,
and echinocandins (Fig. 1). Although these agents are effective within their respective
indications, they are collectively associated with multiple limitations that undermine
their capacity to fully combat the increasing burden posed by invasive fungal diseases
(Table 1) (28).

Polyenes

Polyenes were the first class of antifungals to be clinically introduced, beginning with
the approval of the polyene amphotericin B deoxycholate in the 1950s. Amphoteri­
cin B is notable for its high fungicidal potency and broad-spectrum activity against
several classes of fungal pathogens, including resistant strains of Aspergillus and Candida
(29, 30). These properties arise from its ability to directly bind and sequester ergo­
sterol, a major lipid component of fungal cell membranes that is essential for main­
taining membrane fluidity and integrity (31). This interaction promotes the formation
of extramembranous aggregates that extract ergosterol from the membrane, disrupt­
ing membrane integrity and function (Fig. 1) (31). Ergosterol complexation perturbs
membrane organization and triggers oxidative and metabolic stress, further amplifying
its fungicidal effects (32–34). Owing to this multifaceted, sterol-targeted mechanism,
amphotericin B remains the most potent and broad-spectrum antifungal agent in clinical
use, with acquired resistance remaining exceedingly rare (35).
