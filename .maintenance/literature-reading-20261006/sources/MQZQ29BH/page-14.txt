nethyl-pantothenamide (α-Me-N-PE-PanAm, here referred to as α-PanAm) has emerged
as a particularly potent and stable pantothenate analog with broad antimicrobial
potential (Fig. 6) (104, 105). The antimicrobial activity of α-PanAm was first demonstrated
in the human malaria parasite Plasmodium falciparum (104). P. falciparum (as well as
several other intraerythrocytic parasites) encodes two PanK homologs, PanK1 and PanK2,
with PanK1 harboring a bona fide catalytic “DIIG” motif not found in PanK2 (106). Genetic
studies in P. yoelii indicated that the expression of both proteins is required for survival
and suggested that the two proteins might form a heterodimer (106). With the exception
of one report (which showed activity of PanK1 in vitro, although the measured Km was
roughly two orders of magnitude greater than physiologically relevant levels) (107),
efforts by several research teams have failed to demonstrate activity of the two parasite
enzymes in vitro using recombinant proteins expressed in E. coli or insect cells (96,
108, 109). Cell-based analyses in P. falciparum employing transgenic parasites expressing
tagged PanK1 and PanK2 provided evidence that the two enzymes form a heterodimer
and that pantothenate phosphorylation activity is observed exclusively in the assembled
complex following purification from parasite extracts (110).
   In addition to competitively inhibiting pantothenate binding to the PfPanK com­
plex, de  Villiers et  al. (111) showed that α-PanAm  is phosphorylated by PfPanK,
resulting in reduced production of phosphopantothenate (111). Moreover, the phos­
phorylated α-PanAm intermediate is further processed by downstream enzymes in the
CoA biosynthetic pathway, ultimately giving rise to nonfunctional CoA analogs. These
nonfunctional CoA derivatives are believed to interfere with CoA-dependent processes
critical to survival, such as fatty acid metabolism and the TCA cycle (111).
    Parallel investigations into the effect of α-PanAm on fungi by Chiu et al. (96) showed
that treatment of S. cerevisiae resulted in a dose-dependent inhibition of yeast growth
(96). The MIC₅₀ of α-PanAm increased significantly when yeast was supplemented with
higher concentrations of pantothenate, suggesting that its inhibitory effect is due to
interference with pantothenate utilization (possibly through inhibition of Cab1p activity).
Consistent with this finding, the hypomorphic cab1G351S mutant exhibited dramatically
higher sensitivity to α-PanAm compared to wild-type yeast (96). Enzyme kinetic and
mass spectrometric studies confirmed that α-PanAm directly competes with pantothen­
ate for phosphorylation by Cab1p (96). Although most efforts currently underway to
develop pantothenamides as antimicrobials are focused on malaria (104, 105, 111), these
findings provide strong evidence to support their development to treat fungal infections.
To our knowledge, the antifungal activity of other pantothenamide derivatives currently
in development as antimalarial drugs has not yet been reported.
