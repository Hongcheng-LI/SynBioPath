Azoles

Azoles emerged as the next major class of antifungals in the 1980s and quickly became
first-line treatments for fungal infections due to their improved safety and oral availabil­
ity (45, 46). The antifungal activity of azoles is primarily mediated by direct inhibition
of lanosterol 14-α-demethylase (Erg11), a fungal enzyme responsible for the conversion
of lanosterol to ergosterol (Fig. 1) (47). By inhibiting Erg11, azoles effectively disrupt
ergosterol synthesis, resulting in accumulation of toxic sterol intermediates, ergosterol
depletion, and loss of membrane integrity. Successive rounds of drug development have
yielded newer-generation azoles marked by expanded antifungal spectra, improved oral
availability, and reduced host toxicity (48). These advancements have allowed azoles
to be used not only for therapeutic treatment in the general population but also for
prophylactic purposes in immunocompromised patients (49).
   The major limitations of azoles are that all (except voriconazole) exhibit fungistatic
rather than fungicidal activity against Candida and Cryptococcus species, increasing the
risk of acquired resistance from long-term use (50). Moreover, the overuse of azoles in
agricultural settings as pesticides has contributed to the emergence of azole-resistant
strains of C. auris and A. fumigatus (5). Investigations into the genetic underpinnings of
azole resistance have revealed various mutations in the ERG11 gene that increase its
stability or expression as key causal factors (47, 51). Use of azoles is also limited by their
high risk of drug–drug interactions mediated by effects on cytochrome P450 enzymes,
which can preclude their use in patients receiving certain medications (52).
