Pantothenate phosphorylation in fungi

The first step of CoA biosynthesis, phosphorylation of pantothenate by PanK, has been
extensively studied to reveal key differences between fungi and humans that present
opportunities for antifungal drug targeting (26, 90). Genome analyses and functional
studies in Saccharomyces cerevisiae demonstrated that yeast cells express a single PANK
(CAB1) gene encoding PanK, and that this gene is essential for viability (91). In con­
trast, the human genome encodes three PANK genes, PANK1, PANK2, and PANK3, which
produce enzymatically active PanK isoforms that differ primarily in their subcellular
localization and tissue-specific distribution (85, 92). Similar to yeast PanK, human PanK1
and PanK3 are cytosolic and presumed essential for life (no individuals with deleterious
mutations in PANK1 or PANK3 have been observed). Human PanK2 is uniquely localized
to the mitochondrial intermembrane space, and several pathogenic variants, including
loss-of-function and hypomorphic mutations, in the PANK2 gene are associated with
the progressive neurodegenerative disorder known as PKAN (Pantothenate Kinase-Asso­
ciated Neurodegeneration) (93). These differences suggest that humans have built-in
redundancy in the CoA biosynthesis pathway through encoding multiple PanK isoforms,
whereas fungi rely on a single, essential PanK enzyme, creating an ideal opportunity for
selective drug targeting.
   Similar patterns hold across other pathogenic  fungi. For example,  C. albicans,
Nakaseomyces glabrata, C. auris, A. fumigatus, C. neoformans, and Histoplasma capsulatum
each harbor a single-copy PanK gene (26, 80). However, genetic validation of PanK
essentiality in fungal pathogens has thus far been demonstrated only in C. albicans and
A. fumigatus (26, 80). This combination of limited redundancy and emerging genetic
validation underscores the broad potential of PanK as an antifungal target.
   The most well-characterized fungal PanK  is encoded by the CAB1 (Coenzyme A
Biosynthesis) gene in S. cerevisiae. Since CAB1 deletion is lethal, its function has largely
been studied through the expression of hypomorphic variants in yeast (94). The first
hypomorphic cab1 mutant was discovered in a chemical mutagenesis screen designed
to identify mutant strains with defects in fatty acid biosynthesis (24, 95). This yielded a
temperature-sensitive strain (cab1ts), inviable at 37°C but viable at lower temperatures
(24, 95). Sequencing and gene complementation studies revealed that the thermosen­
sitive phenotype in the cab1ts mutant was caused by a glycine-to-serine mutation at
amino acid position 351 (G351S) in CAB1 (24, 96). Biochemical characterization of this
mutant revealed that it retained less than 10% of wild-type PanK activity and exhibited
a growth defect on non-fermentable carbon sources (an indicator of impaired mitochon­
drial function) (26, 96).
  More recently, Gihaz et al. (26) reported the first crystal structure of a fungal PanK
enzyme (that of S. cerevisiae Cab1p) at a resolution of 1.8 Å (26). Structural analysis
revealed that, like human PanKs, Cab1p is a homodimer with each monomer comprised
of a parallel β-sheet flanked by α-helices to form an α/β Rossmann-like fold typical
of kinases  (Fig. 4A and B) (26, 97, 98). The enzyme’s active site  is buried at the
interface between the two monomers, where pantothenate and ATP bind to distinct
but adjacent pockets lined by catalytic residues Asp24, Glu105, Ser158, and Tyr220,
corresponding to Asp45, Glu138, Ser189, and Tyr254 in human PanK3 (Fig. 4C) (26, 90).

transfer. Although the catalytic cores are nearly identical, the surrounding nucleotide-
