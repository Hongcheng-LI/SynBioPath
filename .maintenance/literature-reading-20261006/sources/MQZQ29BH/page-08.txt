Among these, acetyl-CoA represents the most central and versatile intermediate.  It
donates two-carbon units for ergosterol and fatty acid biosynthesis, provides the acetyl
donor for protein acetylation and epigenetic regulation, fuels energy production via
TCA cycle, and contributes vacuolar function and organelle integrity (Fig. 2). By linking
nutrient availability with biosynthetic output and stress adaptation, acetyl-CoA functions
as a major regulator of fungal growth and virulence (22, 82).
   Given the fundamental role of CoA and acetyl-CoA in cellular metabolism,  it  is
not surprising that its biosynthetic pathway is conserved across organisms including
bacteria, archaea, and eukaryotes, reflecting the universal requirement for CoA in central
metabolism (Fig. 3) (22, 83–85). The universal precursor for CoA biosynthesis is panto­
thenic acid (vitamin B5). In most bacteria and eukaryotes, pantothenic acid is phosphory­
lated by pantothenate kinase (PanK) to form 4′-phosphopantothenate (PPan). In contrast,
many archaea and some symbiotic bacteria bypass pantothenate and instead generate
PPan via an alternative route in which pantoate is first phosphorylated to 4-phosphopan­
toate and subsequently converted to PPan (84). In the second step of CoA biosynthesis,
phosphopantothenoylcysteine synthetase (PPCS) catalyzes the condensation of PPan
with cysteine, yielding 4′-phosphopantothenoylcysteine (PPan-Cys). The cysteine moiety
of this intermediate is then decarboxylated by phosphopantothenoylcysteine decarbox­
ylase (PPCDC), producing 4′-phosphopantetheine (PPanSH). Next, phosphopantetheine
adenylyltransferase (PPAT) transfers an adenylyl group to PPanSH, producing dephos­
pho-CoA. Finally, dephospho-CoA kinase (DPCK) phosphorylates the 3′-hydroxyl of the
ribose in dephospho-CoA to form the fully functional CoA molecule (Fig. 3). While the
core architecture of CoA biosynthesis is conserved, the individual enzymes and their
modes of regulation vary substantially between microorganisms and the host, providing
opportunities for selective antimicrobial targeting (22).

The salvage and de novo synthesis pathways for pantothenate

Although the CoA biosynthetic pathway is generally conserved across eukaryotes, key
differences exist between fungi and humans that have informed drug development
efforts. Most notably, while both humans and fungi can salvage pantothenic acid from
their extracellular environment via transporter-mediated uptake, fungi also possess
the unique ability to synthesize pantothenic acid de novo via a noncanonical route
that generates β-alanine from polyamine catabolism (Fig. 3) (86, 87). Instead of using
aspartate decarboxylase (as in many bacteria), yeast degrades the polyamine spermine
through the action of an amine oxidase (encoded by FMS1 in S. cerevisiae), produc­
ing spermidine and 3-aminopropanal (86, 88); the latter is subsequently oxidized to
β-alanine by aldehyde dehydrogenases (encoded by ALD2 and ALD3 in S. cerevisiae)
(87). The produced β-alanine is subsequently condensed with pantoate by pantothenate
synthase (encoded by PAN6 in S. cerevisiae) to form pantothenic acid, which then feeds
into CoA biosynthesis. Because  all four enzymes of the polyamine pathway (orni­
thine decarboxylase, S-adenosylmethionine decarboxylase, spermidine synthase, and
spermine synthase encoded by SPE1, SPE2, SPE3, and SPE4, respectively, in S. cerevisiae)
are required to generate spermine, perturbation of polyamine metabolism disrupts
β-alanine, pantothenate, and ultimately CoA production. In yeast, the pantothenate
