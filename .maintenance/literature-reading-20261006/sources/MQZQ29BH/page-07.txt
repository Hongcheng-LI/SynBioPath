GPI anchor biosynthesis inhibitors

Fosmanogepix (APX001), a first-in-class Gwt1 enzyme inhibitor, targets the glycosylphos­
phatidylinositol (GPI) anchor biosynthesis pathway, which is essential for fungal cell
wall integrity and virulence (69, 70). By disrupting anchoring of mannoproteins to the
cell surface, Fosmanogepix exerts broad fungicidal activity against Candida spp., C.
auris, Aspergillus, and some Mucorales, with limited cross-resistance relative to azoles
or echinocandins. Its high oral bioavailability (>90%) and CNS penetration make it a
strong candidate for treating disseminated and CNS infections (71).

Dihydroorotate dehydrogenase inhibitors

Olorofim (F901318) inhibits fungal dihydroorotate dehydrogenase in the pyrimidine
biosynthesis pathway  (72).  It displays potent  activity against Aspergillus spp. and
difficult molds (Lomentospora, Scedosporium) though it lacks efficacy against Candida
and Mucorales (73). As the first representative of this mechanism, olorofim provides a
complementary option for mold infections that are resistant to azoles and polyenes.

Novel azoles and polyenes

Oteseconazole (VT-1161) and opelconazole (PC945) exemplify improved azole deriv­
atives optimized for safety and tissue distribution (74, 75). Oteseconazole, recently
FDA-approved for recurrent vulvovaginal candidiasis, shows enhanced selectivity for
fungal Erg11p and minimal cytochrome P450 inhibition  (74,  76). Opelconazole  is
formulated for inhalation, enabling localized delivery against pulmonary Aspergillus
infection (75). In parallel, oral formulations of amphotericin B (CAmB) aim to overcome
the toxicity and IV dependence of polyene therapy (77).

Mitochondrial respiration inhibitors

ATI-2307 disrupts fungal mitochondrial function, resulting in loss of mitochondrial
membrane potential and inhibition of ATP synthesis (78). Though preclinical, it demon­
strates potent activity against Candida spp. and select molds (79).
   Currently approved and investigational antifungal agents, including those highligh­
ted in this review, target well-defined pathways involved in cell wall synthesis, ergosterol
biosynthesis, nucleotide metabolism, or mitochondrial respiration (65, 66). In contrast,
the coenzyme A (CoA) metabolic pathway has only recently been identified as a potential
antifungal target, opening an unexplored avenue for therapeutic development (25–
27, 80). This pathway represents a distinct metabolic vulnerability central to CoA and
acetyl-CoA biosynthesis. Targeting this pathway, therefore, offers a fundamentally new
strategy for antifungal drug development and a promising approach to address the
growing challenge of drug-resistant fungal infections. In this review, we highlight CoA
metabolism as a promising antifungal target and discuss its potential to overcome
current therapeutic limitations.
