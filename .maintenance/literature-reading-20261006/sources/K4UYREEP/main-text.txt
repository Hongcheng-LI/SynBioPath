ABSTRACT: Paclitaxel (Taxol) is among the most successful
natural anticancer agents, yet its manufacturing still relies on
expensive semisynthetic methods carried out under harsh alkaline
conditions. Deciphering the entire Taxol biosynthetic pathway has
been a longstanding challenge for over 50 years because of its
complex regulatory network, but it has only recently been
understood. Differences between plant and microbial systems
render current knowledge insufficient for scalable biotechnological
production, necessitating systematic metabolic engineering to
overcome multiple bottlenecks. Artificial pathways can compensate
for an incomplete understanding of native biosynthesis and offer an
alternative strategy to alleviate supply constraints. Here, we report
the construction of an artificial pathway in yeast that uses (2R,3S)-3-phenylisoserine as the substrate and requires only three
enzymatic steps to convert baccatin III to Taxol, demonstrating the broad substrate scope of β-phenylalanoyl-CoA ligases (PCLs).
Structure−activity analysis of Baccatin III:3-amino-3-phenylpropanoyltransferase (BAPT) is further employed to enhance its
catalytic efficiency toward non-natural substrates. This work offers a practical strategy for the first step of sustainable production of
Taxol using synthetic biology.
KEYWORDS: Taxol, artificial pathway design, protein engineering, CRISPR/Cas9, Saccharomyces cerevisiae
■INTRODUCTION
Plants have evolved a diverse repertoire of biologically active
secondary metabolites.1−3 Among them, the diterpenoid
alkaloid paclitaxel (Taxol) stands out for its complex 6/8/6-
taxane skeleton, distinctive oxetane ring motif, and phenyl-
isoserine side chain.4,5 Taxol exerts anticancer activity by
stabilizing microtubules and arresting cell-cycle progression,6,7
and it was approved by the U.S. FDA in 1992 for the treatment
of several solid tumors, including ovarian, breast, and lung
cancers.8,9 Taxol and its related taxane analogues (e.g.,
docetaxel) are among the most successful natural anticancer
therapeutics, with global annual sales exceeding USD 15.8
billion.10,11 Current medical production relies primarily on
semisynthesis from baccatin III or 10-deacetylbaccatin III
isolated from yew needles.12,13 These processes require
protection and deprotection of the C7 hydroxyl group under
strongly basic conditions, as well as incorporation of a
protected phenylpropanoid side chain. Commercial production
has also been implemented using plant cell cultures derived
from various Taxus species.14 Nevertheless, the high
production cost underscores the need for deeper insight into
the Taxol biosynthetic pathway or for the development of
alternative strategies capable of alleviating persistent supply
constraints.15
Although more than 60 research groups worldwide have
reported 12 total syntheses of Taxol,16−18 these routes remain
economically impractical. Fully elucidating the biosynthetic
pathway has been a long-standing goal in plant natural product
research. Early efforts beginning in the late 1990s19 stalled for
nearly two decades due to limited comprehensive genomic and
transcriptomic resources. Recently, chromosome-level assem-
blies of the large genomes of Taxus wallichiana,20Taxus
chinensis var. mairei,21 and Taxus yunnanensis22 have provided
critical insights into Taxol biosynthesis. Through cumulative
contributions from several influential studies,23−27 the pathway
has now been largely delineated, revealing far greater
enzymatic and regulatory complexity than previously antici-
pated (Supplementary Figure S1).28 Yet, as with many other
representative natural products, including vinblastine,29,30
cannabinoids,31 opioids,32 and scopolamine,33 pathway eluci-
dation provides a foundation but is insufficient for scalable

Artificial biosynthetic pathways can not only compensate for

more streamlined reaction sequences.44 A representative

remained poorly understood, particularly the oxidation of the
β-phenylalanine side chain at C2′ by a taxane 2′α-hydroxylase

Here, we sought to reconstruct the biosynthetic pathway
from baccatin III to Taxol in Saccharomyces cerevisiae. Under
the conditions tested in this study, phenylalanine amino-
mutases (PAMs) exhibited low apparent activity in yeast,
despite their activities having been validated in vitro51 and in
Nicotiana benthamiana.26,27 Consequently, we devised an
alternative biosynthetic route employing (2R,3S)-3-phenyl-
isoserine, a readily available chemical, as the substrate (Figure
1). Together with the recently characterized taxoid-3′-N-
benzoyltransferase (T3′NBT),27 this strategy enabled Taxol
production in the absence of PAM and T2′αΗ, providing
evidence that β-phenylalanoyl-CoA ligase (PCL) can catalyze
diverse non-native substrates. Protein engineering of baccatin
III:3-amino-3-phenylpropanoyltransferase (BAPT) further in-
creased its catalytic efficiency toward (2R,3S)-3-phenylisoser-
inoyl-CoA, thereby enhancing overall pathway productivity.
■RESULTS
Low Activity of PAMs in the Yeast Chassis
The native Taxol assembly pathway has long been proposed to
involve five enzymatic steps converting baccatin III to Taxol
(Figure 1a).27 In the first step, α-phenylalanine is isomerized to
β-phenylalanine by PAM, which is subsequently activated to β-
phenylalanoyl-CoA by PCL in the presence of acetyl-CoA.
BAPT then installs the phenylpropanoid side chain to the C13
position of baccatin III to generate 3′-N-debenzoyl-2′-
deoxytaxol. Hydroxylation at the C2′ position produces 3′-
N-debenzoyltaxol, which is finally converted to Taxol via
benzoylation at the 3′-N position by a selective N-
benzoyltransferase using benzoyl-CoA as the acyl donor.
As the initial and committed step of side-chain assembly,
two PAMs have been identified: TcaPAM from Taxus
canadensis (GenBank accession no. AAT47186.1) and
TchPAM from Taxus chinensis (GenBank accession no.
AAU01183.1) (Supplementary Figure S2).51 We individually
expressed these two enzymes in S. cerevisiae strains harboring
the downstream pathway genes, generating strains BTP2-2 and
BTP2-3, respectively (Table 1 and Supplementary Figure S3).
We then assessed their enzymatic activity by quantifying the
accumulation of the intermediate 3′-N-debenzoyl-2′-deoxytax-
ol following supplementation with either L-phenylalanine or β-
phenylalanine in the presence of baccatin III. Unexpectedly, we
only observed substantial accumulation of 3′-N-debenzoyl-2′-
deoxytaxol in the presence of β-phenylalanine (Figure 2a).
With the supplementation of L-phenylalanine as the substrate,
strain BTP2-2 expressing TcaPAM showed no detectable
accumulation of the intermediate 3′-N-debenzoyl-2′-deoxytax-
ol, whereas strain BTP2-3 expressing TchPAM produced only
trace amounts, markedly lower than those obtained with β-
phenylalanine feeding. These results indicate that PAMs
exhibit marginal activity under the current experimental
conditions in yeast, in contrast to their reported functionality
in N. benthamiana.26,27 This discrepancy may arise from
multiple factors, including but not limited to insufficient
expression levels, improper protein folding, limitations in
cofactor availability within the microbial host, and/or potential
interference from endogenous yeast metabolism.

Design of an Artificial Pathway for Baccatin III Side-Chain
Modification
The low apparent activity of PAMs under the current
experimental conditions in yeast, together with the absence
of conclusive evidence for the C2′-oxidase at the onset of this
work, posed significant barriers to heterologous Taxol
production. In vitro studies by the Walker group found that
the adenylation (A) and thiolation (T) domains of the
nonribosomal peptide synthetase tyrocidine synthetase A
(PheAT) function as a CoA ligase, producing α- and β-
phenylalanoyl-CoA as well as (2R,3S)-3-phenylisoserinoyl-
CoA from the corresponding amino acids, CoA, and N-
acetylcysteamine.52,53 Following thioesterification by PheAT,
these CoA thioesters could serve as acyl donors for baccatin III
modification by a 13-O-acyltransferase, enabling the synthesis
of Taxol analogs with improved therapeutic potential.54 These
findings demonstrated the feasibility of BAPT-catalyzed
utilization of (2R,3S)-3-phenylisoserinoyl-CoA, providing
both conceptual inspiration and a feasibility basis for our
design of an artificial pathway using (2R,3S)-3-phenylisoserine
as the upstream substrate. However, the CoA ligase activity of
PheAT depends on relatively high concentrations of CoA due
to competition effects, which limits its applicability in vivo.55
Here, we proposed an artificial pathway that bypasses the
need for the C2′ hydroxylase and PAM, requiring only three
enzymatic steps (Figure 1b). In this pathway, the native
substrate L-phenylalanine is replaced with (2R,3S)-3-phenyl-
isoserine, which is activated by a CoA ligase with robust in vivo
activity. The resulting CoA thioester is then transferred to the
C13 position of baccatin III by BAPT, generating the 3′-N-
debenzoyltaxol intermediate that already contains the correctly
configured C2′-hydroxyl group characteristic of Taxol. Finally,
as in the native pathway, T3′NBT catalyzes benzoylation at the
3′-N position, completing whole-cell conversion of baccatin III
to Taxol.
Activity Characterization of PCLs toward Non-Natural
Substrates
PrPCL from Penicillium chrysogenum and its activity-enhanced
mutant PrPCLA321G have previously been shown to accept
aromatic amino acids, such as α-phenylalanine, α-tyrosine, and
β-phenylalanine, prompting us to hypothesize that they might
also activate (2R,3S)-3-phenylisoserine through thioesterifica-
tion.56 In addition, a recently identified PCL from Taxus
chinensis (TchPCL) was expected to confer advantages over
microbial-derived PCLs (Supplementary Figure S4).26 To
evaluate substrate specificity in vivo, we individually integrated
TmeBAPT from Taxus × media and each PCL into the genome
of the chassis strain BTP0, generating strains BTP1a and
BTP1b, respectively (Table 1 and Supplementary Figure S3).
We then quantified the accumulation of 3′-N-debenzoyl-2′-
deoxytaxol or 3′-N-debenzoyltaxol following the supplementa-
tion of β-phenylalanine or (2R,3S)-3-phenylisoserine in the
presence of baccatin III. Notably, we detected trace amounts of
Taxol in commercial baccatin III standards obtained from
multiple suppliers; therefore, we further purified the standards
by chromatography to eliminate interference during product
quantification (Supplementary Figure S5). Our results
demonstrated that both PrPCLA321G and TchPCL exhibited
CoA ligase activity toward β-phenylalanine and (2R,3S)-3-
phenylisoserine, with TchPCL displaying substantially higher
activity than PrPCLA321G (Figure 2b). Upon β-phenylalanine
supplementation, strain BTP1a expressing TchPCL accumu-
lated 3′-N-debenzoyl-2′-deoxytaxol at 8.1-fold higher than that
of strain BTP1b expressing PrPCLA321G. Strikingly, with the
supply of (2R,3S)-3-phenylisoserine, BTP1a accumulated 3′-
N-debenzoyltaxol at 134.8-fold that of BTP1b. Nevertheless,
the accumulation of 3′-N-debenzoyl-2′-deoxytaxol consistently
exceeded that of 3′-N-debenzoyltaxol, indicating a strong
preference of both PCLs toward the native substrate β-
phenylalanine.

Protein Engineering of TmeBAPT with Better Performance
Previous studies reported that TmeBAPT fused to an MBP
solubility tag exhibits decreased substrate affinity (KM) and
catalytic efficiency (kcat) toward the non-native substrate
(2R,3S)-3-phenylisoserinoyl-CoA compared with β-phenyl-
alanoyl-CoA (Supplementary Table 1).54 We therefore
hypothesize that the intrinsically low activity of TmeBAPT
toward (2R,3S)-3-phenylisoserinoyl-CoA constitutes a major
rate-limiting step in the artificial pathway and that engineering
TmeBAPT to enhance this activity could relieve this bottle-
neck.
As no crystal structure or close homologue was identified
using Foldseek,57 we first predicted the structure of TmeBAPT
using AlphaFold 3.0.58 The resulting model showed high
confidence, with a predicted template modeling (pTM) score
of 0.9 (Supplementary Figure S6). We then sequentially
docked β-phenylalanoyl-CoA and baccatin III into the
predicted active pocket using qvina and selected the most
plausible conformations (Supplementary Figure S7).59 The
elongated substrate channel likely accommodates the CoA
moiety, whereas a larger cavity at one end positions the β-
phenylalanoyl side chain such that its carbonyl group can be
attacked by the C13 hydroxyl group of baccatin III to enable
acyl transfer. To identify positions that may influence catalytic
activity toward (2R,3S)-3-phenylisoserinoyl-CoA, we analyzed
residues located within 4 Å of the β-phenylalanoyl side chain,
including F43, M384, L385, Q386, Q387, L394, P395, Y397,
and F416 (Figure 3a). Among these, Q386 lies closest to the
carbonyl and amino groups of the β-phenylalanoyl moiety and
may form hydrogen bonds with the substrate, suggesting a
direct catalytic role. Given the flexibility of loops surrounding
the active site, neighboring residues Q387 and Q388 also lie
near the substrate and could engage in similar interactions; we
therefore incorporated these positions into our engineering
efforts (Figure 3b).
We constructed variants in which glutamine residues were
substituted with glutamate or aspartate to introduce carbox-
ylate groups capable of forming stronger hydrogen-bonding or
electrostatic interactions. In parallel, we generated glutamine-
to-asparagine substitutions to examine whether shortening the
side chain could provide additional space to accommodate the
non-native substrate, which contains an extra hydroxyl group.
We integrated genes encoding TmeBAPT mutants at positions
Q386, Q387, and Q388 into the genome of the BTP0 chassis
strain together with TchPCL and evaluated their activities
toward (2R,3S)-3-phenylisoserinoyl-CoA by quantifying 3′-N-
debenzoyltaxol production. As anticipated, substitution of
these residues with glutamate or aspartate consistently
increased catalytic activity, likely because the introduced
carboxylate groups enhance substrate stabilization via stronger
hydrogen-bonding interactions (Figure 3c). Notably, the

Whole-Cell Conversion of Baccatin III to Taxol
Taxol is formed through benzoylation of the 3′-N position of
the intermediate 3′-N-debenzoyltaxol. Using the surrogate
substrate 3′-N-debenzoyl-2′-deoxytaxol, Walker et al. identified
an N-benzoyltransferase from Taxus canadensis (TcaDBTNBT,
GenBank accession no. AAM75818.1) as the enzyme
responsible for the terminal acylation step in Taxol biosyn-
thesis.60 More recently, however, Liang et al. reported a
distinct 3′-N-benzoyltransferase from Taxus chinensis
(TchT3′NBT, GenBank accession no. XCT47277.1) to
efficiently catalyze the conversion of 3′-N-debenzoyltaxol to
Taxol (Supplementary Figure S8).27 To evaluate their roles in
the artificial pathway, we further integrated genes encoding
TchT3′NBT or TcaDBTNBT, together with the T. media
benzoyl-CoA ligase TmeAAE4 (GenBank accession no.
QID19982.1), into the BTP1a-M1 and BTP1b-M1 strains
expressing the high-activity BAPTQ386E variant, yielding strains
BTP3, BTP4, and BTP5 (Table 1 and Supplementary Figure
S3).61 In the presence of (2R,3S)-3-phenylisoserine, we
detected Taxol exclusively in the fermentation broth of strain
BTP5 expressing TchT3′NBT and TchPCL, confirming the role
of TchT3′NBT in catalyzing the conversion of 3′-N-
debenzoyltaxol to Taxol and demonstrating the feasibility of
the proposed artificial pathway that bypasses the native C2′-
oxidation step (Figure 4a and 4b). In contrast, we failed to
detect any Taxol in strain BTP3 expressing TchT3′NBT and
PrPCLA312G, indicating that the low catalytic activity of
PrPCLA312G is insufficient to support downstream reactions.

TchT3′NBT or TcaDBTNBT together with TmeAAE4 and
T2′OGD, yielding strains BTP2-1, BTP6-1, and BTP7-1,
respectively (Table 1 and Supplementary Figure S3). Upon
supplementation with β-phenylalanine, Taxol production was
detected in strains BTP2-1 and BTP6-1 expressing
TchT3′NBT, with the Taxol titer in BTP2-1 being 5.2-fold
higher than that in BTP6-1, highlighting the critical role of the
highly active Taxus-derived TchPCL in this pathway.
Consistent with results from the artificial pathway, no Taxol
accumulation was observed in strain BTP7-1 expressing
TcaDBTNBT. Given previous reports that TcaDBTNBT can
catalyze the conversion of the pathway intermediate 3′-N-
debenzoyl-2′-deoxytaxol, we omitted T2′OGD and integrated
TcaDBTNBT and TmeAAE4 into the chassis strains BTP0 to
generate BTP1c. We then introduced either PrPCLA312G or
TchPCL together with TmeBAPT, yielding strains BTP8 and
BTP9, respectively. In the presence of β-phenylalanine, 2′-
deoxytaxol was detected in strains BTP8 and BTP9 expressing
TcaDBTNBT, whereas no accumulation of 2′-deoxytaxol was
observed in strain BTP2 expressing TchT3′NBT (Figures 4c
and 4d). These results demonstrate that both TcaDBTNBT
and TchT3′NBT are capable of catalyzing 3′-N benzoylation
but exhibit distinct and highly specific substrate preferences
(Supplementary Figure S9).
In addition, we compared the conversion efficiencies of the
artificial and native pathways from baccatin III to Taxol. As
shown in Figure 4b, strain BTP5 produced 1.47 ng L−1 Taxol,
corresponding to a significantly higher conversion efficiency
than that of the native pathway initiated from L-phenylalanine.
Nevertheless, this titer represented only 18.7% of that achieved
by strain BTP2-1 when supplied with β-phenylalanine,
indicating that the current artificial pathway remains far from
optimal. It requires further improvements in enzyme
expression levels, catalytic performance, and pathway flux
balancing to fully capitalize on the theoretical advantages of the
streamlined biosynthetic route. Notably, all three enzymes
constituting the artificial pathway (TmeBAPT, TchPCL, and
TchT3′NBT) harbor a canonical C-terminal peroxisomal
targeting signal (SKL or SRL). Fluorescence confocal
microscopy further confirmed the peroxisomal localization of
these enzymes in yeast, underscoring the key role of
peroxisomes in mediating baccatin III side-chain modifications
(Supplementary Figure S10).
■DISCUSSION
Leveraging the substrate plasticity of pathway enzymes, we
establish an artificial biosynthetic pathway that employs
(2R,3S)-3-phenylisoserine, a readily accessible compound, as
the acyl donor, thereby bypassing the native phenylalanine
isomerization and C2′ α-hydroxylation step. With only three
enzymatic reactions, this pathway enables whole-cell con-
version of baccatin III to Taxol in yeast, providing a potentially
sustainable alternative strategy for producing this clinically
important anticancer agent.
Although Taxol titer achieved via the artificial pathway
remains lower than that of the native pathway supplied with β-
phenylalanine, this limitation does not preclude its broader
significance. The decreased number of catalytic steps is
expected to minimize intermediate loss and improve
theoretical conversion efficiency. Meanwhile, the limited
activity of pathway enzymes toward non-native substrates
reflects an early stage of optimization; further mechanistic
insights and protein engineering are anticipated to enhance
overall flux.62,63 Notably, confocal microscopy revealed that all
three enzymes in the artificial pathway (PCL, BAPT, and
T3′NBT) are colocalized within the peroxisome, whereas PAM
and T2′OGD lack peroxisomal targeting signals. Introducing a
C-terminal ePTS1-tagged T2′OGD into the native pathway did
not improve Taxol production upon β-phenylalanine supple-
mentation (Supplementary Figure S11), suggesting that the
C2′ hydroxylation step may be incompatible with the
peroxisomal environment. These observations highlight a
potential advantage of the artificial pathway in enabling
effective subcellular cocompartmentalization.64−66 In addition,
the artificial route expands substrate flexibility. Our results
demonstrate that PCL exhibits activity toward substrates
beyond its native β-phenylalanine, indicating a broader
substrate scope than previously recognized. This property
opens opportunities for generating Taxol analogs with diverse
aromatic amino acid-derived side chains, which may display
improved pharmacological properties.
At present, the artificial pathway remains at a proof-of-
concept stage, with relatively low conversion efficiency. Several
bottlenecks require further optimization, including substrate
uptake, enzyme catalytic performance, pathway flux, and
cofactor availability. The poor membrane permeability of
substrates such as baccatin III limits cellular uptake and often
necessitates the use of toxic cosolvents such as dimethyl
sulfoxide (DMSO), which can compromise cell viability.
Reconstruction of de novo baccatin III biosynthesis or
identification of dedicated transporters may alleviate this
limitation. Although protein modeling and substrate docking
have improved the catalytic efficiency of TmeBAPT toward the
non-native substrate, other enzymes, such as TchPCL, have not
yet been engineered for altered substrate specificity, constrain-
ing (2R,3S)-3-phenylisoserine activation. Furthermore, con-
focal microscopy suggests relatively low expression levels of
pathway enzymes in yeast, indicating that transcriptional and
translational optimization could enhance functional expression.
Importantly, peroxisomal PCL requires coenzyme A (CoA) as
an essential cosubstrate, whereas CoA biosynthesis primarily
occurs in the cytosol and mitochondria. In the absence of
efficient CoA transport systems in eukaryotic microorganisms,
the peroxisomal CoA pool relies largely on recycling during β-
oxidation. This competition may perturb β-oxidation and
cellular energy homeostasis, thereby introducing an additional
metabolic constraint that must be addressed to achieve
efficient heterologous Taxol production.
In summary, we successfully establish an alternative artificial
pathway for converting baccatin III to Taxol in yeast, offering a
promising framework for sustainable production. Despite its
currently lower efficiency compared to the native pathway
supplemented with β-phenylalanine, the artificial route
provides distinct advantages in pathway simplification,
subcellular cocompartmentalization, and structural diversifica-
tion potential, warranting further optimization and develop-
ment.

■MATERIALS AND METHODS
Chemicals and Reagents
Polyethylene glycol (PEG) and salmon sperm DNA sodium salt
(ssDNA) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, Missouri,
USA). The DNA PCR Purification Kit was obtained from Thermo
Fisher Scientific (Shanghai, China). Baccatin III and Taxol standards
were purchased from Chengdu DeSiTe Biological Technology Co.,
Ltd. (Chengdu, China). Phanta Max Super-Fidelity DNA Polymerase
and ClonExpress II One Step Cloning Kit were purchased from
Vazyme (Nanjing, China). All restriction enzymes and T4 DNA ligase
were purchased from New England Biolabs (Beijing, China). Unless
otherwise specified, all chemicals were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich.
Strains and Growth Media
Yeast strains used in this study were listed in Table 1. Escherichia coli
DH5α (Tsingke Biotech, China) was used for plasmid construction,
propagation, and maintenance. Recombinant E. coli strains were
cultured in Luria−Bertani (LB) medium supplemented with
ampicillin (100 mg L−1). Yeast strains were routinely cultivated in
yeast extract−peptone−dextrose (YPD) medium containing 10 g L−1
yeast extract, 20 g L−1 peptone, and 20 g L−1 glucose at 30 °C with
shaking at 250 r.p.m. When required, G418 sulfate (Sangon Biotech,
Shanghai, China) was added to a final concentration of 100 mg L−1.
Plasmid Construction
The Cas9 expression plasmid pRS41K-iCas9 and gRNA helper
plasmids pRS423-SpSgH and pRS426-SpSgH were constructed in our
previous studies.67,68 Guide RNAs (gRNAs) were designed using the
Benchling CRISPR tool (https://benchling.com) and cloned into
BsaI-digested pRS423-SpSgH or pRS426-SpSgH. For construction of
multi-gRNA expression plasmids, individual gRNA expression
cassettes were amplified by PCR and assembled using Golden Gate
assembly. Site-directed mutagenesis of ΤmeBAPT was performed by
overlap extension PCR. Genes hypothesized to be involved in Taxol
production via baccatin III side-chain modification (TcaPAM,
TchPAM,
PrPCLA312G,
TchPCL,
ΤmeBAPT,
TchT2′OGD,
TcaDBTNBT, TchT3′NBT, and TmeAAE4) expression were synthe-
sized by GenScript Biotech (Nanjing, China). These genes were
cloned into the multiple cloning sites (MCSs) of pESC-series vectors
(pESC-URA, pESC-LEU, pESC-TRP, and pESC-HIS) using either
restriction digestion and ligation (MCS1: BamHI/XhoI; MCS2: NotI/
SacI) or Gibson Assembly. Oligonucleotide synthesis and DNA
sequencing were performed by Tsingke Biotech (Hangzhou, China).
Plasmids were extracted from E. coli using the AxyPrep Plasmid
Miniprep Kit (Axygen, Shanghai, China).
Yeast Transformation and Strain Construction
All genetic modifications in yeast were performed using CRISPR/
Cas9-mediated genome editing.69,70 Heterologous gene expression
cassettes were amplified by PCR with 40 bp homology arms
corresponding to the target chromosomal loci and cotransformed,
together with the appropriate sgRNA plasmids, into Cas9-expressing
yeast strains using the lithium acetate/single-stranded DNA/poly-
ethylene glycol (LiAc/ssDNA/PEG) method.71 Unless otherwise
specified, 600 ng of gRNA plasmid and 600 ng of each donor DNA
fragment were used per transformation. All sgRNA plasmids for
genome integration used in this study are listed in Supporting
Information Table S2.
In Vivo Feeding Assays
Three colonies were picked from fresh YPD agar plates and
inoculated into 3 mL YPD medium. After cultivation for 24 h at 30
°C with shaking at 250 rpm, 30 μL of the seed culture was transferred
to 24-well plates containing 3 mL of fresh YPD medium and grown
under identical conditions for an additional 48 h. Cells were harvested
by centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 5 min and resuspended in 3 mL of
fresh YPG medium containing 20 g L−1 galactose. Cultures were
supplemented with 50 μM baccatin III and 500 μM β-phenylalanine
or (2R,3S)-3-phenylisoserine. Benzoic acid was additionally supplied
when a benzoyl-CoA ligase was expressed. To prevent precipitation
and improve substrate availability, DMSO was added to a final
concentration of 3% (v/v). Cultures were subsequently incubated at
30 °C with shaking at 250 rpm for 48 h.
Qualitative and Quantitative Analysis of Taxane
Metabolites were extracted from the fermentation broth containing
cells and culture broth. The culture was mixed with an equal volume
of ethyl acetate and vigorously shaken to disrupt yeast cells, followed
by extraction at low speed for 4 h. Phase separation was achieved by
centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. The organic phase was
transferred to a fresh tube and concentrated to dryness under vacuum
to remove residual solvent. After evaporation, the residue was
redissolved in methanol, filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane filter,
and analyzed by LC−MS.
For the separation and analysis of the treated samples, 2 μL was
injected into a 1290 Infinity II LC system (Agilent Technologies)
equipped with a Zorbax Rapid Resolution HT C18 column
(30 × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm; Agilent Technologies). Metabolites were
separated using the following gradient: from 5% B to 95% B from 0 to
10 min (0.3 mL min−1), held at 95% B from 10 to 13 min
(0.3 mL min−1), and held at 5% B from 13 to 16 min (0.3 mL min−1).
Solvent A was 0.1% formic acid in water, and solvent B was 0.1%
formic acid in 100% methanol. The eluent from the LC system was
injected into a 6470 triple quadrupole LC/MS system (Agilent
Technologies) equipped with an ESI ion source. The resolution,
capillary voltage, and source temperature were set to 100,000, 4 kV,
and 350 °C, respectively. The UPLC−MS data were processed and
analyzed using MassHunter software (Agilent Technologies). LC−
MS multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode was employed for the
quantitative analysis of Taxol and its intermediates, and detailed
parameters are listed in Supplementary Table S3.
Confocal Microscopy
The EGFP was fused to the N-terminus of target proteins based on
structural features. The corresponding coding sequences were
genomically integrated into the wild-type yeast strain and verified
by colony PCR. The gene encoding PEX3, used as a peroxisomal
marker, was amplified from the yeast genome, fused to mCherry at the
C-terminus, and integrated into strains expressing EGFP fusion
proteins using previously described methods. Yeast strains were
cultivated in YPD medium for 18 h, harvested, and washed twice with
10 mM phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Subsequently, 2.5 μL of cell
suspension was applied to a microscope slide, covered with a
coverslip, and imaged immediately. Confocal images were acquired
using a Nikon Ti microscope equipped with a 100× Plan Apo oil-
immersion objective (NA 1.49) and four diode lasers (405, 488, 561,
and 639 nm). Samples were illuminated using highly inclined
laminated optical sheet (HILO) total internal reflection fluorescence
(TIRF) illumination with 488 and 561 nm lasers and corresponding
filter cubes (Chroma). EGFP fluorescence was collected over an
emission range of 503−541 nm with a line time of 0.39 μs and a gain
of 600−1,000. mCherry fluorescence was collected over an emission
range of 571−625 nm using identical acquisition settings. A line
averaging factor of 4 was applied to both channels. Image processing
and analysis were performed using NIS-Elements (version 2.0).
Statistics and Reproducibility
All experimental data were at least in triplicate and expressed as mean
± standard error. All data analyses were performed by GraphPad
Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, USA).
