ABSTRACT: The adenosine 5′-monophosphate-mimicking nucleoside
sulfamates are pharmaceutically important compounds that target
aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases (aaRSs) and other adenylate-forming
enzymes. Ascamycin (1) is a unique naturally occurring alanyl adenosine
sulfamate isolated from Streptomyces sp. 80H647. In this study, we
successfully enhanced the in vivo conversion of dealanylascamycin (2) to
ascamycin by constitutively expressing an extra copy of bldA and adpA
regulatory genes. More importantly, we performed gene expression
analysis and protein structure−function prediction to identify alanyl-
tRNA synthetase (AlaRS)-like enzymes: AcmF and AcmD. AcmF
efficiently catalyzes the aminoacylation reaction during ascamycin
biosynthesis, and this reaction is dependent on the Ala-tRNAAla supplied
by AcmD. AcmF is a truncated AlaRS-like enzyme containing only the aminoacylation and tRNA recognition domains, whereas
AcmD has an intact AlaRS-like architecture that efficiently supports AcmF activity by exhibiting resistance to the AcmF-produced
ascamycin. In addition, we have produced the glycyl- and seryl-derivatives of ascamycin using the AcmD-AcmF enzyme pair. The
mechanistic study identified Glu232 and several residues that play important roles in AcmF catalysis. To the best of our knowledge,
AcmF is a representative class II aaRS-like enzyme catalyzing tRNA-dependent aminoacylation during natural product biosynthesis.
These findings provide a potential chemoenzymatic approach for the synthesis of aminoacyl nucleoside sulfamates.
KEYWORDS: alanyl-tRNA synthetase, aminoacylation, ascamycin, BldA-AdpA regulation, nucleoside sulfamate, Streptomyces
■INTRODUCTION
The nucleoside sulfamates are a class of pharmaceutically
important compounds that inhibit protein synthesis in both
bacteria and malaria parasites.1−3 Recently, Xie et al. reported
that the synthetic compound ML901 (Scheme 1) specifically
targets tyrosine-tRNA synthetase in malaria parasite Plasmo-
dium falciparum via a reaction-hijacking mechanism.3 They also
found that the adenosine 5′-monophosphate (AMP)-mimick-
ing adenosine-5′-sulfamate (AMS) compound could be
converted to multiple amino acid-AMS conjugates presumably
by the enzymes in P. falciparum.3 Because the S−O bonds of
AMS are more stable than the native P−O bonds of aminoacyl-
AMPs, the aminoacyl-AMSs are often chemically synthesized
for mechanistic and crystallographic studies of aminoacyl-
tRNA synthetases (aaRSs) and other adenylate-forming
enzymes.4 In particular, ascamycin (1) and dealanylascamycin
(AT-265, 2) are unique, naturally occurring adenosine
sulfamate metabolites isolated from Streptomyces sp. 80H647
at RIKEN.5,6 They show structural and genetic similarity to
nucleocidin (Scheme 1), which is another natural nucleoside
sulfamate antibiotic isolated from Streptomyces.7 Moreover, 1
contains an L-alanine moiety bound to the 5′-O-sulfamate
group of 2, representing an aminoacyl nucleoside sulfamate.
However, the production yield of 1 is relatively low in the wild-
type strain, and the biochemical mechanism underlying the
aminoacylation reaction during the 1 biosynthesis remains
unclear.
The aaRSs are essential housekeeping enzymes for protein
synthesis. They function via a conserved two-step reaction
mechanism:8−10 the activation of the cognate amino acid by
adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and the transfer of activated
amino acid to the cognate tRNAs (Figure S1A). Given the
ubiquity of these enzymes in almost all living organisms, the
aaRSs are also becoming attractive therapeutic targets.9,10
Generally, naturally occurring aaRSs consist of a Rossmann-
fold (class I) or β-stranded fold (class II) catalytic domain for
the aminoacylation reaction, and an anticodon binding domain
for tRNA interaction.10 The class II alanyl-tRNA synthetase
(AlaRS) architecture, for example, shows the presence of N-

The Streptomyces spp. are important sources of antibiotics
and other bioactive secondary metabolites and are known for
their complex life cycle. Notably, some Streptomyces strains
have the “bald” phenotype, are unable to form sporulating
aerial mycelia, and produce secondary metabolites, owing to
defective bldA or adpA genes.16−19 The bldA gene encodes the
only tRNALeu (UUA) molecule that is essential for the
translation of the rare TTA codon in GC-rich Streptomyces
genomes (Figure 1A), whereas the adpA gene activates the
expression of multiple genes including the bldA gene by
binding to their promoters (Figure 1B).16−19 Therefore, both
bldA and adpA genes function as global transcriptional
regulators for morphological differentiation and antibiotic
production.16−19 For example, nucleocidin production is
known to be dependent on the bldA and adpA genes.17,19 In
the bldA-deficient Streptomyces calvus ATCC 13382 and adpA-
deficient Streptomyces asterosporus DSM 41452 strains, in
which nucleocidin production is abolished, the production can
be restored by complementation with functional bldA and
adpA genes, respectively.17,19
In this study, we constitutively expressed an extra copy of
bldA and adpA genes in Streptomyces sp. 80H647 with the aim
to enhance the conversion of 2 to 1. We combined gene
expression analysis and protein structure−function prediction
to identify enzymes involved in 1 biosynthesis. We report the
identification of AcmF, which is an AlaRS-like enzyme that
catalyzes the aminoacylation reaction, and AcmD, which is
another AlaRS-like enzyme that supplies Ala-tRNAAla to AcmF
for 1 biosynthesis. Additionally, we were able to produce
glycyl-ascamycin (3) and seryl-ascamycin (4) through AcmD-
AcmF enzyme pair-catalyzed reactions. Furthermore, we report
the identification of Glu232 and several important catalytic
residues underlying AcmF activity through a mechanistic study.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Constitutive Expression of bldA-adpA Regulator
Genes Promoted Conversion of Dealanylascamycin to
Ascamycin in Streptomyces sp. 80H647. Considering the
structural similarity between 1, 2, and nucleocidin, and the
genetic similarity of the acm and nuc gene clusters (Scheme 1),
we speculate that bldA and adpA genes may also regulate 1
production. To examine the functionality of these genes, the
wild-type Streptomyces sp. 80H647 strain was grown on a
mannitol soy (MS) agar plate and sporulated well. We
retrieved the sequence of the bldA gene from the Streptomyces
sp. 80H647 genome and predicted the secondary structure of
its product (Figure 1A). The A21G point mutation, which
resulted in defective bldA in S. calvus ATCC 13382 (Figure
S2A), was not detected in the Streptomyces sp. 80H647 bldA
gene, and the tRNALeu (UUA) product folded properly.17
Furthermore, we located the adpA gene in the Streptomyces sp.
80H647 genome and did not find an insertion of transposons
in the putative promoter region, which incidentally resulted in
defective adpA in S. asterosporus DSM 41452 (Figure S2B).19
The predicted structure of the Streptomyces sp. 80H647 AdpA
protein was compared with the crystal structure of Streptomyces
griseus AdpA, and proper folding was observed (Figure 1B).

We performed quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)
analysis of the Streptomyces sp. 80H647 strains transformed
with pKU492::rpsLp-adpA-bldA80H647 and pKU492 empty
vector using gene specific primers (Table S3). We found a
2.1-fold increase in the mRNA transcript level of acmF,
whereas acmD did not show a significant fold change in
expression (Figure 1D). We also observed a 0.74-fold decrease
in the expression of acmE. We examined the upstream

179 bp (5′-TGGCAATTAC-3′) and 19 bp (5′-TGGCT-
GGAAC-3′) upstream of acmE coding region, and one site at
the 284 bp (5′-TGGCCCTATC-3′) upstream of the acmF
coding region.24,25 Although AdpA protein binding to the
promoter region can recruit RNA polymerase and facilitate
gene transcription, it can also hinder RNA polymerase access
to the promoter depending on the expression level.26,27 On the
other hand, the bldA gene, which encodes the tRNALeu (UAA)
molecule, is unlikely to be directly involved in the regulation of
the acmE and acmF genes due to the absence of rare TTA
codons in their sequences (Scheme 1A).

AlaRS-like AcmD and AcmF Cooperate to Catalyze
the Aminoacylation Reaction in a tRNA-dependent
Manner, whereas AcmE is a Hydrolase. To verify our
predictions, we isolated substrate 2 from Streptomyces sp.
80H647 transformed with pKU492::rpsLp-adpA-bldA80H647
(Figures S10−S12). We also purified the His-tagged
recombinant proteins from E. coli for in vitro enzyme reactions
(Table S4, Figure S13). As expected, 1 was synthesized
efficiently when 2 was incubated with AcmF, AcmD, L-alanine,
ATP, and an E. coli tRNA mixture containing all aminoacyl-
tRNAs (Figure 3A, Figure S14), with a catalytic efficiency
(kcat/KM) of 20.87 ± 0.25 min−1 mM−1 (Figure S15). In
contrast, no activity was detected when the reaction involved
AcmF alone or a combination of other recombinant proteins
(AcmD, AlaRS2, or AcmE) was used (Figure 3A), consistent
with the lack of ATP binding residues in AcmF for amino acid
activation (Figure 2B).
Interestingly, AlaRS2 and E. coli AlaRS from the S30 extract
system were also able to support AcmF activity, albeit at a
much lower efficiency than that of AcmD (Figure 3A, Figure
S16). We speculated that this is because AlaRS2 and E. coli
AlaRS are more sensitive to the AcmF-produced 1 in the
reaction system.2−4 To verify this hypothesis, we performed
enzyme reactions of the AcmD-AcmF and AlaRS2-AcmF pairs
under different concentrations of substrate 2 (Figure 3B). We
found that AcmD activity could efficiently link with AcmF to
produce more than 200 μM of 1. However, AlaRS2 activity
coupled with AcmF could produce only up to 50 μM of 1.
Notably, the production of 1 can be recovered by increasing
the concentration of AlaRS2 in the reaction mixture (Figure
S17). AcmD is more resistant to 1 than AlaRS2 (Figure 3B),
probably because the active site cavity of tRNA aminoacylation
in AcmD is reactively closed in the protein structure (Figure
3C, Figure S6).

Currently, aminoacyl-tRNA-dependent enzymes involved in
natural product biosynthesis can be broadly divided into three
groups: the transferase enzymes with GNAT fold, the
dehydratase enzymes with LanB-like fold, and the cyclo-
dipeptide synthases with class I aaRSs-like Rossmann-fold.36
One recently reported aaRSs-like enzyme is the class I
isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase-like SbzA, which catalyzes the
transfer of L-isoleucine moiety from Ile-tRNAIle in SB-203208
biosynthesis.37 Thus, AcmF is the first reported representative
of a class II aaRS-like enzyme adopting the β-stranded fold.
Moreover, it is important to note that the truncated AcmF and
the aminoacyl nucleoside sulfamate 1-resistant AcmD
(compared to AlaRS2) work separately but cooperatively to
aminoacylate the noncanonical substrate 2 (Scheme 1B, Figure
3C), probably to avoid reaction hijacking like that observed
during ML901-Tyr complex formation using P. falciparum
tyrosyl-tRNA synthetase.3
Furthermore, we tested the substrate specificity of the
AcmD-AcmF enzyme pair against that of the other 19 standard
proteinogenic amino acids besides L-alanine. As a result, only L-
glycine and L-serine were accepted as substrates with lower
efficiency to form the glycyl-ascamycin (3) and seryl-ascamycin
(4) (Figure 4C, Figure S18−19). This can be explained by the
fact that the initial amino acid activation is selectively
performed by AcmD and that AlaRSs tend to misrecognize
L-glycine and L-serine as L-alanine at lower affinity.11
Additionally, we removed the 35-amino acid N-terminal
signal peptide sequence from AcmE (Figure S8B), and we
obtained the recombinant protein of AcmEΔN35 from E. coli
expression (Figure S13). We also confirmed its hydrolase
activity in cleaving the L-alanine moiety of 1 to form 2 in the in
vitro assay (Figure S20), which is consistent with our
prediction.
Mechanistic Study of AcmF. To gain further structure−
function insights into the AcmF-catalyzed aminoacylation
reaction in 1 biosynthesis, we performed Ligplot analysis to
visualize the protein−ligand interactions based on the
molecular docking results (Figure S21). We observed that
the carboxyl group of the Glu232 residue interacts and holds
the α-NH3
+ group of the alanyl moiety by hydrogen bonds
(Figure 5A, Figure S21A). The similar interaction was also
previously reported in the cocrystallization of E. coli AlaRS and
alanyl-AMS, in which the acidic Asp235 residue is essential to
hold the α-amino group of the bound amino acid for the
aminoacylation reaction (Figure 2C).11 We also observed that
the conserved Arg93 residue may interact with the oxygen
atom of the alanyl moiety during Ala-tRNAAla substrate
binding (Figure 5A, Figure S21A), although this residue does
not appear to be essential for the interaction with substrate 2
(Figure 5A, Figure S21B). This is different from the normal
amino acid activation step of AlaRSs, in which the Arg residue
facilitates the nucleophilic attack of the L-alanine α-carboxylate
oxygen on the ATP α-phosphate group in
L-alanyl-AMP
formation.11,38 Instead, hydrogen bonds are formed between
AcmF and the product 1 (Figure 5A, Figure S21C), similar to
the previous report observed in E. coli AlaRS and alanyl-AMS
(Figure 2C).11 In addition, we found certain nonligand
residues such as Phe104, Trp177, Tyr179, Gln219, Glu216,
Ile221, and Gly234 that likely contributed to the formation of
the hydrophobic active site cavity to enable the sulfamate NH−
group attack of substrate 2 on the activated oxy-ester bonds of
the Ala-tRNAAla substrate provided by AcmD (Figure 5A).3
Besides, AcmF has an additional Trp100 residue compared
with other AlaRSs (Figure 2C), which may help to stabilize the
binding of substrate 2 by π−π stacking interactions.
Next, we performed alanine substitution in these residues in
the active site cavity of AcmF, purified the His-tagged mutants
from E. coli (Figure S22), and performed in vitro assays with
AcmD. We found that the E232A mutant of AcmF was
completely inactive to produce 1; however, the activity was
restored by substitution with a functionally equivalent Asp
residue (Figure 5B). The R93A mutation exerted almost no
effect on AcmF activity, in accordance with our proposed
catalytic mechanism (Figure 5A). Notably, substitution of the
Gly234 residue to a larger Ala residue resulted in an 82% loss
of AcmF activity, probably due to the spatial shrinkage in the
binding pocket for the alanyl moiety of the Ala-tRNAAla
substrate.11 The Ala-substitution mutants of Trp100, Phe104,
Trp177, Tyr179, Glu216, Gln219, and Ile221 showed 29−57%
loss of activity to produce 1 in the in vitro assays (Figure 4B),
which can be attributed to the effects on hydrophobic
interactions and stabilization of the substrates during the
aminoacylation reaction. Finally, we performed alanine
substitution of the Lys295 and Glu374 residues that are
putatively responsible for Ala-tRNAAla substrate interaction
(Figure S7).14 We found a 76% loss of activity in the E374A
mutant (Figure S23), suggesting that the Glu374 residue is
important for the Ala-tRNAAla interaction. In contrast, the
Lys295 residue did not seem to be involved in AcmF activity.
However, given the complexity of the aaRS-tRNA inter-
action,12,35 it is worthwhile to cocrystallize AcmF with the
dealanylascamycin (2) and Ala-tRNAAla ligands in the future
for more detailed mechanistic analysis and enzyme design.
■CONCLUSIONS
In conclusion, we have successfully enhanced the conversion of
dealanylascamycin (2) to ascamycin (1) in the Streptomyces sp.
80H647 strain by introducing an extra copy of bldA and adpA
regulatory genes for constitutive expression. We have predicted
AcmF to be a truncated AlaRS-like enzyme consisting of only
N-terminal aminoacylation and tRNA-recognition domains.
Furthermore, we have identified its activity in catalyzing the
aminoacylation reaction in ascamycin (1) biosynthesis during
an in vitro enzymatic reaction, in which another AlaRS-like
enzyme AcmD acts as the supplier of Ala-tRNAAla for AcmF.
Additionally, we produced glycyl-ascamycin (3) and seryl-
ascamycin (4) derivatives using the AcmD-AcmF enzyme pair.
The mechanistic study of AcmF identified Glu232 and several
important catalytic residues essential for the recognition of the
nucleoside sulfamate group of dealanylascamycin (2) on the
alanyl moiety of Ala-tRNAAla during the aminoacylation
reaction. Taken together, our report of AcmF-AcmD
represents the first class II aaRSs-like enzymes that catalyze
aminoacylation reactions during the biosynthesis of natural
products. Furthermore, our study sheds light on the future
enzymatic design of aminoacyl nucleoside sulfamates for future
drug development.
