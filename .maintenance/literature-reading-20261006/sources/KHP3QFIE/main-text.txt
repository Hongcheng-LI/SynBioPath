Liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS)-mediated formation of membraneless, biomolecule-rich organelles formed via LLPS is critical to 
numerous biological processes, but how this spatiotemporal control enables efficient catalysis by biosynthetic enzymes to produce natural 
products (NPs) remains poorly understood. Here, we demonstrate that two C7/C6-dimethylallyl tryptophan synthases (DMATSs), IsaA/InaA 
and a tryptophanase IsaB, from Streptomyces possess intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) to undergo LLPS. IDRs from IsaA/InaA are 
required for enzyme activity, while the IDR from IsaB is not, but it mediates enzyme interaction and preferentially undergoes inter-IDR 
condensation with IsaA, leading to stronger LLPS and efficient catalysis of cascade reactions to produce isoprenyl alkaloids. These prop­
erties were leveraged for enzyme engineering with the IsaA IDR to enhance the catalytic efficiency of taxadiene synthase, and with 
interactive IDRs from IsaA/IsaB to improve the production of taxadiene and farnesyl-pyrophosphate. Our work proposes LLPS-mediated 
catalysis regulation and demonstrates new orthogonal toolkits for efficient biosynthesis of isoprenoid NPs.

INTRODUCTION  
Liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) is a biophysical process 
whereby biomolecules form membraneless condensates (Alber­
ti et al., 2019). In microbes, LLPS is involved in cell division, 
pathogenesis, and host fitness, etc. (Krypotou et al., 2023). 
Particularly, some microbial cells biosynthesize natural pro­
ducts (NPs), which often have intriguing bioactivities, to 
function as physiological modulators and chemical weapons 
of producers, and also contribute substantially to human 
health and life (van Bergeijk et al., 2020). It is well known that 
the biosynthetic enzymes are responsible for the assembly of 
these structurally complex NPs from simple metabolic inter­
mediates through cascade catalysis (Seshadri et al., 2025). 
Nevertheless, how LLPS is involved in the catalysis of 
biosynthetic enzymes for the efficient production of NPs 
remains elusive. 
Here, we focused on dimethylallyl tryptophan synthases 
(DMATSs), which catalyze the transfer of dimethylallyl pyropho­
sphate (DMAPP) to ʟ-Trp. It is speculated that LLPS-mediated 
protein condensation might establish distinctive local physico­
chemical microenvironments, which will effectively exclude 
water molecules to stabilize carbocation (Schvartzman et al., 
2023), increase local concentrations of reactants, and accom­
modate the augmented hydrophobicity of products (Luo et al., 
2020). These hints suggest that LLPS might be essential for 
efficient catalysis of DMATSs. 
In Streptomyces sp. neau-D50 (Zhang et al., 2014), with the 
identification of several new isoprenoid alkaloids, we demon­
strate that two endogenous C7/C6-DMATSs, IsaA/InaA, contain 
native IDRs to drive enzyme LLPS and are required for enzyme 
catalytic activity. More interestingly, two IDRs from the C7- 
DMATS IsaA and a tryptophanase IsaB favor inter-IDR 
condensation to undergo stronger LLPS and mediate enzyme 
interaction for the efficient cascade reaction. Engineering of 
exogenous biosynthetic enzymes with IDRs from IsaA/IsaB 
remarkably promotes production of isoprenoid NPs, including 
taxadiene and farnesyl-pyrophosphate (FPP). Thus, our work 
elucidates a novel catalytic regulation involving the intrinsic 
spatiotemporal organization of biosynthetic enzymes, and 
provides new synthetic biology toolkits for efficient production 
of NPs.

RESULTS  
Native IDRs of DMATSs drive LLPS essential for catalysis 
and enable enzyme engineering for isoprenoid production  
Proteins undergoing LLPS often have intrinsically disordered 
regions (IDRs) with disordered and highly flexible structures 
(Holehouse and Kragelund, 2024; Li et al., 2025). AlphaFold3- 
based protein structure modeling was performed on 22 microbial 
DMATSs (An et al., 2023), and 20 DMATSs have terminal 
unstructured IDRs with various lengths (Figure S1 and Table S1), 
suggesting that DMATSs generally have unstructured IDRs and 
the competence to undergo LLPS. 
A bacterial strain, Streptomyces sp. neau-D50, has been 
reported to produce C6/C7- dimethylallyl (DMA) indole deriva­
tives (Zhang et al., 2014); thus, it should contain DMATS(s) and 
cascade biosynthetic enzymes. After genome sequencing, two 
putative DMATSs, IsaA (ORF35285) and InaA (ORF32400), 
were identified (Figure 1A). isaA is in an operon with isaB 
(orf35290), encoding a tryptophanase (TnaA) to catalyze C3- 
C10 bond cleavage of tryptophan to produce indole (Snell, 1975), 
while inaA is co-localized with orf32405, encoding a flavin- 
dependent monooxygenase. 
In silico sequence analysis and structure modeling showed that 
both DMATSs have IDRs at their N- and C-termini (Figure 1B). 
These four IDRs were expressed and purified in fusion with GFP, 
and three of them (IDR IsaAn, IDR IsaAc, and IDR InaAc, 30 μmol/L) 
could form round droplets with 10% PEG8000, but these droplets 
disassemble with 10% 1,6-hexanediol (1,6-HEX) (Figure 1C). 
Droplet fusion and rapid fluorescence recovery after photobleach­
ing (FRAP) (Figures S2 and S3) further support that these IDRs 
undergo typical LLPS in a dynamic and reversible manner. 
However, IDR InaAn did not form droplets with 10% PEG8000 
(Figure S4). Moreover, when GFP-IsaA and GFP-InaA were 
expressed in E. coli, they showed apparent green condensate spots 
within cells, as well as effective disassembly with 20% 1,6-HEX 
(Figure 1D). Additional FRAP assays showed that the fluores­
cence from these GFP-fusion proteins in E. coli could partially 
recover after photobleaching, in contrast to the GFP protein, 
which had no detectable FRAP (Figure S5). All the above data 
suggest that both enzymes undergo LLPS.  
To functionally characterize IDRs of DMATSs for enzyme 
catalysis, we initially deleted the biosynthetic gene cluster of 
borrelidin (bor) (Liu et al., 2012) (Figure S6), and subsequently 
overexpressed the isaA-isaB operon. C7-DMA-ʟ-Trp (1), C7-DMA- 
indole (3), and its oxidative derivatives (4, 5) were predomi­
nantly produced, along with two new isoprenoid alkaloids C7, 
C7’-DMA-indigo/indirubin (8, 9) as dimers (Figure 1Ei–iii and F). 
Their structures were determined based on NMR and crystal data 
(Figure S7–S34 and Tables S2–S5). Meanwhile, deletion of the 
isaA-isaB operon (Figure S35) along with co-overexpression of 
both inaA and isaB led to the production of new isoprenoid 
alkaloids C6-DMA-ʟ-Trp (2), C6-DMA-indole derivatives (6, 7), 
and C6, C6’-DMA-indigo/indirubin (10, 11) (Figure 1Eiv–v and 
F; Figures S7, S36–S57 and Table S6).  
Further in vitro assays confirmed the catalytic activities of 
IsaA/InaA to produce 1, 3 and 2, 6, respectively (Figure G, Hi–v 
and ix). Interestingly, removal of the N-, C-, or both terminal 
IDRs of IsaA completely abolished its activity (Figure 1Gvi–viii). 
Moreover, deletion of the C-terminal or both N-/C-terminal IDRs 
of InaA also abolished its activity (Figure 1Hvii, viii). However, as 
IDR InaAn did not undergo LLPS (Figure S4), deletion of this IDR 
did not affect enzyme activity (Figure 1Hvi). These observations 
were consistent with in vivo data that compounds 1, 2 and 4–11 
were not produced if one or both IDRs of IsaA (Figure S58A) or 
the C-terminal IDR of InaA (Figure S58B) were truncated and 
overexpressed along with isaB in Streptomyces. To exclude the 
possibility of protein misfolding, we constructed gradual C- 
terminal IDR truncations of IsaA (Δ5, Δ10, and Δ15 aa). While 
all variants maintained robust soluble expression, which 
indicates intact folding (Figure S59A), they exhibited a synchro­
nized, length-dependent loss of both in vivo LLPS capability 
(Figure S59B) and in vitro catalytic activity (Figure S59C). 
Notably, the soluble IsaA Δ15 aa mutation completely abolished 
both LLPS formation and enzymatic activity, demonstrating that 
IDR-driven LLPS is essential for DMATS catalysis.   
Next, we aimed to expand the application of IDRs for the 
efficient production of NPs, especially isoprenoids, whose 
biosynthetic mechanisms are similar to those of DMATSs. 
IDR IsaAc was linked to the C-terminus of a taxadiene synthase 
(TS) from Taxus brevifolia to produce the paclitaxel diterpene 
backbone from geranyl-geranyl-pyrophosphate (GGPP) (Ajiku­
mar et al., 2010) (Figure S60A). When expressed in E. coli and 
under the facilitation of a GGPP synthase (GGPPS) (Bian et al., 
2017) to supply GGPP, the fusion enzyme (TS-IDR IsaAc) produced 
about 4-fold more taxadiene (Figure 1I; Figure S61A). To rule out 
generic fusion tag effects, TS was alternatively fused with a 
highly soluble protein (GFP) or a non-phase-separating IDR 
(IDR InaAn) as negative controls. Neither control improved 
taxadiene production compared with the wild-type TS (Figure 
S60B), confirming that the observed catalytic enhancement is 
specifically driven by IDR IsaAc-mediated LLPS rather than mere 
protein stabilization. This significantly improved enzyme activity 
was further confirmed by in vitro assays (Figure 1J). Along with 
the purified GGPPS to produce GGPP from the substrates FPP and 
isopentenyl diphosphate (IPP), the purified enzymes (TS, TS- 
IDR IsaAc) were able to catalyze taxadiene production, and fusion 
of IDR IsaAc to TS resulted in about one-fold higher productivity 
(Figure S61B). 
LLPS mediates enzyme interaction for efficient isoprenyl 
alkaloid biosynthesis and serves as a toolkit to enhance the 
catalytic efficiency of biosynthetic enzymes  
Interestingly, we found that another enzyme, tryptophanase 
IsaB, also contains an IDR at its C-terminus (Figure 2A). IDR IsaBc- 
RFP was expressed in homogeneity and showed typical LLPS 
features, including droplet formation with 10% PEG8000 and 
droplet disassembly by 10% 1,6-HEX (Figure 2B). Droplet fusion 
and rapid FRAP further confirmed that IDR IsaBc could undergo 
typical LLPS (Figure S62). Moreover, when GFP-IsaB was 
expressed in E. coli, it displayed bright green spots within cells, 
and the fluorescence diminished with 20% 1,6-HEX (Figure 2C). 
In addition, partial FRAP was observed in vivo (Figure S63). 
These data demonstrate that IsaB also undergoes LLPS. 
Our in vitro assays showed that IsaB could only use ʟ-Trp as a 
substrate to produce indole, like other tryptophanases (Snell, 
1975), but did not recognize C7-DMA-ʟ-Trp (1) (Figure 2Di–vi). 
Meanwhile, we found that the IDR-truncation form of IsaB 
(IsaB ΔIDRc) exhibited comparable catalytic efficiency to the intact 
IsaB (Figure 2Dvii), suggesting that this IDR is not essential for 
the tryptophanase activity of IsaB. However, when IsaB ΔIDRc was

Next, we investigated how this interaction might contribute to 
LLPS. When GFP-IDR IsaAc and RFP-IDR IsaBc were mixed along 
with 10% PEG8000, only yellow droplets, but no green or red 
droplets, were found on the merged images, when compared with 
the control groups (Figure 2F). Moreover, the yellow droplets 
were much bigger (average area=18.44 m 2) than those in the 
controls (average area=0.31 and 2.08 m 2, respectively) (Figure 
2G), suggesting that these two IDRs are more prone to inter-IDR 
condensation than self-assembly and form stronger LLPS. 
Further droplet fusion assays support that the mixed IDRs 
undergo typical LLPS (Figure S64), and these droplets showed 
faster FRAP than the control groups (Figure S64), suggesting 
better fluidity of droplets from the mixed IDRs. These data suggest 
that IDR IsaBc plays a distinct role from IDRs of DMATSs by 
mediating enzyme-enzyme interaction to strengthen LLPS, 
thereby enabling an efficient cascade reaction for isoprenoid 
alkaloid production. 
These data prompted us to further investigate whether the 
two inter-condensation IDRs from IsaA/IsaB represent a new 
“plug-and-play” toolkit for cascade enzyme engineering to 
produce NPs more efficiently, as they enable effective relay of 
the cascade enzymes. For this purpose, GGPPS was C- 
terminally linked to IDR IsaBc, which works along with TS- 
IDR IsaAc (Figure S60A). Interestingly, this fusion protein 
combination further boosted taxadiene production and showed 
about 12-fold and 2-fold higher taxadiene production than the 
native free enzyme pairs in E. coli and in vitro assays, 
respectively (Figure 2H and I; Figure S65). The C-terminal 
fusion orientation for both enzymes was selected based on a 
systematic screening of all possible N- and C-terminal 
combinations, which revealed that only simultaneous C- 
terminal fusions significantly increased pathway flux (Figure 
S60C). This configuration likely provides the necessary 
conformational flexibility for inter-IDR condensation-driven 
LLPS. 
To further generalize the application of these IDRs to improve 
the production of isoprenoid NPs, we selected enzymes Idi and 
IspA to produce FPP in a cascade reaction, where IPP is 
interconvertible to DMAPP by Idi and polymerized with DMAPP 
to form FPP by IspA (Wang et al., 2022). Here, IspA and Idi were 
expressed in fusion with IDR IsaAc and IDR IsaBc, respectively, in 
Saccharomyces cerevisiae, along with reinforcement of the 
mevalonate pathway to supply IPP (Figure S66). We found that 
the introduction of this IDR pair remarkably enhanced FPP 
production by about 3-fold (Figures S67 and S68). This 
observation was confirmed by in vitro assays, where IspA- 
IDR IsaAc and Idi-IDR IsaBc catalyzed the production of about 2.5- 
fold higher FPP from IPP, compared with the free enzymes IspA 
and Idi (Figures S69 and S70). 
DISCUSSION  
Microbes produce plentiful NPs, usually with complex chemical 
structures and intriguing bioactivities (Seshadri et al., 2025). 
NPs are assembled by biosynthetic enzymes through their highly 
efficient and ordered catalysis. Thus, most chemo-biologists have 
been trying to understand the biosynthetic pathways of NPs, as 
well as the catalytic mechanisms of these enzymes, e.g., the 
catalytic roles of enzymes in chemical reactions within living 
organisms (Seshadri et al., 2025; Tang et al., 2017). However, 
their catalytic regulation has always been neglected. Recent 
studies have shown that LLPS-mediated cellular compartmenta­
tion regulates enzyme activities in primary metabolism (O’Flynn 
and Mittag, 2021). For example, ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate 
carboxylase/oxygenase complex (Rubisco) forms condensates 
via LLPS, which are essential for carboxysome biogenesis and 
CO 2 fixation (Wang et al., 2019). However, these enzymes 
cannot undergo LLPS by themselves. LLPS development of 
Rubisco is externally facilitated by a carboxysome-shell protein 
CcmM or IDR-containing proteins CsoS2/EPYC1 (Turnšek et al., 
2024; Zang et al., 2021). Given that there is no accessory 
biomolecule (proteins or RNA) in the system for NP biosynthesis, 
we found that biosynthetic enzymes intrinsically possess IDRs 
that drive autogenous LLPS, and the capacity to undergo LLPS is 
essential for enzyme activity, even though the functional 
domains are intact. 
These findings have encouraged us to engineer biosynthetic 
enzymes. Simply anchoring IDR to a taxadiene synthase 
apparently improved the enzyme activity of these chimeric 
enzymes. This strategy would be particularly useful for promot­
ing the catalytic efficiency of enzymes in biosynthetic reactions 
where LLPS might be involved, for instance, by stabilizing the 
carbocation transients and creating a hydrophobic microenvir­
onment as proposed herein. Meanwhile, these IDRs can also be 
rationally designed and optimized as standard “plug and play” 
modules in synthetic biology to regulate enzyme catalysis, 
enabling more compatible and efficient production of NPs, as 
some recent work has explored (Lim and Clark, 2024; Wan et al., 
2023).  
One more interesting finding is that IDRs from IsaA and IsaB 
are more prone to undergo stronger LLPS through inter-IDR 
condensation rather than self-assembly, and these two IDRs also 
mediate the direct interaction between IsaA/IsaB, as they 
catalyze cascade reactions from ʟ-Trp to DMA-indole. This more 
robust LLPS would ultimately enhance the spatiotemporal 
concentrations of enzymes and substrates and benefit the 
creation of a hydrophobicity-demanding microenvironment 
and the seamless delivery of intermediates between enzymes.  
Recently, there have also been many efforts to develop 
orthogonally phase-separation-promoting proteins to cluster 
metabolic enzymes into synthetic compartments for efficient 
biosynthesis of NPs (Wan et al., 2023) or create biosynthetic 
communicating membrane-less organelles for on-demand deliv­
ery of molecules (Ji et al., 2025), based on some well-studied but 
randomly selected LLPS-forming proteins. We found that two 
IDRs from IsaA/IsaB are evolutionarily compatible and sponta­
neously undergo stronger LLPS via inter-IDR condensation. 
Moreover, IDR IsaAc/IDR IsaBc are relatively small (23 and 47 aa, 
respectively), compared with the widely used RGG domain 
(168 aa) and its tandem repeat RGGRGG from Caenorhabditis 
elegans protein LAF-1 (Elbaum-Garfinkle et al., 2015), and would 
be advantageous over others to have less spatial interference with 
enzymes for their interaction and catalysis. We demonstrated 
that this IDR pair promotes NP biosynthesis more prominently, at 
least for taxadiene and FPP production. These advantages 
suggest that IDR IsaAc/IDR IsaBc could be developed into a native 
orthogonal toolkit for constructing biomolecular organelles 
through heterotypic interactions in future synthetic biology 
applications (Dai et al., 2023).

MATERIALS AND METHODS   
Strains and growth conditions  
All strains listed in this study are shown in Table S7, while the 
plasmids and primers used are provided in Tables S8 and S9, 
respectively. Streptomyces mycelia were cultured in liquid tryptic 
soy broth (TSB) supplemented with 5% PEG6000, while 
compound production was carried out in GYM medium contain­
ing 0.4% glucose, 0.4% yeast extract, 1% malt extract, 0.2% 
CaCO 3, and 2% soluble amylum. Streptomyces strains were 
incubated at 30°C with shaking at 250 r min −1. Sporulation of 
Streptomyces was facilitated using the MS solid medium. E. coli 
strains were grown in either Luria-Bertani (LB) medium or 
Terrific Broth (TB) medium with appropriate antibiotics. Anti­
biotics were added for selection at the following concentrations: 
100 μg/mL ampicillin, 34 μg/mL chloramphenicol, 50 μg/mL 
hygromycin, 50 μg/mL apramycin, and 50 μg/mL kanamycin. 
An engineered Saccharomyces cerevisiae strain BJ5464-NpgA-M 
(Cao et al., 2021) with four auxotrophs (ura3-, trp1-, leu2-, his3-) 
was used for FPP production. Yeast strain BJ5464-NpgA-M and 
its derivatives were routinely grown in YPD medium at 30°C 
with shaking at 220 r min −1. Synthetic dropout (SD) media (2% 
glucose, 0.5% ammonium sulfate, 0.17% yeast nitrogen base 
without amino acids) supplemented with appropriate nutrients 
were used for plasmid selection. For FPP production, transfor­
mants were first cultured in the corresponding SD dropout 
medium, then transferred to modified YPD (1% glucose, 1% yeast 
extract, 2% peptone) and fermented at 150 r min −1, 30°C. After 
16–18 h, dodecane (1 mL) was added to extract FPP. 
Bacterial two-hybrid assays  
The fragments isaA, isaA Δidrc, idr isaAc genes were recombined into 
pUT18, and the fragments isaB, isaB Δidrc, idr isaBc, idr atrac were 
inserted into pKT25. Then, the two plasmids (pUT18-gene 
+pKT25-gene) were transformed into E. coli BTH101 as 
described previously (Ma et al., 2023). 
In vitro liquid-liquid phase separation assays  
Purified GFP, GFP-IDR IsaAn, GFP-IDR IsaAc, GFP-IDR InaAn, GFP- 
IDR InaAc, RFP, and RFP-IDR IsaBc were dialyzed into a protein 
buffer composed of 50 mmol/L NaH 2PO 4 (pH 7.4), 0.1 mmol/L 
EDTA, 10% glycerol, and 0.1 mmol/L dithiothreitol (DTT). The 
proteins were then diluted to the desired concentrations in a 
droplet formation buffer, which included the protein buffer and 
10% PEG8000 or an additional 10% 1,6-hexanediol (final 
concentration). To observe droplet formation, we applied 5 μL 
of the protein solution to a microscope slide, and fluorescent 
images were captured using a Zeiss LSM 800 microscope. The 
purified GFP-IDR IsaAc and RFP-IDR IsaBc proteins were mixed in a 
buffer containing 10% (w/v) PEG8000, 150 mmol/L NaCl, and a 
final protein concentration ranging from 5 to 50 μmol/L. Images 
were acquired sequentially in the green (GFP) and red (RFP) 
channels and then merged to show colocalization. The co- 
localization of GFP-IDR IsaAc and RFP-IDR IsaBc was indicated by 
the appearance of yellow puncta in the merged images, 
suggesting their incorporation into the same condensates. 
Quantification of the droplets was determined using ImageJ, 
and the following batch analysis parameters were used: image 
type, 8-bit; adjust, auto threshold; method=RenyiEntropy white; 
analyze particles, size=0–infinity; circularity=0.00–1.00.  
FRAP  
FRAP experiments were conducted using a Zeiss LSM 800 
microscope equipped with a 63× oil immersion objective. Purified 
proteins were added to 8-well chamber slides (Cellvis, C8 1.5H-N) 
and photobleached with a laser at 20% intensity at 488 nm (for 
GFP) and 587 nm (for RFP). Fluorescence recovery was recorded 
over a specific time. The prebleached fluorescence intensity was 
normalized to 1, and post-bleaching fluorescence signals were 
normalized to the prebleached level. 
Enzyme assays  
Enzymatic activities of IsaA, InaA, and their respective IDR- 
truncated variants were assessed in 100 μL reaction systems 
containing 50 mmol/L Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 5% glycerol, 5 mmol/L 
MgCl 2, 0.25 mmol/L ʟ-Trp, and 0.1 mmol/L DMAPP. Reactions 
were initiated by adding purified enzyme to a final concentration 
of 10 μmol/L, followed by incubation at 30°C for 2 h. Control 
reactions contained heat-inactivated enzyme (100°C, 10 min). 
All reactions were terminated with 10 μL of 1.5 mol/L trifluor­

General chemical methods    
1H and  13C NMR spectra were recorded on BRUKER AVANCE III 
spectrometers ( 1H at 600 MHz;  13C at 125 or 150 MHz) in CDCl 3 
or DMSO-d 6, with chemical shifts reported in ppm relative to TMS 
as internal standard. High-resolution mass spectra (HRMS) were 
obtained from an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography 
system (Shimadzu, Japan) coupled to an electrospray ionization 
tandem mass spectrometer (AB SCIEX ZENO Q-TOF 7600, 
America) (column: Agilent, TC-C18, 1.6 μm, 30 mm×2.1 mm) 
with a linear gradient of 10%–100% acetonitrile-H 2O (0.1% 
formic acid) in 7 min, followed by 100% MeCN for 3 min with a 
flow rate of 0.3 mL/min, and MS range was 100–1,500 using 
positive and negative mode electrospray ionization. HPLC 
analyses were performed on a Shimadzu LC-20ADXR UFLC 
(UV-vis or ELSD detector, chromatographic separation was 
performed on an Agilent Poroshell SB-C18 column (2.7 μm, 
4.6 mm×150 mm) with a linear gradient of 30%–100% 
acetonitrile in 0.1% formic acid-water over 15 min, then 100% 
acetonitrile for 5 min at 0.3 mL/min. GC/MS analyses were 
carried out on a 7890B/5977A series gas chromatography/mass 
selective detector (Agilent, USA). The GC was equipped with an 
HP5-MS fused silica capillary column (0.25 mm i.d., 0.50 μm 
film; Agilent). The oven temperature was set at 70°C for 5 min, 
then increased to 320°C at a rate of 10°C/min, and finally held at 
320°C for 5 min.

Statistics and reproducibility  
Experimental data were acquired from three independent 
experiments, and representative microscopy images are pre­
sented. Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism 
8 software, with data expressed as mean±SD. A two-tailed 
Student’s t-test was used to compare differences between two 
groups; P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.
