ABSTRACT
Antimycins are a class of depsipeptide compounds that exhibit 
diverse bioactivities. However, their potential clinical applications 
are hampered by high cell toxicities. Glycosylation usually has pro­
found impacts on the physicochemical properties, bioactivities and 
toxicities of natural products. In this study, we overexpressed an 
artificial glycosyltransferase (GT) gene sbmGT1 in deepsea-derived 
Streptomyces albus ZH66, leading to the discovery of three new 
antimycin derivatives glucosylantimycins A, B and C (1–3). Their 
structures were determined by a combination of spectroscopic 
methods, including high resolution electrospray ionisation mass 
spectrometry (HRESIMS) and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) 
analysis. The glycosylation remarkably improved the water solubil­
ity but simultaneously reduced the cytotoxicities of antimycins 
towards human cervix epidermoid carcinoma (HeLa) and human 
hepatic carcinoma (HepG2) cell lines.

1.  Introduction
Glycosylation has been demonstrated to be a useful method for modifying natural 
products, as it can significantly impact the essential pharmacological and pharmaco­
kinetic properties of aglycones, such as water solubility, stability, bioactivity and 
toxicity (Lairson et  al. 2008; Thibodeaux et  al. 2008; Williams et  al. 2009). 
Glycosyltransferases (GTs) are a group of enzymes catalysing the transfer of sugar 
moieties from activated glycosyl moieties onto specific acceptor molecules (Lairson 
et  al. 2008; Liang et  al. 2015; Williams and Thorson 2009). The high efficiency and 
regioselectivity of GTs have attracted much interests in synthetic glycochemistry for 
compound structural glycodiversification (Liang et  al. 2015). Therefore, applying sub­
strate promiscuous GTs in natural products modification may be helpful for drug 
discovery and development (Luzhetskyy et  al. 2008).
Antimycins are a family of di-lactone depsipeptides generated by a hybrid nonri­
bosomal peptide synthetase (NRPS)-polyketide synthase (PKS) assembly line (Seipke 
& Hutchings 2013). Their potent and diverse biological activities, which encompass 
anti-fungal, insecticidal, anti-cancer and anti-inflammatory properties, have garnered 
considerable interest (Pettit et al. 2007; Strangman et al. 2009). However, their further 
development as potential drug candidates is limited to the high cell toxicities (Liu 
et al. 2016). Recently, Several investigations have been impeded to rich their structural 
complexity (Awakawa et  al. 2018; Hu et  al. 2024). Structural modifications, such as 
glycosylation, may give access to antimycin derivatives with altered properties.
In our previous studies, a macrolide GT BmmGT1 from marine-derived Bacillus 
methylotrophicus B-9987 was identified, which exhibits promiscuous substrate flexibility 
with regard to different aglycons, such as 24-membered macrolide macrolactins, 
polyunsaturated enamines bacillaenes and phenolic compounds (Liu et  al. 2018; Qin 
et al. 2014). Moreover, heterologous expression of a BmmGT1-encoding gene sbmGT1 
in marine-derived Streptomyces youssoufiensis OUC6819 led to the production of glu­
copiericidins with potent cytotoxic activities (Liu et al. 2021). Inspired by the substrate 
promiscuity of BmmGT1, to discover novel glycosides, we introduced sbmGT1 into 
different marine-derived Streptomyces strains. Three new antimycin derivatives glu­
cosylantimycins A, B and C (1–3, Figure 1) were thus discovered when sbmGT1 was 
heterologously expressed in deepsea-derived Streptomyces albus ZH66 (previously 
named as Streptomyces somaliensis SCSIO ZH66 (Zhang et  al. 2015)). The water solu­
bilities and cytotoxic activities of compounds 1–3 were then tested.
2.  Results and discussion
Considering the substrate promiscuity of BmmGT1, we constructed an expression 
plasmid pWLI2001 (Table S1), harbouring the codon-optimized sbmGT1 under the 
control of a strong constitutive promoter PgapDH, and then introduced it into different 
deepsea-derived Streptomyces strains. When it was heterologously expressed in S. 
albus ZH66, high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis of the fermen­
tation products showed that, compared to the control ZH66/pIJ10500 (Figure S1A, 
panel ii), ZH66/pWLI2001 accumulated a couple of new compound peaks (Figure S1A, 
panel i) with similar UV spectra (Figure S1B).

In order to elucidate the structures of the accumulated compounds, a large-scale 
fermentation of ZH66/pWLI2001 was performed, from which compounds 1–3 were 
isolated. Their structures were characterised via HRESIMS and NMR assignments. 
Compound 1 was isolated as a yellow, amorphous solid. The molecular formula of 1 
was determined to be C29H42N2O13 according to the HRESIMS data ([M + H]+ at m/z 
627.2773, calcd for [C29H42N2O13]+ at m/z 627.2760, Δm/z = 2.1 ppm; Figure S3). In the 
1H-1H COSY spectrum, we observed two proton spin systems that consist of NH-3 (δH 
9.37)/H-3 (δH 5.30)/H-4 (δH 5.57)/H-11 (δH 1.28), and H-10 (δH 1.39)/H-9 (δH 4.78)/H-8 
(δH 3.69)/H-7 (δH 2.39)/H-1′′ (1.49)/H-2′′ (δH 1.24), respectively (Figures S2 and S6). The 
13C chemical shifts of C-2 (δC 169.6), C-4 (δC 72.1), C-6 (δC 175.4) and C-9 (δC 74.9), 
as well as the 1H chemical shifts of H-4 and H-9 revealed the existence of two ester 
groups. Additionally, the HMBC correlations from NH-3, H-3, H-4, H-9 to C-2, and from 
H-4, H-7, H-8 to C-6 finally assigned the substructure as a nine-membered dilactone 
ring (Figures S2 and S8). The 1H-1H COSY correlations of H-4′ (δH 8.25)/H-5′ (δH 
6.91)/H-6′ (δH 7.88), together with the HMBC correlations from H-5′ to C-1′ (δC 114.1), 
from H-4′ to C-2′ (δC 150.7), from NH-3′ to C-4′ (δC 125.0), and from H-9′, H-5′ to C-3′ 
(127.0) revealed a trisubstituted aromatic ring connected with a NH-CHO moiety at 
C-3′. The downfielded 13C chemical shift value of C-2′ proved a hydroxyl group sub­
stituted. The HMBC correlations from H-2′, H-3 to C-7′ (δC 169.9) revealed these two 
moieties were linked at NH-3′. The 1H-1H COSY correlations of H-5′′ (δH 1.24)/H-6′′ (δH 
0.85), together with the HMBC correlations from H-2′′ (δH 1.15) to C-3′′ (δC 28.6) 
revealed an n-hexyl side chain attached on C-7 of the dilactone ring. The 1H and 13C 
NMR data of compound 1 are similar to those of antimycin A2a (Inai et al. 2011) and 
DA-4 (Yan et al. 2013) except for the substitutions at C8-OH. Consecutive 1H-1H COSY 
correlations of H-1′ (δH 4.31)/H-2′ (δH 2.97)/H-3′ (δH 3.17)/H-4′ (δH 3.02)/H-5′ (δH 3.17)/
H2 −6′ (δH 3.72, 3.41), together with the HMBC correlations from H-5′ (δH 3.17) to C-1′ 
(δC 103.6), and from H-1′′′ (δH 4.31) to C-8 (δC 82.3) indicated a glucosyl group is 
located at C-8. Thus, the planar structure of 1 was established as shown in Figure S2. 
The 1H and 13C chemical shifts of 1 were listed in Table S2.

The NMR assignment of 2 (Figures S2, S11-S16) demonstrated that its structure was 
similar to 1, except the C7-linked alkyl side chain. The 1H-1H COSY correlations of H-7 
(δH 2.35)/H-1′’ (δH 1.46)/H-2′’ (δH 1.02)/H-3′’ (δH 1.48)/H-4′’ (δH 0.84) and H-3′’/H-5′’ (δH

planar structure of 2 was established as shown in Figure S2. The 1H and 13C chemical 
shifts of 2 were listed in Table S2.
Compound 3 was isolated as a yellow, amorphous solid. The molecular formula of 
3 was determined to be C29H42N2O13 according to the HRESIMS data ([M + H]+ at m/z 
627.2789, calcd for [C29H42N2O13]+ at m/z 627.2760, Δm/z = 4.6 ppm; Figure S17). The 
NMR assignments of 3 (Figures S2, S18-S23) revealed that its structure was similar to 
1 and 2, except the alkyl side chain substituted at C-7. The 1H-1H COSY correlations 
of H-7 (δH 2.36)/H-1′’ (δH 2.23)/H-2′’ (δH 1.15)/H-3′’ (δH 1.28)/H-4′’ (δH 1.28)/H-5′’ (δH 
0.80) and H-3′’/H-6′’ (δH 0.82) suggested that a 3-methylhexyl side chain is located at 
C-7. The planar structure of 3 was established as shown in Figure S2. The 1H and 13C 
chemical shifts of 3 were listed in Table S2.
The relative configurations of compounds 1 − 3 were established by nuclear over­
hauser effect spectroscopy (NOESY) correlations (Figure S2). The NOE correlations of 
H-3/H-4, NH-3/H-11, and the small J value (7.2 Hz, 8.4 Hz) between H-3 and H-4 indi­
cated the assignment of syn- configuration between C-3 and C-4. The NOE correlations 
of H-7/H-9, H-8/H-1′’, and the large J value (10.2 Hz) between H-7 and H-8 indicated 
the assignment of anti- configuration between C-7, C-8 and C-9. The NOE correlations 
of H-3/H-10 suggested they had the same orientation. Furthermore, the 1H and 13C 
chemical shifts of the aglycon of 1 − 3 were similar to those of antimycin A2a (Inai 
et  al. 2011) and DA-4 (Yan et  al. 2013), indicating C3, C4, C7, C8, C9 in 1 − 3 share 
the same absolute configurations, which were determined as 3S, 4 R, 7 R, 8 R and 9S, 
respectively. To determine the configurations of the glucose moiety, we performed 
acid hydrolysis and derivatization of the sugar moiety. LC-MS analysis revealed the 
presence of a D-glucose unit in compounds 1 − 3 that compared with standard sugar 
(Figure S27). The coupling constant of the anomeric proton H-1′′′ (δH 4.32, J = 7.6 Hz) 
indicated that the glycosyl linkage in 1 − 3 have a β-configuration. Therefore, com­
pounds 1 − 3 were identified as antimycin glucosides named glucosylantimycin A, B 
and C, respectively. As far as we know, they are the first reported glucosylated anti­
mycin derivatives. These results suggested that BmmGT1 was able to recognise anti­
mycins with variable side chains at C-7, transferring glucosyl group onto 8-OH.
Furthermore, we confirmed the catalytic activity of BmmGT1 towards antimycins 
in vitro. BmmGT1 was overexpressed and purified following the previously described 
procedures (Qin et  al. 2014) (Figure S28). The reactions were set up using UDP-D-
glucose as the sugar donor, and antimycin DA-4 or A2a as the sugar acceptor. As 
shown in Figure S29, BmmGT1 could attach a glucosyl group onto the 8-OH of DA-4 
to yield compound 1, in consistence with the in vivo data (Figure S1A). However, 
antimycin A2a could not serve as a substrate, suggesting that BmmGT1 specifically 
catalysed glucosylation at 8-OH but not 2′-OH of antimycins.
We then measured the water solubilities of DA-4 and the antimycin glucosides 1–3 
by minimum shake-flask solubility method (Veseli et al. 2019) (Figure S30). As expected, 
the water solubility of compounds 1 (345 μg/mL), 2 (738 μg/mL) and 3 (254 μg/mL) 
were increased by ~8-, 24- and 20-fold in comparison to that of DA-4 (31 μg/mL),

respectively. We then detected their cytotoxicities towards human cervix epidermoid 
carcinoma (HeLa) and human hepatic carcinoma (HepG2) cell lines. Unfortunately, 
none of them displayed activities (CC50 > 100 μM) (Table S4). These results supported 
that the acyl group at C-8 is of vital importance to the cytotoxicities of antimycins, 
which is consistent with that previously reported (Yan et  al. 2013). Nevertheless, our 
results demonstrated that glucosylation could greatly improve the water solubilities 
of antimycins. Glycosylation at alternative position (2′-OH) may generate new antimycin 
glycosides with improved bioactivities and properties. Enzymatic engineering of 
BmmGT1 to change its regio-selectivity and finding new GTs with desired catalytic 
activities are needed to fulfil this purpose.
3.  Experimental
3.1.  Strains, plasmids, and chemicals
The bacterial strains and plasmids utilised in this study are enumerated in Table S1, 
primers are listed in Table S3. S. albus ZH66 was isolated from deepsea sediment 
collected at a depth of 3536 m of the South China Sea (120° 0.250’E; 20° 22.971’N 
(Zhang et  al. 2015). Escherichia coli strains were cultivated at 37 °C in lysogeny broth 
(LB) liquid medium or on LB agar. S. albus ZH66 was cultured at 30 °C on MS agar 
for genetic manipulation. When necessary, the medium was supplemented with 100 μg 
mL−1 of hygromycin, 20 μg mL−1 of nalidixic acid for Streptomyces, and 100 μg mL−1 
of hygromycin, 100 μg mL−1 of kanamycin for E. coli. DNA isolation was carried out 
according to established protocols (Sambrook et al. 1989). Gel extraction and plasmid 
extraction were performed using commercial kits (OMEGA). Antimycin A2a, DA-4 and 
UDP-D-glucose were purchased from Sigma, BOC Sciences and Cayman, respectively.
3.2.  General experimental procedures
The HPLC analysis was carried out using an Agilent 1260 infinity system equipped 
with a diode array detector. HRESIMS data were obtained on a Q-TOF Ultima Global 
GAA076 LC-MS spectrometer. The 1D and 2D NMR spectra were recorded on either 
Bruker Avance III 600 or Agilent DD2-500 spectrometers. Chemical shifts were reported 
with reference to the residual solvent peaks (δH 2.50 and δC 39.5 ppm for DMSO-d6). 
The ECD spectra were recorded using a JASCO J-715 spectropolarimeter with MeOH 
as the solvent.
3.3.  Construction of the sbmGT1 heterologous expression strain
To construct the heterologous expression plasmid, the codon-optimized sbmGT1 and 
the constitutive promoter PgapDH were respectively amplified using the primer pairs 
of sbmGT1-F/R and promoter-F/R (Table S3), and then cloned into the KpnI and HindIII 
digested pIJ10500 using the Gibson method to give pWLI2001. The plasmid was 
passed through E. coli ET12567/pUZ8002 and then introduced into S. albus ZH66 by 
conjugation, resulting in the heterologous expression strain ZH66/pWLI2001, in which 
sbmGT1 was expressed under the control of the constitutive promoter PgapDH.

3.4.  Production, analyses, and purification of glucosylantimycins
To detect the fermentation products of ZH66/pIJ10500 and ZH66/pWLI2001, a 
small-scale (50 mL) fermentation was performed. The fermentation medium was pre­
pared as follows: 1% soluble starch, 2% glucose, 4% corn syrup, 1% yeast extract, 
0.3% beef extract, 0.05% MgSO4·7H2O, 0.05% KH2PO4 and 0.2% CaCO3, pH 7.0. The 
strain was inoculated into the fermentation medium and incubated at 30 °C on a 
rotary shaker at 220 rpm for 7 days. The fermentation cultures were harvested by 
centrifugation, and the supernatant was extracted twice with an equal volume of 
ethyl acetate. The pelleted mycelia were conducted with acetone, followed by 
extraction of ethyl acetate. The combined residues were dissolved in methanol and 
subjected to HPLC analysis. HPLC was performed on a C18 YMC-Pack ODS-A column 
(5 μm, 150 mm by 4.6 mm) developed with a linear gradient from 10% to 100% ace­
tonitrile (ACN)/H2O in 40 min at flow rate of 1 mL·min−1 and UV detection at 240 nm.
For the isolation of compounds 1–3, 20 L fermentation broths were harvested and 
extracted as above. The resulting crude extracts were subjected to a partitioning 
process using a mixture of 90% methanol and n-hexane. Subsequently, the methanol 
layer was purified using a stepped-gradient open column (ODS-A, 120 Å, S-30/50 
mesh), with a mobile phase ranging from 20% to 70% MeOH, giving five fractions 
(Fr.). Compounds 1 (2 mg) and 3 (2.5 mg) were obtained through further purification 
of Fr.4 using reversed-phase HPLC (YMC-Pack ODS-AA C18 column, 120 Å, 250 by 
10 mm, 5 μm) with an isocratic elution consisting of 50% ACN plus 0.1% HCOOH (vol/
vol) (2 mL min−1). Compound 2 (2 mg) was obtained from Fr.3 eluting with 40% ACN 
plus 0.1% HCOOH (vol/vol) (2 mL min−1).

3.5.  Acid hydrolysis and UPLC-MS analysis
Compounds 1–3 were heated at 80 °C with 1 N HCl for 3 h in a metal bath. The 
hydrolysed sugar was dissolved in 0.5 mL pyridine, followed by the addition of 3 mg 
L-cysteine methyl ester hydrochloride. The solution was then heated at 60 °C for 1 h. 
Subsequently, 3 mL of isothiocyanate was added to the mixture and heated at 60 °C

for an additional 1 h. Reference compounds D-glucose and L-glucose were derivatized 
using the same protocol. The derivatized products were analysed directly by UPLC-MS. 
Analytical UPLC was conducted on a Luna 5 μm C18 column (4.6 × 250 mm) at 25 °C 
using a gradient system (10–90% of B, A: 0.1% TFA in H2O, B: CH3CN). The extracted 
ion chromatograms (EIC) of D/L-glucose derivatives ([M + H]+ at m/z = 433.1098) were 
shown in Figure S27, and D-glucose was identified from each sample by comparing 
their retention times with those of authentic D-glucose and L-glucose.
3.6.  In vitro assays
The recombinant BmmGT1 was purified from Escherichia coli BL21/pWLI206 as previ­
ously report (Qin et al. 2014). The in vitro reaction mixtures (30 μL) consisted of 1 mM 
sugar acceptors (either antimycin A2a or DA-4), 2 μM BmmGT1, 2 mM sugar acceptors 
(UDP-D-glucose) and 10 mM MgCl2 in a 50 mM Tris-HCl buffer at pH 8.0. The mixtures 
were incubated at 30 °C for 2 h and then quenched by adding 30 μL of ACN. Denatured 
proteins were removed by centrifugation at 12,000 g for 20 min. The assays were 
monitored by HPLC analysis, using a C18 YMC-Pack ODS-A column (5 mm, 150 mm 
by 4.6 mm) with UV detection at 240 nm under the following program: solvent system 
(phase A, 0.1% formic acid in H2O; phase B, 0.1% formic acid in ACN); 10% B (0 to 
5 min), 10% to 100% B (5 to 40 min), 100% B (40 to 50 min), 100% to 10% (50 to 
51 min) 10% B (51 to 56 min) at a flow rate of 1 mL min−1.
3.7.  Water solubility of antimycin and its glucosides
We used the shake-flask solubility method (Veseli et  al. 2019) to measure the water 
solubility of antimycins and their glucosides. In brief, DA-4 and compounds 1–3 was 
each slowly added to 100 μL of distilled water until the turbidity was visible. They 
were then treated with sonication for 30 min and left at room temperature for 3 h to 
achieve saturation. The insoluble substance was removed through centrifugation at 
12,000 g for 20 min. Subsequently, the supernatant was filtered using a 0.22 μm filter 
followed by HPLC analysis using a C18 YMC-Pack ODS-A column (5 μm, 150 mm by 
4.6 mm) with a linear gradient from 10% to 100% acetonitrile (ACN)/H2O in 40 min at 
flow rate of 1 mL·min−1 and UV detection at 240 nm. The corresponding standard 
concentrations and peak areas of DA-4, compounds 1-3 were subjected to linear 
regression analysis using Excel. The water solubilities of the compounds were deter­
mined by calculating the linear regression equations (Figure S30).
3.8.  Cytotoxicity assay
The cytotoxicities of compounds 1–3 towards HeLa and HepG2 cell lines were eval­
uated. The cells were seeded in 96-well culture plates and cultured at 37 °C for 24 h. 
Subsequently, each compound with different concentrations (0, 0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.5, 1, 
10, 30 and 100 μM) of 10 µL per well was used to treat cells for 48 h. And then 10 μL 
water-soluble tetrazolium (WST) reagent was added to each well, incubating at 37 °C 
for another 2 h. Finally, the absorbance of each well was measured at 450 nm using 
a microplate reader (SpectraMax I3; Molecular Devices, USA).

4.  Conclusions
In this study, three new antimycin glycosides (1–3) were effectively generated by 
heterologous expression of sbmGT1 gene in a deepsea-derived Streptomyces strain. 
Glycosylation remarkably improved the water solubilities of antimycins while simul­
taneously reduced their cytotoxicities. This strategy is effective for expanding the 
chemical space of the compounds, which may contribute to the improvement of the 
pharmaceutical properties of therapeutically important molecules.
