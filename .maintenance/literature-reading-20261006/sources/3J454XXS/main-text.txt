ABSTRACT: Polyketides are a diverse class of natural products with
a wide range of therapeutic properties. They have been widely used in
clinical settings as antibiotics, antifungals, anticancer agents, and
immunosuppressants. A major subset of these molecules is produced
by type I assembly line polyketide synthases (T1ALPKSs), large
enzyme complexes that catalyze their stepwise construction. Under-
standing the selectivity and scope of T1ALPKS domains could greatly
expand the diversity of products generated by these synthases and
their components. This study explores the reactivity and selectivity of
the terminal domains of T1ALPKSs, known as thioesterases (TEs). A
panel of 25 wild-type and 10 mutant (Ser→Cys) thioesterases was
reacted with semisynthetic substrates. The selectivity with which these
TEs form 12- and/or 14-membered macrolactones and one or both of
a pair of diastereomeric 14-membered macrolactones was quantified. The results indicate that the selectivity with which wild-type
TEs react with unnatural substrates correlates with their parent T1ALPKSs, enabling the assignment of TEs into groups.
Additionally, our experiments reveal that mutant (Ser→Cys) TEs often have expanded scopes compared to their wild-type
counterparts and that some wild-type TEs are capable of hydrolyzing macrolactones. We demonstrated the synthetic applications of
TEs with kinetic resolutions and preparative-scale reactions. This study provides a framework for organizing TEs and predicting their
reactivity and selectivity, facilitating future research on structure−activity relationships and supporting directed evolution studies.
■INTRODUCTION
Polyketides are a class of natural products that have been
extensively employed as therapeutics and drug precursors
because of their antibiotic,1,2 antifungal,3 antiparasitic,4
immunomodulatory,5,6 insecticidal,7 and antimitotic8,9 proper-
ties. A subset of polyketides is synthesized by large enzyme
complexes known as type I assembly line polyketide synthases
(T1ALPKSs) via sequential decarboxylative Claisen condensa-
tion reactions.10−13 These megasynthases consist of repeating
components called modules, each of which is composed of
multiple enzyme domains. Acyltransferase (AT), acyl carrier
protein (ACP), and ketosynthase (KS) domains sequentially
load, receive/tether, and condense acyl units, resulting in
polyketide chain elongation. Ketoreductase (KR), enoylreduc-
tase (ER), and dehydratase (DH) domains modify polyketide
chains as they translocate across a T1ALPKS.10,11 Terminal
thioesterase (TE) domains release the final polyketide
intermediates via macrocyclization or hydrolysis reactions.14
The discovery of the genes for 6-deoxyerythronolide B
(DEBS) T1ALPKS in the erythromycin producer Saccharopo-
lyspora erythraea15−18 inspired numerous attempts to re-
engineer T1ALPKSs to create artificial polyketides.10,19,20
This process, known as “combinatorial biosynthesis,” has the
potential to revolutionize organic synthesis by drastically
reducing the labor, cost, and waste required to produce
polyketide natural products. However, in practice, the
applications of combinatorial biosynthesis have been limited
because T1ALPKSs generated by this approach are often
inactive or produce trace quantities of their target mole-
cules.10,21 The complexity of engineered T1ALPKSs compli-
cates efforts to identify which components, interactions, or
other parameters are responsible for low yields or low titers.
To determine the factors responsible for these outcomes, the
selectivity and substrate scope of individual components within
the T1ALPKSs must be established. In vitro studies address
this challenge by providing direct information about the
selectivity with which individual T1ALPKS components react
with natural and unnatural substrates. Thioesterase (TE)
domains are excellent candidates for in vitro studies because
they have practical standalone synthetic applications and are
underexplored. These cyclases selectively catalyze the for-
mation of complex macrolactones, which are the core

A pioneering study published in 2003 demonstrated that the
excised epothilone T1ALPKS TE domain from a mixed
nonribosomal peptide synthetase (NRPS)/T1ALPKS system
catalyzes the macrocyclization of its native polyketide chain
elongation intermediate in vitro.24 With a few notable

reactions of semisynthetic substrates with PikTE (PikTE-
Sv15439) and DebsTE (DebsTE-Se11635), domains derived
from the 10-deoxymethynolide/narbonolide synthase of
Streptomyces venezuelae31 and the 6-deoxyerythronolide B
synthase (DEBS) of Saccharopolyspora erythraea,32−34 respec-
tively.31,33,35−50
A key study in 2017 reported the ability of PikAIII-TE to
elongate and, in some cases, cyclize unnatural pentaketides
with functional groups and stereochemical configurations that
differed from those in the “native” pentaketide.43 Reactions of
PikAIII-TE with synthetic pentaketide analogues bearing a
distal C-11 (S)-configured nucleophilic hydroxyl group
(inverted relative to the native C-11 (R) configuration)
resulted in no observed macrolactones and low levels of
degradation products derived from the hydrolyzed (seco-acid)
hexaketide. Reactions of an analogous N-acetylcysteamine
(SNAC)-activated hexaketide bearing the corresponding distal
(S)-configured nucleophilic hydroxyl group with the excised
PikTE domain also failed to generate a macrolactone product.
This result suggested that the TE domain in the PikAIII-TE
fusion protein prevents the offloading of cyclized products in
the reactions of the pentaketides containing C-11 (S)-
configured hydroxyl groups (Figure S1). A companion study
demonstrated that a PikTE Ser→Cys active site mutant
catalyzed the formation of a new macrolactone from the (S)-
hydroxy hexaketide, but the generality of this gain-of-function
mutation was not investigated.44 In vitro studies of TEs that
employ near-native substrate analogues are scarce due to the
challenges associated with synthesizing complex, and often
unstable,40,45 polyketide chain elongation intermediates.
Studies that bypass this issue by employing truncated substrate
mimics31,33,46−50 or simplified full-length substrates41,42 have
provided important information about substrate scope and
selectivity of TEs, but many key questions remain.

their synthetic applications, we reacted two full-length linear
heptaketide substrates and one hexaketide substrate with an
enzyme panel consisting of 25 phylogenetically related TEs
and 10 mutant TEs. This experimental approach enabled us to
establish whether a given TE could catalyze the formation of

■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Synthesis of Substrates. To accomplish the objectives of
our investigation, we attempted to identify substrates that
would allow us to address the following two questions: (1)
Can a given TE form macrolactones of different sizes? (2) Can
a given TE form macrolactones containing C-3 hydroxyl
groups with different stereochemical configurations (Figure 1)?
We required that our substrates meet the following criteria: (1)
Substrates must be full-length and capable of forming a 12- or
14-membered macrolactone, (2) substrates must possess
structural and stereochemical features similar to those of the
polyketide intermediates cyclized (or predicted to be cyclized)
by wild-type TEs, (3) substrates must be accessible by
semisynthetic routes that are concise and scalable, and (4)
substrates (and their precursors) must be stable and not
undergo rapid hemiketalization.35,36,40,45
Three substrates that adhere to the above guidelines were
selected: hexaketide (10a), which can be converted into a 12-
membered macrolactone, and diastereomeric heptaketides (C-
3-(R)−OH, 11a) and (C-3-(S)−OH, 12a), which possess
opposite stereochemical configurations at their C-3 positions
and can form 14-membered macrolactones (Figure 1). We
synthesized these substrates by isolating either 10-deoxyme-
thynolide (10-DML) (1) or narbonolide (2) in gram-scale
quantities from multiliter fermentations and converting them
into the corresponding linear N-acetylcysteamine (SNAC)
acylthioesters via subsequent synthetic transformations.51 A
previously developed synthetic route was employed to convert
the 12-membered macrolactone 10-deoxymethynolide (1) to
SNAC thioester hexaketide (10a) in four steps (Figure S4).40
We developed two new synthetic routes to convert
narbonolide (2) into heptaketide substrates (11a) and (12a)
(Figures S5 and S6). These routes employed diastereoselective
reductions of the narbonolide C-3 ketone, which afforded
products containing the corresponding (R) or (S) secondary
alcohols. The C-5-OH group was methylated to prevent
hemiketalization and/or dehydration35,36,40,45 and preclude
formation of a δ-lactone.37,52 Although a thioester substrate
bearing a C-3-OH group could in principle cyclize to form a β-
lactone, thioesterases rarely catalyze such reactions.53,54
Consequently, we chose not to employ C-3-OH methylation
(protection) in our substrate design strategy. We also
generated authentic seco-acids (10b), (11b), and (12b)
(Figures S4−S6), which are formed in enzymatic reactions
by hydrolysis of the corresponding SNAC thioester substrates
(10a), (11a), and (12a). Authentic standards for the 12- and
14-membered macrolactone products (10c), (11c), and (12c)
(Figures S4−S7) were synthesized for degradation studies and
calibration purposes. With our substrates in hand, we set out to
construct a library of TEs that could convert our linear
hexaketide and heptaketides into macrolactones and resolve
the questions driving our study.
Assembling a Library of TEs. The criteria for selecting
and organizing the TEs for our library were inspired by the
structures of the macrolactones that they generate (or are
predicted to generate) in their native systems. Specifically, we
chose TEs for our library based on three main criteria: (1)
Structural similarity to substrates: TEs associated with
macrolactones whose structural features resemble those of
our substrates were prioritized to improve the likelihood of
successful cyclization. (2) Macrolactone ring size: we selected
TEs predicted to generate 12-, 14-, and 16-membered
macrolactones. This criterion was inspired by PikTE’s ability
to offload both 12- and 14-membered macrolactones from its
native T1ALPKS. We hypothesized that T1ALPKSs, through
evolutionary processes such as module duplication or deletion,
may have shifted toward producing rings of different sizes, but
their TEs may have retained the ability to form macrolactones
resembling their ancestral products. By including TEs
predicted to form macrolactones of varying sizes, we sought
to test whether TEs could cyclize rings larger or smaller than
those produced by their modern pathways. (3) Structural
features at the C-3 position: to investigate the impact of
stereochemistry and functional groups on TE-catalyzed
macrolactonization, we included TEs in our library that are
linked to macrolactones featuring either a ketone at the C-3
position, as seen in narbonolide, or a C-3-(S)-hydroxyl group,
as found in 6-deoxyerythronolide B. We hypothesized that TEs
from pathways producing these macrolactones may have
originated from ancestral T1ALPKSs that generated a
macrolactone with a C-3-(S)−OH group, a C-3-(R)−OH
group, or a C-3-ketone. We believed that including TEs
associated with macrolactones featuring different C-3 groups
would enable us to establish general trends linking TE
selectivity to compounds produced by their T1ALPKSs. We
also included TE mutants in our library because a previous
study demonstrated that a single Ser→Cys mutation in PikTE
enables the resulting enzyme to cyclize a hexaketide containing
a C-11-OH group with non-native stereochemistry.44 We
hypothesized that mutant Ser→Cys TEs might be able to
cyclize a wider range of substrates than their native
counterparts, generating macrolactones of different sizes or
with alternate stereochemical configurations. After we
established our selection criteria, we systematically assembled
our TE library.
Our library was constructed in four stages. In the first stage,
well-studied T1ALPKSs that produce macrolactones that
adhere to the criteria described above were identified,51,55−61
their terminal domains were designated as reference TEs, and a
naming convention for these TEs that incorporated informa-
tion about their pathways and bacterial strains of origin was
created (see Section S3). Our reference TEs include PikTE-
Sv15439 (from a Group 1,2 10-DML/narbonolide synthase),
OleTE-Sl11891 (from a Group 3 8,8a-deoxyoleandolide
synthase), DebsTE-Se11635 (from a Group 4 6-deoxyery-
thronolide B synthase), LkmTE-Sr7434 (from a Group 5 8,15-
dideoxylankanolide synthase), NidTE-Sc2821 (from a Group 6
platenolide I synthase), JuvTE-Mc21561 (from a Group 7
tylactone synthase), ChmTE-Sb2737 (from a Group 8
chalcolactone synthase), and MycTE-Mg11725 (from a
Group 9 protomycinolide IV synthase). In the second stage
of our library assembly, we identified protein homologues of

Analysis of Reactions Catalyzed by Wild-Type TEs.
Substrates (10a), (11a), and (12a) were reacted with the 25
wild-type TEs in our library using standard reaction conditions
with 4-nitrobenzamide as an internal standard (Figure 3). After
each reaction, the quantities of macrolactone, linear seco-acid,
and remaining starting material were measured. The seco-acid
(10b, 11b, 12b) and macrolactone (10c, 11c, 12c) products
were also reacted with individual TEs (see Section S9) to
determine if they would undergo degradation or hydrolysis,
respectively. These experiments allowed us to determine if
seco-acid products in reactions of (10a), (11a), and (12a)
were derived from the hydrolysis of an acyl-TE intermediate or
from the hydrolysis of a macrolactone product (Figure S3).
TEs associated with the same group of T1ALPKSs generally
reacted with (10a), (11a), and (12a) to generate products in
similar yields and with similar selectivities. All TEs associated

(10a) and (12a) to produce the corresponding macrolactones
in similar yields. Remarkably, despite PikHTE-Nc44551
sharing only 58% amino acid sequence identity with PikTE-
Sv15439, both enzymes reacted with (10a), (11a), and (12a)
to generate macrolactone/seco-acid products in nearly the
same ratios. NbmTE-Sn19790 and PikTE-Sv15068 are derived
from Streptomyces species that generate different relative

The ability of TEs associated with 10-DML (1)/narbonolide
(2) synthases to generate semisynthetic macrolactones of
different sizes is likely related to their native function: cyclizing
both hexa- and heptaketides to form 12- and 14-membered
macrolactones. Despite the fact that these TEs have evolved to
generate (2), which bears a ketone at the C-3 position, they
also catalyze the cyclization of the diastereomeric heptaketides
(11a) and (12a), which contain (R)- and (S)-configured
secondary alcohols, respectively, at their C-3 positions. In
contrast, this relaxed selectivity was not observed for TEs
derived from T1ALPKSs (Groups 3, 4, and 5) that evolved to
generate 14-membered macrolactones with (S)-configured C-3
secondary alcohols. The TEs associated with 10-DML (1)/
narbonolide (2) synthases also catalyzed the hydrolysis of
macrolactones (10c) and (11c) to generate the corresponding
seco-acids (10b, 11b) in trace quantities (Section S9). These
observations suggest that macrolactones (10c) and (11c) can
reenter the substrate channels of these TEs,70 where they are
transacylated to re-form Ser-acyl ester complexes (Figure S3).
These complexes then undergo hydrolysis to form (10b) and
(11b).

or macrolactone (12c). Among TEs from Groups 6−8, the
highest selectivity for the production of macrolactone (12c)
was achieved in the reaction of ChmTE-Sb2737 (Group 8)
with a thioester (12a). TylTE-Sf19609 (Group 7), in contrast,
reacted with thioester (12a) to generate a moderate quantity
of seco-acid (12b) (66%) and a small amount (8%) of
macrolactone (12c). The platenolide I (6) and tylactone (7)
synthases each generate a compound that contains a C-3
secondary alcohol with the same spatial configuration as that in
(12c). The presence of this stereochemical feature in the
native substrates of Groups 6 and 7 may explain why thioester
(12a) underwent higher conversion than (11a) in the presence
of the corresponding TEs. The TEs associated with T1ALPKSs
that produce 16-membered rings did not catalyze the
hydrolysis of the three macrolactones (10c, 11c, 12c) to an
appreciable (>0.5%) extent (Section S9).
Analysis of Reactions Catalyzed by Serine-to-Cys-
teine Mutant TEs. A previous study indicated that
incorporating a Ser→Cys mutation in the active site of
PikTE-Sv15439 can broaden its substrate scope.44 To explore
the generality of this observation, we expressed a series of
Ser→Cys mutant TEs and reacted them with (10a), (11a),
and (12a) (Figure 4). Reactions of Ole*TE-Sl11891 and
Debs*TE-Se11635 with thioester (10a) produced high yields
of macrolactone (10c), whereas analogous reactions of (10a)
with OleTE-Sl11891 and DebsTE-Se11635 produced seco-
acid (10b) as the major product and small quantities of
macrolactone (10c). The ratios of products obtained from
these reactions demonstrate that the Ser→Cys active site
mutation alters the selectivity of the catalytic offloading step
(Figure S3).
The Ser→Cys active site mutant versions of TEs from
Groups 6-8 (16-membered macrolactones) reacted with
thioester (10a) to generate low to moderate quantities of the
12-membered macrolactone (10c). By contrast, reactions of
their wild-type counterparts with (10a) resulted in trace yields
of (10c). The improvement in both conversion and yield
observed in reactions of (10a) with the mutant TEs
demonstrates that the Ser→Cys active site mutation increases
the rate at which substrates are loaded and/or reduces the
extent to which TEs are deactivated.
Reactions of the Ser→Cys mutant TEs with thioester (11a)
generated products in yields and selectivities that differed from
those obtained in analogous reactions with their wild-type
counterparts. For example, reactions of Ole*TE-Sl11891,
Debs*TE-Se11635, Lkm*TE-Sr7434, and Chm*TE-Sb2737
with thioester (11a) produced moderate to high yields of
macrolactone (11c), whereas reactions of (11a) with the wild-
type TEs generated negligible or low yields of (11c).
Surprisingly, the reaction of thioester (11a) with Ole*TE-
Sl11891 generated the highest overall yield of macrolactone
(11c).
Reactions of the Ser→Cys mutant TEs with thioester (12a)
typically generated macrolactone (12c) in yields equal to or
lower than those obtained from analogous reactions with their
wild-type counterparts. For example, reactions of Pik*TE-
Sv15439, Debs*TE-Se11635, Juv*TE-Mc21561, and
Chm*TE-Sb2737 with thioester (12a) generated lower
quantities of macrolactone (12c) compared to analogous
reactions with their wild-type counterparts. Reactions of
Srm*TE-Sa23877 and Nid*TE-Sc2821 with thioester (12a)
produced lower quantities of seco-acid (12b) compared to
reactions with their wild-type counterparts.
With some exceptions, we found that the Ser→Cys mutant
TEs reacted with (10a) and (11a) to generate products in
higher yields, conversions, and selectivities compared with
their wild-type counterparts. We hypothesize that the
introduction of a cysteine residue in the active site of a TE
may affect the ability of the resulting enzyme to load substrates
and/or the selectivity of acyl-intermediate release (Figure S3).
Additionally, significant quantities of seco-acid products were
not observed when Ser→Cys mutants were reacted with
macrolactones (10c), (11c), and (12c) (Section S9). This
observation could be exploited in future crystallographic
studies. Enzyme−product complexes may be challenging to
form with thioesterases (TEs) that can hydrolyze their
macrolactone products, such as LkmTE-Sr7434, which hydro-
lyzes product (12c). One potential solution to this problem is
to use a Ser→Cys mutant incapable of hydrolyzing the
macrolactone product as a substitute for the wild-type enzyme.
This approach could facilitate the formation of stable enzyme−
product complexes, thereby overcoming this limitation.

accomplish using nonenzymatic catalysts. Previous studies have
employed high catalyst loadings (typically 1%) of TEs to
convert substrates to the desired products. We found that, for
some reactions, high loadings of TEs were not required to
achieve excellent selectivities and conversions. OleTE-Sl11891

which may serve as late-stage intermediates for semisynthetic

resolution experiment to selectively convert macrolactone
(12c) to seco-acid (12b), leaving the majority of (11c)

employed as diastereoselective hydrolases for synthetic
reactions. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first
hydrolytic kinetic resolution ever accomplished with a TE from
a T1ALPKS.
To demonstrate the synthetic utility of our enzymatic
transformations, we performed a series of preparative-scale
reactions to determine the isolated product yields (Figure 6).
We conducted macrolactone-forming reactions with substrates
(10a), (11a), and (12a), and we conducted a hydrolytic
reaction with macrolactone (12c) as the substrate. High
isolated yields were obtained (73−96%) from all reactions.
The products were characterized by NMR, and the resulting
spectra were compared with those of our authentic standards
(Section S7). Our results demonstrate that T1ALPKS TEs
catalyze selective transformations on scales suitable for
synthetic chemistry applications.
■CONCLUSIONS
In the first part of this study, 25 wild-type and 10 mutant
T1ALPKS TEs were successfully expressed as monodomain
proteins, and their abilities to cyclize three semisynthetic
substrates were quantified. We evaluated (1) if our TEs could
cyclize hexaketide and heptaketide substrates to form 12- and/
or 14-membered macrolactones, respectively, and (2) if our
TEs could form one or both of a pair of diastereomeric 14-
membered macrolactones from our heptaketide substrates.
The results of these experiments suggest that the selectivity
with which TEs react with semisynthetic substrates is
correlated with the macrolactones they produce in their native
systems and that Ser→Cys mutant TEs may both load and
offload substrates with different selectivities than their wild-
type counterparts. Degradation studies indicated that certain
TEs have the ability to hydrolyze macrolactones. In the second
part of this study, we applied the information gathered in the
first part to expand and showcase the synthetic utility of
thioesterases. We developed ring-forming and ring-opening
kinetic resolutions with different TEs, demonstrated that
T1ALPKS TEs can convert thioesters with high TTNs, and
confirmed our analytical yields with prep-scale reactions. These
enzymes provide new solutions to problems that would be
difficult to solve using traditional synthetic methods.74,75
Prior to this work, trends in the reactivity and selectivity of
TEs from many well-known systems were not established. The
selectivity of individual TEs could not be ascertained or
compared because the seco-acid byproducts resulting from the
hydrolysis of full-length substrates had not been quantified
rigorously.76 Additionally, general principles for organizing TE
libraries and predicting TE reactivity and selectivity were
lacking. Finally, the ability of TEs to hydrolyze macrolactones
was understudied, and synthetic applications had not been
envisioned or investigated. This work has addressed all of these
challenges and lays the groundwork for the widespread
application of TEs in both traditional organic synthesis and
combinatorial biosynthesis. The phylogenetic relationships we
have uncovered among TEs, which were correlated with
T1ALPKSs and their macrolactone products, may provide
deeper insights into T1ALPKS evolution and offer a means of
identifying orphan pathways. Future studies are needed to
understand the relationships between structural features of TE
domains and the reactions they catalyze.77,78 We have initiated
structural, computational, and mutagenic studies to uncover
these relationships and expect this work will expand the
synthetic applications of this valuable class of enzymes.
