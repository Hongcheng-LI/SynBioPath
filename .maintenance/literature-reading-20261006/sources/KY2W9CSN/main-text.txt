ABSTRACT: Polyketides are a diverse class of compounds with significant interest
in the pharmaceutical industry. They are synthesized by polyketide synthases (PKSs),
which consist of large multienzyme systems. Post-translational modification of acyl-
carrier protein (ACP) domains is crucial for PKS and fatty acid synthase function and
involves phosphopantetheinyl transferases (PPTases) that attach a 4′-phosphopante-
theine (Ppant) moiety from coenzyme A (CoA) to a conserved serine residue on the
ACP domain. This modification enables communication between the ACP and other
enzymatic domains within the same PKS. In Mycobacterium tuberculosis, PptT is
essential for bacillus replication and survival during infection. This study focuses on
unraveling the activation mechanism of an ACP domain by PptT. It combines
biochemical and structural analyses with advanced molecular dynamics simulations to
elucidate the precise mechanism of CoA’s Ppant arm transfer onto the ACP domain.
The findings from this research contribute to a deeper understanding of polyketide
biosynthesis with major implications for anti-TB drug discovery efforts.
KEYWORDS: PPTase, PptT, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Ppant transfer, X-ray crystallography, molecular dynamics, DFT, QM/MM
■INTRODUCTION
Polyketides represent a chemically and functionally diverse
class of compounds that are of great interest in the
pharmaceutical industry.1 Typically, aromatic polyketides are
biosynthesized by type II polyketide synthases (PKSs) in an
iterative process that involves a small set of discrete proteins.
In contrast, the biosynthesis of reduced polyketides, such as
the lipids present in the cell envelope of mycobacteria, involves
protein domains housed within gigantic multienzyme systems
termed type I PKSs. Essential for PKS activity are the
ketosynthase (KS), acyl transferase (AT), and acyl carrier
protein (ACP) domains. AT, often referred to as the
“gatekeeper”, catalyzes the transfer of an elongation unit
(from malonyl-CoA, (2S)-methylmalonyl-CoA, or carboxyl-
acyl-CoA) to the ACP domain, while KS catalyzes the
formation of C−C bonds between the acyl-ACP and an
elongation unit, resulting in the production of an ACP-bound
β-keto ester.
Post-translational modification of ACP domains is a
universal process indispensable to the proper functioning of
PKS molecular factories. This modification is catalyzed by
phosphopantetheinyl transferases (PPTases), which utilize
coenzyme A (CoA) and divalent cations as cofactors (Figure
1A). This step implies the covalent linkage of a 4′-
phosphopantetheine (Ppant) moiety from CoA to a conserved
serine residue of the ACP domain.2 Anchoring the starting unit
at the SH extremity of the Ppant arm acts as a signal for the
“shuttle” ACP domain to communicate with the other
enzymatic domains, facilitating the proper reactions. In
Mycobacterium tuberculosis, PptT is essential for the replication
and survival of the bacillus during both acute and chronic
phases of infection in mice.3 Consequently, PptT is the focus
of intensive anti-TB drug discovery programs between
academia and the pharmaceutical industry.4,5 In this study,
we set out to unravel the intricate steps involved in the
activation mechanism of an ACP domain by PptT. Our
approach involves a combination of biochemical and structural
analyses as well as cutting-edge density functional theory
(DFT)-cluster and quantum mechanics/molecular mechanics
(QM/MM) molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to shed
light on the precise mechanism by which the Ppant arm of
CoA is transferred onto its cognate ACP domain.

whether both cofactors were essential for the enzyme’s
stability. Considering that Mn2+ greatly enhances the activity
of Sfp-type PPTases7 and possibly their stability, we
implemented a two-step purification procedure in which the
immobilized metal affinity chromatography (IMAC) step was
performed with 1 mM MnCl2, while the size exclusion
chromatography (SEC) step was carried out in the absence
of the metal ion. In this scenario, both the extraction and
purification buffers lacked CoA. Although the SEC profile and
SDS-PAGE confirmed the enzyme’s monodispersity and high
purity (Figure S1), differential scanning fluorimetry (DSF)
experiments revealed relatively low melting temperature (Tm)
values of approximately 30 °C (Figure 1B), indicating the low
stability of PptT under these conditions. In contrast, when
MnCl2 was consistently present throughout the purification
process, the Tm values could be increased by approximately 15
°C (Figure 1B).
Furthermore, we examined the effects of Ca2+ and Mg2+ on
the stability of PptT as these ions have been reported to
facilitate the transfer reaction with homologues of PptT: Sfp
from Bacillus subtilis7,8 and PptAb from Mycobacterium
abscessus.9 Additionally, we expanded the panel of divalent
ions to include Ni2+ and Co2+. Both ions are commonly used
during the IMAC step, and Co2+ has shown activity restoration
in previous studies.10 Importantly, these divalent ions were
found to be compatible with the PptT conditioning buffer. At a
final concentration of 1 mM, each cation affects the stability of
the enzyme. Among these cations, Ni2+ has the most favorable
impact, with Tm values above 55 °C, followed by Mn2+ and
Co2+, while Ca2+ and Mg2+ exhibit the least advantageous
effects, with Tm values as low as 35 °C (Figure 1B). When
divalent ions and CoA are combined, there is a significant
increase in the stability of PptT, leading to Tm values up to 10
°C higher compared to those using the divalent ion alone
(Figure 1B). In contrast, replacing CoA with the reaction
product 3′,5′-ADP has a limited effect on the stability of the
enzyme, and this effect is observed solely in the presence of Co
2+ and Ni 2+ ions.
We also confirmed the enzymatic activity of PptT in vitro by
using the ACP domain from the type I PKS PpsC of M.
tuberculosis. PpsC catalyzes crucial biosynthetic steps in the
production of lipid virulence factors, including phthiocerol
dimycocerosates and phenolic glycolipids.11 To facilitate this
analysis, we generated a library of fragments centered on the
PpsC ACP domain and selected well-expressed and soluble
constructs.12 The selected ACP fragment (2042−2188) was
successfully purified using a two-step purification strategy (see
Methods section). Furthermore, we demonstrated that PptT
could transfer the P-pant moiety from CoA onto the ACP in
the presence of either Mn2+ or Mg2+. This transfer was
visualized through gel electrophoresis on a 10% polyacrylamide
gel containing 2.5 M urea (Figure 1C).
Structural Changes upon PptT Activation. The detailed
structural dissection of the catalytic cycle of PptT begins with
its three-dimensional structure in the absence of CoA. While
previous structural studies have reported apo forms of Sfp-type
PPTases,13,14 purifying PptT in the absence of CoA posed a
significant challenge due to its beneficial impact on the stability
of the enzyme.6 From the DSF experiments, we identified
Mn2+ as a promising cation to successfully compensate for the
absence of CoA in vitro. Crystals could only be obtained under
acidic pH conditions, which can hinder cation binding due to
the protonation state of acidic residues involved in
coordination, as previously observed by us9 and others.15

While the apo-PptT structure confirmed the absence of Mn2+
at both sites, we identified one Mg2+ ion and its counter acetate
ion from the crystallization conditions exclusively at site I. The
coordination sphere is completed by the side chain of D114
and four water molecules (Figure 2A). Additional density in
the active site could be attributed to the vector region
containing the thrombin cleavage site (LVPR↓GF) and the
NdeI site coding for H-1 and M0 located between the N-
terminal 6His tag and the PptT sequence, with the histidine
main-chain and side-chain nitrogen atoms at hydrogen bond
distances to the oxygen atoms of E157 (Figure 2A).
The capture of a CoA molecule in the active site cavity of
PptT has a minimal impact on the positioning of structural
elements within the characteristic α/β fold, despite the large
number of polar and apolar interactions involving main-chain
and side-chain residues (Figure 2B,D). Most differences
concern the orientation of residue side chains that contribute
to the anchoring and stabilization of CoA in the cavity (Figure
2D). Notably, the side chain of R48 undergoes a ∼120°
rotation, bringing it in the vicinity of the 3′-phosphate of CoA
and forming salt bridges with the amino groups. Mutating this
arginine to alanine in PptT slightly decreases the affinity for
CoA, with limited effects on the activity.15 The absence of
divalent cations at both sites in the PptT-CoA structure caused
by the crystallization conditions at pH 4, as previously
observed in the X-ray structure of PptAb,9 disturbs the
positioning of side-chain residues involved in metal ion
coordination. This is the case for H93, a key player during
catalysis,15 where the imidazole ring of H93 undergoes a 125°
rotation, bringing its δ-nitrogen at a hydrogen bond distance to
the α-phosphate of CoA (Figure 2B). The observed conforma-
tional changes in R48, H93, and, to a lesser extent, R56,
between the apo and CoA-bound structures cannot be
attributed to the vector tag since they occur at a significant
distance from these residues. Interestingly, H-1 and M0 fill the
gap left by the absence of the Ppant extremity and the
adenosine group, respectively, while the acetate takes the place
of the α-phosphate to complete the coordination sphere of
Mg2+ (Figure 2D). On the other hand, minor conformational
changes were observed for residues bordering the Ppant
moiety and the tunnel entrance (Figure 2D).
Once the Ppant transfer onto an ACP domain is completed,
the reaction product 3′,5′-ADP is released, and the cycle
restarts. Good-quality crystals of PptT in complex with 3′,5′-
ADP were obtained at physiological pH, confirming the
presence of Mn2+ at both sites with octahedral geometry
(Figure 2C). The main local rearrangements observed in the
complex include the rotation of the side chain of E157 toward
site I and the repositioning of the imidazole ring of H93 to
favor direct interactions with the 3′-phosphate and the metal
ion, while all other side-chain positions remain unchanged
(Figure 2D).

MD Study of PptT-CoA/ACP and CoA Conformation.
Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were used to study the
conformational changes of CoA-bound PptT upon interactions
with the ACP domain and prepare the complex structures for
subsequent QM/MM calculations. To this end, we have
compared the systems in the absence and presence of ACP.
From the classical MD, both complex structures are very stable
along the trajectories, with RMSD fluctuations (calculated on
the backbone atoms) around 0.2 Å with and without ACP
(Figure S3).

remains stable (Figure 5A,B). However, the main difference is
observed around the pyrophosphate group. In the absence of
ACP, a significant fluctuation of the Oβ-Pβ-Oα-Pα dihedral
angle is observed, with values ranging from ±180 to −60°

QM Cluster and QM/MM Study of the Ppant Transfer
Reaction. Both DFT-cluster and QM/MM calculations were
used to elucidate the possible reaction pathways. From the
DFT-cluster approach, we optimized the geometry of the
reactant, intermediates, and transition states (TSs), facilitating
rapid screening of the reaction mechanism. Beginning with the
reactant (Figure S7A), we propose a reaction intermediate
where the oxygen of residue S2105 is bound to the β-
phosphate of CoA, while its proton is transferred to an oxygen
of residue E157. The reaction enthalpy was determined to be
10.5 kcal/mol, accompanied by an energy barrier of 40.1 kcal/
mol (Figure S8). Analysis of the intermediate structure (Figure
S7B) revealed the notable breakage of the phosphodiester
bond Pα-Oα-Pβ, suggesting a bimolecular reaction mecha-
nism. We then modeled the product in which the 3′,5′-ADP
moiety, dissociated in the intermediate, is protonated by an
activated metal-bound water molecule (Figure S7C). This
subsequent reaction, when compared to the intermediate, was
found to be exothermic with an energy barrier of 4.5 kcal/mol
and a reaction energy of 0.4 kcal/mol (Figure S8).
To gain a more comprehensive understanding of enzyme
reactions and consider the whole environment, we imple-
mented a QM/MM-steered MD (SMD) protocol in which the
QM region was expanded and treated at the semiempirical
PM6 level of theory.17 To accelerate the SMD simulations
without introducing bias due to large dispersion in the
nonequilibrium work distribution,18−20 we applied the
Jarzynski relationship21 to estimate the free energy (a detailed
discussion is presented in the following section). This enabled
us to thoroughly examine the various reaction pathways
implicated in the enzymatic reaction.
We first investigated the dual mechanism proposed by
Pedelacq and colleagues.9 In their model, the first step of the
reaction involves the deprotonation of the hydroxyl group of
the serine acceptor (S2105 in ACP), either by the conserved
metal-bound glutamate side chain (E157 in PptT) or
alternatively an activated metal-bound water molecule. This
step is followed by a nucleophilic attack on the β-phosphate of
CoA. Although both reaction models propose the formation of
an intermediate alkoxide group on S2105, our SMD
trajectories do not support this hypothesis. Indeed, a stable
alkoxide ion was not observed in our simulation (Figures S9
and S10). As a result, we propose a slightly different reaction
model, suggesting that proton transfer and nucleophilic attack
occur simultaneously in a concerted manner, as illustrated by
the model in steps 1a and 1b (Figure 6A), respectively. For
both reaction pathways, we observed the formation of stable
intermediates labeled IM1a and IM1b (Figure 6B). These
intermediates correspond to a PptT-CoA/ACP complex with a
Mg2+ distorted coordination sphere, as depicted in Figure 6A.
This observation, in contrast to the results obtained from the
quantum cluster model, suggests that the nucleophilic
substitution at the phosphorus could take place via a
unimolecular SN1 associative mechanism. Additionally, a
notable change was observed in the dihedral angle of S2105,
shifting from 90 to 0° along trajectories (Figure 6A).
Simultaneously, the Pα-Oα-Pβ angle flipped by approximately
180° from its initial position (Figure 6A, IM1a and IM1b).
This rotation is suggested to be the driving force behind the
reduction of the serine-pyrophosphate distance during
nucleophilic attack. Based on the calculated energy difference
of 8.4 kcal/mol between the TSs 1a and 1b from the two
proposed pathways, we conclude that step 1a, which is
associated with the formation of IM1a, is energetically more
favorable than step 1b, which is associated with IM1b (Figure
6B).

complex, leading to the formation of a second intermediate,
named IM2a. As for the quantum cluster model, we propose an

to highlight the chemical equivalence of products P1a and P1b
as they result from the dissociation of the 3′,5′-ADP moiety

discrepancy in energy can be attributed to the difference in the

■DISCUSSION
PPTases are receiving increased attention due to their essential
role in the biosynthesis of a broad range of natural lipids,
including lipopolysaccharides and membrane lipids, in all three
domains of life. In this study, we focused on PptT, a critical
enzyme for the growth of M. tuberculosis in vitro and during
infection in mice.3 PptT catalyzes the transfer of a 4′-
phosphopantetheinyl moiety of CoA to an ACP domain of
type-I PKSs and nonribosomal peptide synthases. Although
three-dimensional structures of Sfp-type PPTases, including
the mycobacterial PptT and PptAb enzymes (69% sequence
identity), have been described either alone or in complex with
an ACP domain,9,15,16,22 significant unanswered questions
remain regarding the chronological events preceding the
activation of an ACP domain and the mechanism of Ppant
transfer leading to the formation of the reaction product 3′,5′-
ADP. This study provides a unique opportunity to recapitulate
these events by combining a detailed structural analysis of key
steps with cutting-edge DFT-cluster and QM/MM MD
simulations, offering unparalleled insights into the activation
process.
In the apo-PptT structure, the void is filled by a histidine
residue (H-1) and the initiator methionine (M0) from an
adjacent PptT macromolecule in the crystal packing, replacing
the Ppant arm and the adenosine group (Figure 2A). To
investigate the potential inhibition of PptT activity in vitro, we
incubated PptT with a synthesized His-Met dipeptide (data
not shown). The rationale behind this experiment was to
determine if the dipeptide could bind to PptT and inhibit its
activity, thereby serving as a promising inhibitor for future
studies. Regrettably, no significant inhibition of the PptT
activity was observed. Furthermore, our attempts to crystallize
the PptT/His-Met complex were unsuccessful, suggesting that
the dipeptide may not bind tightly or specifically to the active
site of PptT.
We previously demonstrated that divalent metal ions are
essential for the post-translational modification of ACP
domains. In the active site cavity of PptAb of M. abscessus,9
two metal ion-binding sites have been identified, which are also
present in the PptT structure (Figure 2C). Site I plays a crucial
role in enzymatic activity by favorably orienting the side chain
of key catalytic residue E157, while site II contributes
significantly to the stability of the enzyme through numerous
direct and indirect water-mediated interactions. The fact that
metal binding can occur independently of CoA binding
suggests a two-step scenario in which metal ions bind first,
followed by CoA. This process would involve the local
repositioning of the metal ions, water molecules, and side-
chain residues, ultimately completing the coordination sphere.
CoA binding also leads to significant structural rearrangements
in two regions of the PPTase. Most of the changes occur in
residues R48, R56, and H93. The first two residues stabilize
the ribose moiety through direct interactions with the 3′-
phosphate group, while H93 is involved in stabilizing the 3′-
phosphate and α-phosphate of the 3′,5′-ADP moiety.
Despite the increasing number of isolated type I ACP
structures in the PDB, there is limited information available
regarding the structural organization of the linker regions at the
N- and C-terminal extremities. Using the AlphaFold2 server
(https://colab.research.google.com/github/sokrypton/
ColabFold/blob/main/AlphaFold2.ipynb), we conducted a
3D structure prediction for the ACP domain of PpsC, as
depicted in Figure S11A. This prediction unveiled a 16-amino
acid-long random coil starting at position 2041, as well as a 21-
amino acid-long unstructured segment followed by a 20-amino
acid-long α-helical segment that initiates at position 2145. This
α-helical segment, also referred to as the docking domain
(DD),23 plays a crucial role in mediating protein−protein
interactions between the upstream ACP and the downstream
DD of the ketosynthase (KS) domain within PpsD, thereby
facilitating the transfer of the polyketide chain. In our
previously reported X-ray structure of the PptAb-ACP
complex,9 only the amino acid residues constituting the
protein core of the ACP domain were visible in the electron
density map (Figure S11B). In this study, approximately half of
the N-terminal region could be reconstructed, which is
attributed to stabilization effects with an adjacent PptT
molecule within the crystal lattice. However, the predicted α-
helical region in this segment could not be confirmed (Figure
S11C). In contrast, residues within the C-terminal region
remain indiscernible.
In a previous study, we identified two potential activation
pathways in the initial step of the Ppant arm transfer
mechanism on the ACP domain.9 One pathway involves the
metal-bound E157, abstracting the hydrogen from S2105,
while the other suggests deprotonation of the hydroxyl group
of S2105 by an activated metal-bound water molecule. To
investigate these pathways, we conducted molecular modeling
coupled with QM/MM calculations. Our findings revealed that
the absence of an intermediate alkoxide group conflicts with
the hypothesis of a concerted proton transfer and a
nucleophilic attack on the β-phosphate of CoA. Subsequently,

Furthermore, we investigated the protonation reaction of the
3′,5′-ADP moiety using both the QM/MM and quantum
cluster methods. While both methods consistently described
this process, the quantum cluster model suggests, in contrast to
the QM/MM model, a bimolecular reaction mechanism
resulting in the formation of a single intermediate. This
discrepancy underscores the limitations of the quantum cluster
model in fully capturing the complexity of the enzymatic
environment. For instance, the restricted size of the active site
excludes specific effects from the solvent or distant residues
that may be crucial for catalytic activity. Additionally, allosteric
and entropic effects are generally not taken into account in this
approach.
Analysis of the energy profiles also reveals disparities
between models, with the QM/MM method showing a
reaction energy of 15.2 kcal/mol (Figure 6B), potentially
overestimated due to the application of a semiempirical level of
theory. In contrast, the DFT-cluster model yielded a more
reasonable reaction energy of 0.4 kcal/mol (Figure S8).
However, the same DFT-cluster model shows an over-
estimated energy barrier of 40.1 kcal/mol for the TS1 TS
compared to 19.0 kcal/mol calculated with the QM/MM
approach (TS1a). This difference may be attributed to the
inherent simplifications in the cluster model (Methods) and
the stabilizing effect of a more complex environment
considered in the QM/MM calculations. Notably, the DFT-
cluster calculations provide energies for the intermediate (IM)
and TS2 states, with values of 10.5 and 15.0 kcal/mol,
respectively, falling within the same range as those found with
QM/MM. Despite differences in the first energy barrier, both
methods yield similar path profiles, with step 1 emerging as the
limiting step in both cases. Both methods converge on
predicting an endergonic reaction energy and similar geo-
metries, thereby reinforcing our proposed pathway.
The attractiveness of PPTases as targets for the development
of new drugs has fueled numerous research initiatives. In the
case of Mtb PptT, collaborative efforts between academia and
the pharmaceutical industry have led to the identification of
promising molecules, such as amidinourea 8918, and
subsequent amidino-derived compounds active against PptT
both in vitro and in vivo4,5 and PptAb in vitro.24 However,
further optimization of these molecules is still necessary. A
recent high-throughput screening campaign against PptT has
emphasized the importance of obtaining an experimentally
solved apo structure as ligand binding can alter the active site,
potentially hindering inhibitor discovery.25 Taking advantage
of the structural changes associated with the four major steps
of the PptT catalytic cycle described in this study could benefit
the chemical structure optimization of new compounds.

MD Protocol. All classical MD simulations were carried out
using the AMBER package32 with the FF14SB force field.33
The parameters of CoA and Mg2+ ions were extracted from the
AMBER parameter database34,35 and prepared with Ante-
chamber programs.36 The LEaP module was used to prepare
the systems (PptT-CoA and PptT-CoA/ACP) and the H++37
server to adjust the residue protonation states at pH 7 (the
effect of pH is discussed in Figure S12). Both systems were
studied in an explicit solvent, surrounded with a rectangular
water box (with a separation of 10 Å between the protein and
the sides of the box), using the TIP3P model.38 Na+
counterions were added to neutralize the total charge. For
each prepared complex, 50,000 cycles of energy minimization
were first performed with a 500 kcal·mol−1·Å−2 constraint
applied to the solute. A second minimization without restraint
was performed on the whole system. Subsequently, the entire
system was heated from 0 to 300 K during 200 ps using a
Langevin thermostat with a collision frequency of 2 ps−1 at
constant volume. The restraints initially applied on the
complex were slowly released during the heating process,
and the system was then equilibrated for another 200 ps.
Finally, the production setup was implemented in the NPT
ensemble with 1 atm target pressure using the Berendsen

DFT-Cluster Protocol. The cluster model consists of 126
atoms, including a section of the CoA substrate where the
cysteamine tail and adenine base have been truncated and
replaced by a hydrogen atom. It also includes Mg2+ metal ions
and the side chains of H93, D114, E116, E157, and S2105
(Figure S7A). However, crucial residues in the vicinity of the
catalytic site, such as T92 and K161, which may significantly
stabilize the PptT-CoA/ACP complex, as predicted in the
QM/MM calculations, were not incorporated into the model.
Geometrical optimizations were carried out using DFT,
threatening the system with the hybrid B3LYP41,42 functional
in conjunction with the Grimme’s D3 dispersion43 correction.
The Ahlrichs def2-TZVP44 basis set was applied to all the
atoms, except for metal ions, where aug-cc-pVDZ45 was used.
Solvation was included using the conductor-like polarizable
continuum model (CPCM)46 method to estimate the
surrounding effects of the protein, with a chosen dielectric
constant of 4.
QM/MM Dynamics Protocol. The PptT-CoA/ACP
structure, previously obtained from classical MD, was used
for QM/MM calculations and SMD. We have considered a
179-atom QM region with a net charge of −4. This QM region
includes two Mg2+ ions coordinated by five water molecules,
the CoA ligand, and specific residues: E116, E157, and D114
coordinating Mg2+ at site I; H93 coordinating Mg2+ (II); and
D2104 and S2105 from the ACP domain. The QM/MM
boundary cuts were applied to C−C bonds, and the edges were
treated with hydrogen atom links.
During the QM/MM simulations, the quantum part was
treated at the semiempirical PM6 level of theory, while the
MM region was evaluated using the classical ff14SB force
field.33 The nonbonded cutoff for the long-range electrostatic
interactions was set at 12 and 8 Å for the MM and QM
regions, respectively. In the first place, the system was
minimized without constraint, employing the steepest-con-
jugate gradient scheme. It was then heated in the NVT
ensemble for 50 ps at 1 fs/step and equilibrated at a constant
density for 25 ps using the same ensemble. The stochastic
Langevin thermostat47 was used to keep the temperature at
300 K with a collision frequency of 2 ps−1. During the heating
and equilibration steps, a harmonic restraint was applied on the
PptT backbone and ACP domain, with a force constant of 10
kcal·mol−1·Å−2 initially, which was subsequently reduced to 1
kcal·mol−1·Å−2. Finally, the system was equilibrated in the NPT
ensemble for 50 ps without any restraints, and the pressure was
maintained around 1 atm using the Berendsen barostat48 with
a pressure relaxation of 2 ps. Throughout all steps, the SHAKE
algorithm49 was enabled to constraint bonds involving
nonpolar hydrogen atoms. From the last equilibration run,
we extracted a snapshot corresponding to the shortest
interatomic distance found between the atoms of OγS2105-
PβCoA (4.66 Å) and HγS2105-OεE157 (1.81 Å). The
extrapolated structure was considered the initial configuration
(the reactant R indicated in the energy graph of Figure 6B) for
steps 1a and 1b. The graphical representation is included in
Figure 6A.
