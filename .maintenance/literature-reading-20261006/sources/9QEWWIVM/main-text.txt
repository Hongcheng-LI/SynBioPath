Supporting Information
ABSTRACT: Two new compounds, kinanthraquinone C (1) and
kinanthraquinone D (2), were isolated along with two known
compounds, kinanthraquinone (3) and kinanthraquinone B (4),
produced by the heterologous expression of the kiq biosynthetic gene
cluster and its pathway-specific regulator, kiqA, in Streptomyces lividans
TK23. The chemical structures of compounds 1 and 2 were determined
using mass spectrometry and nuclear magnetic resonance analyses. To
examine a biosynthetic pathway of compounds 1 and 2, incubation
experiments were conducted using S. lividans TK23 to supply the
compounds 3 and 4. These experiments indicated that compounds 3 and
4 were converted to compounds 2 and 1, respectively, by the endogenous
enzymes of S. lividans TK23. Compounds 2, 3, and 4 had antimalarial
activities at half-maximal inhibitory concentration values of 0.91, 1.2, and
15 μM, respectively, without cytotoxicity up to 30 μM.
N
atural products, particularly the secondary metabolites of
actinomycetes, play an important role in drug discovery
because of their wide variety of chemical structures and
biological activities.1,2 Anthraquinones are important natural
products obtained from actinomycetes and other bacteria with
diverse structures and biological activities, such as cytotoxic,3−5
antibacterial,6 and antimalarial7 activities. Anthraquinones are
produced by type II polyketide synthases (PKSs) in actino-
mycetes.5,8−10 Kinanthraquinone (KQ, 3) was isolated from
Streptomyces reveromyceticus SN-593-44 in 2018. It contains an
uncommon side chain with a carboxamide group, making it a
rare natural product to be obtained from a microorganism.5
We studied its biological activity and identified the
biosynthetic gene cluster by heterologous gene expression in
Streptomyces lividans TK23. Furthermore, a high-producing
strain of compound 3 was generated by co-expression of the
kiqA gene, followed by isolation of KQB (4), a carboxamide-
free biosynthetic intermediate of compound 3.11 However, the
biological activity of compound 4 could not be assessed as a
result of the instability of the compound. In addition, the
analogues of compound 3 found in the high-producing strain
of compound 3 as minor metabolites have not been previously
characterized. In this study, we isolated new anthraquinones,
investigated their biological activities, compared them to those
of compounds 3 and 4, and analyzed their biosynthetic
pathways.

HRESIMS data indicated that the molecular formula of
compound 2 was C21H19NO7 (Figure S14 of the Supporting
Information). By comparing the molecular formulas of
compounds 2 and 3 (C21H19NO6), we speculated that there
is an additional hydroxy group. The UV−vis spectrum of
compound 2 (Figure S15 of the Supporting Information)
showed absorption maxima at 227, 269, 354, and 481 nm,
which were similar to that of compound 1, except for the
maximum at 420 nm in compound 1. The maximum
absorption of compound 2 at 481 nm shifted to the longer
wavelength compared to that of compound 1, suggesting that
the hydroxy group binds to the peri position of the carbonyl
group in compound 2, which is supported by chemical shifts of
two carbonyl groups in 13C NMR at 186.2 and 186.0 ppm
(Table 1). All proton and carbon connections in compound 2
were revealed by 1H and 13C NMR and HSQC studies (Table
1 and Figures S16−S18 of the Supporting Information). 1H
and 13C NMR chemical shift values of the tetra-substituted
benzene unit (C-5−C-10a) in compound 2 were consistent
with those of compound 1. In addition,
1H−13C HMBC
(Figure S20 of the Supporting Information) from H-8 (δH
8.14) to C-9 (δC 186.2) determined the position of the
carbonyl group of C-9. Positions of 5-OMe and 6-OMe of two
methoxy groups were determined by the HMBC from 5-OMe
(δH 3.83) and H-7 (δH 7.61) to C-5 (δC 149.6) and from 6-
OMe (δH 3.98) and H-8 (δH 8.14) to C-6 (δC 159.5),
respectively. The substructure of side chain in compound 2
was determined by HMBC from H-1′ (δH 6.50) and H3-5′ (δH
1.94) to C-3′ (δC 39.8), from H3-5′ to C-1′ (δC 121.1) and C-
2′ (δC 138.8), and from H2-3′ (δH 3.02) to C-4′ (δC 171.5) of
the carbonyl group (Figure 2A). In addition, 1H−15N HMQC
data suggested that 7.56 and 7.08 ppm signals in 1H NMR are
from an amide group (Figure S21 of the Supporting
Information). These results suggest that the side chain of
compound 2 is the same as of compound 3. The HMBC from
H-3 (δH 7.66) to C-1′ in the side chain and from H-1′ to C-3
(δC 127.0) indicated that the anthraquinone skeleton is
connected to the side chain. The HMBC from H-3 to C-1
(δC 155.0) and the 13C NMR chemical shift at C-1 indicated
that a hydroxy group is attached to C-1. An HMBC from H-3
to C-4a (δC 112.4) was detected. The chemical shift of C-4 (δC
156.2) in compound 2 was significantly downfield compared to
118.3 ppm in compound 1. In addition, the 1H NMR spectrum
of compound 2 showed H-3 as a singlet compared to the same
proton in compound 1, which appears as a doublet (Table 1).
This suggests hydroxylation of C-4, which could be confirmed
by the upfield shift of C-4a (112.4 ppm) in compound 2
compared to 133.0 ppm in compound 1 (Table 1). In addition,
the binding of the hydroxy group to C-4 was supported by the
UV−vis spectrum and chemical shift of the carbonyl group at
C-10 (δC 186.0). Therefore, compound 2 was determined to
be a new derivative of compound 3. The double bond at C-1′
in compound 2 was determined to be in the Z configuration on
the basis of the ROESY correlation (Figure S22 of the
Supporting Information) between H-1′ and H3-5′ (Figure 2B).
Production of Compounds 1 and 2 by Incubation
Experiments. To confirm the involvement of compounds 1
and 2 in the biosynthetic pathway of compound 3, incubation
experiments were performed using two S. lividans TK23 strains
that did not produce compound 3 or 4. When compound 4
was added to the strains, the conversion from compound 4 to
compound 1 within 24 h was confirmed by ultra-performance
liquid chromatography−mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS) anal-
ysis (chromatograms i−vi in Figure 3). By co-injecting
compound 1 with the culture extract (chromatogram vi in
Figure 3), we confirmed that the retention time was the same
as that of the standard (chromatogram vii in Figure 3).
Moreover, when compound 3 was added to the evaluated
strains, the conversion of compound 3 to compound 2 within
24 h was confirmed by UPLC−MS analysis (Figure 4). On the
other hand, conversions from compound 3 to compound 1 and
from compound 4 to compound 2 were not observed (Figures
S24 and S25 of the Supporting Information). Therefore, we
concluded that the endogenous enzymes of S. lividans TK23
catalyzed glycine amide formation and hydroxylation (Figure
S26 of the Supporting Information).

Escherichia coli, Aspergillus fumigatus, Pyricularia oryzae,
Candida albicans, and Plasmodium falciparum were evaluated
(Table 2 and Table S2 of the Supporting Information).

Compound 4 weakly inhibited S. aureus growth [half-maximal
inhibitory concentration (IC50) = 6.9 μg/mL (18 μM), Table
2]. Compounds 2, 3, and 4 were antimalarial, with IC50 values
of 0.91, 1.2, and 15 μM, respectively (Table 2). These results
showed that the hydroxy group at position C-4 within the
anthraquinone skeleton does not affect the antimalarial activity
and the presence of the carboxamide group resulted in better
growth inhibition activity against P. falciparum than carboxylic
acid. None of the compounds were cytotoxic against HeLa and
HL-60 up to 30 μM.
We have previously11 confirmed the conversion of
compound 4 to compound 3 using the endogenous enzyme
of S. lividans TK23. In this study, we confirmed the conversion
of compound 4 to compound 1 and compound 3 to
compound 2 with endogenous enzymes with broad substrate
specificity from the host strain.
■EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
General Experimental Procedures. Normal-phase column
chromatography was performed using a 0.063−0.200 mm silica gel
60. Preparative HPLC was performed on a Waters 600E pump system
(Waters, Milford, MA, U.S.A.) using a SenshuPak PEGASIL ODS
SP100 column (20 mm inner diameter × 250 mm or 10 mm inner
diameter × 250 mm, 5 μm, Senshu Scientific Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan).
Semi-preparative HPLC was performed on the same HPLC system
using a SenshuPak PEGASIL ODS SP100 column (6 mm inner
diameter × 250 mm, 5 μm, Senshu Scientific Co., Ltd.). UPLC−MS
analysis was performed using a Waters ACQUITY UPLC H-Class
system equipped with an ACQUITY QDa detector on a Waters
ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (2.1 mm inner diameter × 50
mm, 1.7 μm). High-resolution mass spectra were obtained using a
Waters SYNAPT G2 mass spectrometer. 1H and 13C NMR spectra
were recorded at 500 and 125 MHz, respectively, in CDCl3 or
deuterated dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO-d6) on a JEOL ECA-500 FT-
NMR spectrometer (JEOL, Tokyo, Japan). Chemical shifts are
reported in parts per million (ppm) and referenced to CDCl3 (7.26
ppm) and DMSO-d6 (2.49 ppm) in the 1H NMR spectra and DMSO-
d6 (39.5 ppm) in the
13C NMR spectra. UV−vis spectra were
measured using a JASCO V-630 BIO spectrophotometer (JASCO
International, Tokyo, Japan). Infrared (IR) spectra were recorded on
a HORIBA FT-720 spectrometer with a DuraSampl IR II ATR
instrument (HORIBA, Kyoto, Japan).
Fermentation. The high-producing strain of compound 3 (S.
lividans TK23/pKU592aac(3)IV::kiq2/pTYM19::sav2794p-kiqA) was
maintained on SY agar medium (0.1% yeast extract, 1% soluble starch,
0.1% N-Z-Amine type A, and 1.5% agar) supplemented with 0.25 μg/
mL apramycin and adjusted to pH 7.0 before sterilization. To produce
the seed culture, three agar pieces from a culture of the strain on a
Petri dish were separately inoculated in three K1 flasks containing 70
mL of seed medium (0.35% yeast extract, 1.4% soluble starch, 0.35%
glucose, 0.21% Bacto peptone, 0.21% Difco beef extract, 0.014%
KH2PO4, and 0.042% MgSO4·7H2O adjusted to pH 7.6 before
sterilization). The flasks were incubated on a rotary shaker (150 rpm)
at 28 °C for 4 days. For the production culture, 2.6 mL seed culture
(OD600 = 3.0) aliquots were inoculated into 68 K1 flasks containing
70 mL of Bacto tryptic soy broth (TSB, soybean−casein digest
medium) and incubated on a rotary shaker (150 rpm) at 28 °C for 3
days.
Extraction, Purification, and Isolation of Compounds. A
total of 4.8 L of acetone was added to the culture broth (4.8 L), which
was then filtered to remove the bacterial cells. The 50% acetone
extract (9.6 L) evaporated under reduced pressure to remove acetone.
The pH of the remaining aqueous solution (4.8 L) was adjusted to 5
using acetic acid and extracted twice using 5 L of EtOAc (total 10 L).
The EtOAc layer was mixed with silica gel resin (15 g), and then dried
using a rotary evaporator. The silica gel adsorbate was subjected to
column chromatography (70 mm inner diameter × 80 mm) and
eluted stepwise using a CHCl3−MeOH mixture (100:0 and 0:100).

Incubation Experiment. Incubation experiments were performed
using a modified method previously described.11 S. lividans TK23/
pKU592aac(3)IV (strain transformed with empty vector) and S.
lividans TK23/pKU592aac(3)IV::kiq2ΔkiqG (kiqG gene disruptant,
which abolished the production of compound 3 derivatives) were
prepared on M4YE agar plates containing 5 μg/mL apramycin for 3
days at 28 °C. Five agar pieces were added to each of 24 mm inner
diameter test tubes containing 10 mL of SK2 medium with 5 μg/mL
apramycin and cultured for 2 days at 28 °C and 150 rpm. The
preculture (OD600 = 3, 2 mL) was added to 100 mL of TSB, mixed
well, and then divided into each of 24 mm inner diameter test tubes
containing the production medium (10 mL). After culturing for 2
days, compound 3 or 4 was added to a final concentration of 0.1 mM
or DMSO was added as the control. The culture broth was collected
after 24 h, and an equal amount of acetone was added. After
centrifugation, 50% acetone extract (5 mL) was concentrated. The
solution (2.5 mL) was extracted thrice with an equal amount of
EtOAc. The organic layer was concentrated and dissolved in 50 μL of
MeOH. The samples were analyzed using an UPLC−MS system.
Solvent A comprised 0.1% aqueous formic acid, and solvent B
comprised MeCN with 0.1% formic acid. After column equilibration
with 10% solvent B, 2 μL of sample was injected into the column,
which was developed using a linear gradient of 10−100% solvent B for
3 min.
Evaluation of Biological Activities. The antimicrobial and
antimalarial activities against S. aureus 209, E. coli HO141, A.
fumigatus Af293, P. oryzae kita-1, C. albicans JCM1542, and P.
falciparum 3D7 as well as the in vitro cytotoxicity against the human
cervical epidermal carcinoma cell line HeLa and human promyelo-
cytic leukemia cell line HL-60 were evaluated using previously
reported methods.12
