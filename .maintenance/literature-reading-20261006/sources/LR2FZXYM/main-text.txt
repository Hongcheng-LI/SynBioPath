ABSTRACT: Lipidation is a promising strategy to enhance the
membrane affinity and the serum stability of peptide drugs.
Cyanobactin prenyltransferases catalyze the prenylation of peptides
with diverse chemoselectivity. Among these enzymes, AutF and
AgcF catalyze the mono- and bisprenylations of Arg-Nω,
respectively, although the structural basis for these distinct
prenylation modes remained unknown. Through genome mining,
we herein identified a new Arg-Nω-bisprenyltransferase, DciF, from
Dolichospermum circinale AWQC310F. Crystallographic analysis
and subsequent mutagenesis studies identified key active site
residues that play pivotal roles in Arg-Nω-bisprenylation. Manip-
ulations of the active site pocket successfully converted an Arg-Nω-
bisprenyltransferase into an Arg-Nω-monoprenyltransferase, and vice versa, elucidating its role as a determinant factor for prenylation
rounds. DciF efficiently catalyzed Arg-Nω-bisprenylation on various cyclic and linear peptides, demonstrating its remarkable potential
as a biocatalytic tool for site-selective peptide modification. This study broadens the scope of biocatalytic peptide modifications and
establishes a new framework for engineering cyanobactin prenyltransferases to control their prenylation rounds.
■INTRODUCTION
Owing to their large molecular surfaces, medium-sized cyclic
peptides can potentially interact with biological targets
previously considered “undruggable” by conventional low-
molecular-weight drugs; however, they often exhibit low
membrane permeability and metabolic stability.1 As a result,
extensive structural optimization is typically required to
improve the pharmacokinetic properties during peptide-based
drug development. One effective strategy to enhance
membrane affinity and serum stability involves introducing a
lipophilic moiety into the peptide (lipidation).2 Indeed,
lipophilic groups are present in FDA-approved peptide drugs
as well as in biologically active natural peptides such as
daptomycin and cyclosporine.
Cyanobactins are a structurally diverse group of naturally
occurring peptides produced by cyanobacteria.3 Their peptide
backbones are synthesized by the ribosome and subsequently
modified post-translationally by a set of biosynthetic enzymes.
These modifications include azol(in)e-forming heterocycliza-
tion of the backbone, head-to-tail macrocyclization, and
prenylation. Prenylation is catalyzed by a cyanobactin
prenyltransferaseoften referred to as an F enzymeusually
as the final stage of cyanobactin biosynthesis.4 Unlike other
ribosomally derived peptide-modifying enzymes, which strictly
require a recognition sequence (RS) either upstream or
downstream of the substrate sequence, cyanobactin prenyl-
transferases act after the RS is removed. Thus, they are unique
peptide-modifying enzymes that act in an RS-independent
manner.
Cyanobactin prenyltransferases are generally highly specific
regarding the prenyl donor (e.g., dimethylallyl diphosphate or
geranyl diphosphate), its orientation (forward or reverse), and
the prenyl acceptor, while they often display a remarkable
tolerance for overall substrate sequences.4 Due to this
combination of specificity and flexibility, cyanobactin prenyl-
transferases are considered to be valuable biochemical tools for
site-selective peptide alkylation. To date, several cyanobactin
prenyltransferases with distinct selectivities have been identi-
fied. Known prenylation sites include Tyr-O,5−9 Ser/Thr-O,10
Trp-N,11 Trp-C3,12,13 Arg-Nω,14−16 and His-C2,17 as well as
the termini of linear substrates.18,19 Among these, the Tyr-O-
prenyltransferase PagF,8 the His-C2-geranyltransferase LimF,17
and the Trp-C3-prenyltransferase KgpF13 have been shown to
tolerate an extremely broad range of substrate sequences,
thereby enabling site-selective modifications of these positions

through biocatalytic approaches. In addition, cyanobactin
prenyltransferases have been the subject of intensive protein
engineering efforts to expand product diversity. To date,
modifications have successfully altered specificity for the prenyl
donor,7,20 the prenyl acceptor residue,17 and the local sequence
near the acceptor residue.21 However, enzyme engineering to
alter the number of prenyl groups introduced remains
unprecedented.
Guanidine prenylation is a unique modification in alkaloids
and peptides, and several enzymatic bases have only been
recently elucidated.14,15,22 AutF in autumnalamide biosyn-
thesis15 and AgcF in argicyclamide biosynthesis14 are
cyanobactin prenyltransferases that selectively modify the
Arg-Nω of peptides (Figure 1a). While both enzymes share
the same chemoselectivity, their outcomes are distinct: AutF
catalyzes the prenylation of a single Arg-Nω to yield a
monoprenylguanidine moiety, whereas AgcF performs an
additional round of prenylation on the other Arg-Nω, resulting
in a bisprenylguanidine moiety. AgcF’s ability to catalyze
multiple rounds of prenylation on the same residue is unique,
making it the only known bisprenyltransferase among
cyanobactin prenyltransferases. However, the molecular basis
of this unique prenylation mode remains unknown.
■RESULTS
DciF is a New Arg-Nω-bisprenyltransferase. The
phylogenetic clades of cyanobactin prenyltransferases are
based on their chemoselectivities.4 Therefore, to explore the
Arg-Nω-prenyltransferase family members present in a public
database, we subjected the top 100 BLAST hits, which were
identified using AgcF (BCR82584.1) as the query, to
phylogenetic analysis. As a result, AutF and AgcF formed a
distinct clade together with 16 putative prenyltransferases
(Figure S1). This clade is further divided into three subclades,
with the first and second clades represented by AgcF and AutF,
respectively. The third clade lacked any characterized enzymes;
therefore, we focused on this putative clade and chose DciF

Structural Basis of Arg-Nω-bisprenyltransferase Re-
actions. To obtain insights into the structural basis of Arg-Nω-
prenylation, we solved the crystal structure of apo DciF at a 2.6
Å resolution. The overall structure adopted a truncated ABBA
fold23 consisting of 10 α-helices and 10 β-sheets, a typical
conformation observed in cyanobactin prenyltrans-
ferases7,9,13,17,24 (Figures 2a and S5a and Table S9). The
putative catalytic residue E49, a general base that is conserved
among cyanobactin prenyltransferases, exists in the middle of
the barrel scaffold to form a putative active site (Figures 2b and
S5b).
The unique feature of a bisprenyltransferase is the
acceptance of a monoprenylated compound for the addition
of a second prenyl group. To determine how DciF achieves
this transformation, a simplified substrate (monoprenyl Arg)
was docked onto the apo DciF structure, together with Mg2+
and DMAPP (Figure 2b). In the complex model, which was
relaxed by a 50 ns molecular dynamics (MD) simulation,
DMAPP was located on the DMAPP-binding site in a manner
similar to that observed in cocrystal structures of homologous
enzymes (Figure S6). The prenylated Nω interacted with E49,
the catalytic residue conserved among cyanobactin prenyl-
transferases (Figure S5b). On the other hand, the non-
prenylated Nω (denoted as Nω′ in Figure 2b) was positioned
3.9 Å on average from the C1 atom of the Mg-coordinated
DMAPP during the MD simulation (Figure S6), likely
representing the state for the second prenyl transfer. This
Nω′ also interacts with H167. An equivalent His residue is
present in the His-C2-geranyltransferase LimF, where it
interacts with the acceptor His in cocrystal structures and
modulates LimF’s chemospecificity (Figure S6).17 Notably, in
DciF, H167 further interacted with T134, forming a putative
dyad. This dyad is apparently conserved specifically among the
Arg-Nω-prenyltransferase clade (Figures S1 and S5b), implying
its significant role in Arg-Nω-prenylation. To ascertain the
importance of these residues, E49, T134, and H167 in DciF
were individually substituted with Ala. As a result, all variants
lost enzymatic activity (Figure 2c), showing their crucial roles
in Arg-Nω-prenylation.
A Key Active Site Pocket Dictates the Number of
Prenylation Rounds. In the docking model, the prenyl group
of the ligand was accommodated in a pocket near E49 (Figure
3a). This pocket is shaped by five residues: S51, R63, G65,
M116, and K131. Notably, the size of this pocket was
substantially decreased in the AlphaFold model of AutF, an
Arg-Nω-monoprenyltransferase, due to the substitution of G65
with D65 (Figure 3b). This bulkier substitution presumably
generates steric hindrance to exclude the monoprenylated
compound, eventually inhibiting the second round of
prenylation in AutF. Based on these observations, we
hypothesized that the size of the pocket near E49 is a primary
factor that dictates the number of prenylations by an Arg-Nω-
prenyltransferase.
To test this, G65 in DciF was substituted with Ala, Val, Ile,
or Leu. While DciF WT quantitatively converted 1 to bisprenyl
1 (bp-1), the G65A variant accumulated both monoprenyl 1
(mp-1) and bp-1, indicating a decreased velocity for the
second round of prenylation (Figure S7). The amount of mp-1
increased as G65 was substituted with bulkier residues, and
finally, bp-1 formation was abolished in the G65I and G65L

To determine whether the simple spatial expansion of the
active site in a monoprenyltransferase confers the activity of
bisprenylation, we expanded the pocket in AutF by substituting
D65 with Gly. While wild-type AutF catalyzed the monop-
renylation of linear substrate 2 with moderate conversion

activity and generated bisprenylated 2 (Figure 3d). Taken
together, the number of prenylation rounds was switched vice
versa by a single residue substitution in the active site. This
result firmly establishes the role of the pocket near E49 as a
determining factor for the number of prenylation rounds in
Arg-Nω-prenyltransferases.
DciF is a Highly Versatile Bisprenyltransferase. To
evaluate the potential utility of DciF as a biocatalyst for Arg-
Nω-specific peptide modification, we next investigated its
substrate scope.
First, we evaluated the tolerance of DciF for an alternative
prenyl donor using geranyl diphosphate (GPP). As a result, no
geranylated product was observed, indicating a marked
preference of DciF for DMAPP as the prenyl donor (Figure
S8). In GPP-accepting cyanobactin prenyltransferases such as
LimF and PirF, a conserved glycine residue (G224 in LimF
and G221 in PirF) creates space to accommodate the bulkier
C10 prenyl group.7,22 In contrast, the corresponding positions
in both DciF and AgcF are occupied by a cysteine residue
(Cys219 in DciF), which likely results in steric hindrance
(Figure S5b), consistent with their preference for C5 prenyl
donors.
Next, we evaluated the tolerance of DciF for prenyl
acceptors. To this end, DciF was tested with three additional
Arg-containing cyanobactins (i.e., argicyclamide C, despreny-
lautumnalamide A, and autumnalamide B) and six additional
cyclopeptides 3−8, whose sequences are based on PP genes
encoded near Arg-Nω-prenyltransferases (Figure S2). DciF
catalyzed the bisprenylation of most of the cyclic peptides with
various conversion efficiencies, ranging from 3% for 8 to
quantitative conversion for 7 (Table 1 and Figure S9).
However, DciF failed to prenylate 6. In addition, DciF did not
process the D-configured Arg in desprenylautumnalamide A
(Figure S10). The scope of DciF was further assessed using
three linear peptides: 2, seco-argicyclamide C, and seco-1.

versatile catalyst when the prenylation site is flanked by at least
one hydrophobic residue. In contrast, the presence of Pro or an
acidic residue on the C-terminal side is contraindicated, leading
to a significant drop in conversion.
■DISCUSSION
Based on the biochemical data, plausible mechanisms for Arg-
Nω-bisprenylation are envisioned as shown in Figure 5. The
omega-nitrogens interacting with E49 and H167 are denoted
as Nω and Nω′, respectively. The first round of prenylation
begins with the deprotonation of Nω by E49, a common
catalytic base in cyanobactin prenyltransferases. A dyad
comprising H167 and T134 is positioned on the opposite
side and is presumably involved in this deprotonating process,
facilitating subsequent Nω′-prenylation. The mutations of
either T134 or H167 abolished the enzymatic activity,
consistent with their proposed roles (Figure 2c). Between
the first and second prenylation, several scenarios can be
considered: (i) the monoprenyl product first dissociates from
the active site and rebinds with the flipped orientation of the
guanidine for the second-round prenylation. Alternatively, (ii)
the monoprenyl product would not dissociate, but the
guanidine moiety rotates within the active site, accomplishing
the second-round prenylation. It is also possible that (iii) the
second DMAPP binds to the active site with its prenyl group
oriented in a distinct direction, perhaps accommodated in the
pocket formed by G65 on the E49 side of DciF, allowing the
monoprenylated product to undergo a second prenylation
without dissociation or rotation. The transient accumulation of
the monoprenyl product is observed at the initial phase of the
reaction with DciF (5−30 min in Figure 1b). A similar yet
clearer accumulation of monoprenyl product is also observed
in the AgcF reaction.14 These observations suggest, but do not
exclusively support, the (i) dissociation and rebound
mechanism. Notably, the rapid consumption of the monop-
renylated intermediate suggests that it is processed more
efficiently than the nonprenylated substrate. Further inves-
tigations are currently being undertaken to elucidate the details
of the bisprenylation mechanism.
H167 is also present in the dual-functional His-C2/Tyr-O-
geranyltransferase LimF as H172. It is essential for the His-C2-
geranylation activity but not for the Tyr-O-geranylation
activity, highlighting its role in defining the chemospecificity
of LimF. Notably, the His-Thr dyad is absent in LimF and is
exclusively found in Arg-Nω-prenyltransferases such as AgcF,
AutF, and DciF. Further elucidation of T134’s role in
modulating the local environment surrounding the catalytic
His should offer valuable insights into the chemospecificity of
cyanobactin prenyltransferases.
Due to their enormous potential as tools for site-selective
peptide modification, cyanobactin prenyltransferases have been
the focus of intensive enzyme engineering efforts aimed at
expanding their scope. These efforts have primarily concen-
trated on altering the specificity toward prenyl donors,7,20
prenyl acceptors,17 and the local environment adjacent to the
prenylation site (i.e., residues flanking the prenylation sites).21
Herein, we demonstrate the manipulation of prenylation
rounds by a single residue substitution, representing a new
mode of enzyme engineering in this family. This finding
suggests that prenylation rounds in homologues with different
chemoselectivities can also be manipulated through the proper
expansion of the binding pocket. Some amino acid residues
possess multiple modifiable sites for cyanobactin prenyltrans-
ferases; for example, Trp-N is prenylated by AcyF,11 while Trp-
C3 is prenylated by KgpF.12,13 Tyrosine also has multiple
prenylation sites: the reverse prenylation of Tyr-O, catalyzed
by LynF5 and its homologues, involves a Claisen rearrange-
ment that yields a product with C-prenylation at the ortho
position. This reaction regenerates Tyr-O as a potential
acceptor site for further prenylation, eventually leading to the
formation of a bisprenylated phenol group similar to that
produced by AcPT1, a membrane-bound UbiA-type prenyl-
transferase.25 We anticipate that the new direction of enzyme
engineering toward prenylation rounds established herein may
further expand the product diversity achievable with this
prolific enzyme family.
■CONCLUSIONS
We identified a new Arg-Nω-bisprenyltransferase, DciF, from
Dolichospermum circinale AWQC310F. Our crystallographic
analysis and docking study identified an active site pocket to
accommodate the prenyl moiety, enabling the second round of
prenylation. Mutagenesis of the pocket residues switched an
Arg-Nω-monoprenyltransferase into an Arg-Nω-bisprenyltrans-
ferase, and vice versa. DciF catalyzes the Arg-Nω-bisprenylation
of linear or cyclic peptides, ranging from as short as 3 to as
long as 31 residues. The versatile Arg-Nω-prenyltransferase
DciF represents a valuable addition to the cyanobactin
prenyltransferase toolbox, expanding the repertoire of bio-
catalytic site-selective peptide modifications. These modifica-
tions facilitate the efficient structural diversification and
enhance peptide pharmacokinetic properties. Furthermore,
this study establishes a new direction in engineering
cyanobactin prenyltransferases: the manipulation of prenyla-
tion rounds.
