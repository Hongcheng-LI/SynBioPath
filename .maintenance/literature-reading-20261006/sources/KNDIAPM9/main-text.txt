Mallotumides A−C: Potent Cytotoxic Cycloheptapeptides from the
Roots of Mallotus spodocarpus

ABSTRACT: The structures of potent cytotoxic cycloheptapeptides,
mallotumides A−C (1−3, respectively) isolated from the roots of
Mallotus spodocarpus Airy Shaw, were elucidated by extensive
spectroscopic analysis. The absolute configuration of 1 was
determined by single-crystal X-ray crystallographic data. All three
cycloheptapeptides exhibited potent cytotoxicity against various
cancer cell lines with IC50 values ranging from 0.60 to 4.02 nM.
T
he Mallotus plants, belonging to the Euphorbiaceae
family, are primarily found in the subtropical region.
Leaves of Mallotus furetianus have been popularly consumed as
aromatic tea and are used to treat hyperglycemia.1
Phytochemical investigation of plants in the Mallotus genus
resulted in the isolation of a diverse array of compounds, such
as terpenoids, diterpenes, phenols, flavonoids, lignans, and
amide derivatives of fatty acids.2−6
Mallotus spodocarpus Airy Shaw is a small endemic plant in
Thailand. Traditionally, its powdered roots are used for skin
whitening. Our group has a continuous interest in searching for
new biologically active compounds from natural products.
Bioassay-guided chromatographic purification of the chloro-
form extract of the roots of M. spodocarpus led to the isolation
of VR3848 and VR12684. Their spectroscopic data (unpub-
lished results) suggested that they are cyclic peptides bearing
seven amino acid residues; however, the information with
regard to their exact gross structures and the absolute
configuration at stereogenic carbons was lacking. Biological
studies of VR3848 and VR12684, including analgesic, anti-
inflammatory, and cytotoxic activities, revealed their highly
potent activities, highlighting that these compounds are
promising for further development.7−11 Herein, we re-isolated
these compounds to determine the structures, including the
absolute configurations. A complete structural elucidation
would be essential, as the pharmacophore is required for
structural optimization and target validation.
In our work, we first screened the cytotoxicity of the freshly
prepared chloroform extract of M. spodocarpus and its fractions
(Table S3). Bioassay-guided purification together with refined
HPLC separation led to the isolation of three cyclo-
heptapeptides named mallotumides A−C (1−3, respectively)
(Figure 1) along with four known compounds (4−7) (Figure
S1).2,12,13 A comparison of NMR data suggested that
mallotumide A (1) and mallotumide B (2) were identical to
VR12684 and VR3848, respectively; however, a detailed
structural elucidation of these compounds has never been
published. Herein, we describe structural determination based
on spectroscopic data (one- and two-dimensional NMR as well
as MS/MS fragmentation). The absolute configuration of
mallotumide A (1) was established by means of single-crystal
X-ray crystallographic analysis.
Mallotumide A (1) had a molecular formula of C46H67N7O13
established by HRESIMS at m/z 948.4688 [M + Na]+ (calcd
for C46H67N7NaO13, 948.4695) and an optical rotation (OR)
value of [α]D
21 −82.1 (c 0.54, MeOH). The IR spectrum gave a
characteristic amide absorption band at 1633 cm−1. The NMR
spectra of 1 showed patterns similar to those of previously
reported cycloheptapeptides.14 In the 13C NMR and DEPT
data (Table S1), the presence of seven amide carbonyl carbons

The 1H NMR spectrum (Table S2) showed typical resonances
of peptides, including seven α-methine protons (δH 5.84, 4.98,
4.78, 4.64, 4.58, 4.57, and 4.48), four amide protons (δH 7.87,
7.55, 6.44, and 5.98), and three N-methyl protons (δH 3.05,
3.01, and 2.95). The amino acid residues were established on
the basis of HMBC and COSY correlations (Figure 2) as well
as ROESY and TOCSY data (Figures S5, S12, and S13),
suggesting the presence of two isoleucines (Ile1 and Ile2), two
N-methylalanines (Me-Ala1 and Me-Ala2), and three unusual
amino acid residues that were determined to be Me-β-(3-
furyl)-O-acetylserine (Me-FAS), β-(3-furyl)-O-acetylserine
(FAS), and α-(2,3-dimethylcyclopropyl)glycine (DMCG).
For the Me-FAS residue, HMBC correlations were observed
from H-α (δH 4.64) to CO (δC 165.5) and C-3 of the furyl
ring (δC 120.8). Furthermore, the correlations from H3 of the
acetyl group (δH 1.99) and H-2 of the furyl ring (δH 6.44) to
C-β (δC 65.1) permitted the assignment for the location of the
acetyl and the 3-furyl groups. Additionally, the correlations
from H3-N (δH 2.95) to C-α (δC 62.3) suggested this residue is
an N-methyl amino acid.
For the FAS residue, in comparison to Me-FAS, the obvious
differences were the replacement of the H3-N (δH 2.95) signal
in Me-FAS with the H-N (δH 6.44) signal in FAS as well as the
observation of HMBC correlations from H-N to C-α (δC
55.7). This analysis was further supported by HMBC, ROESY
and TOCSY experiments (Figures S4, S5, S12, and S13).
For the DMCG residue, the HMBC correlations were
observed from H-Ν (δH 7.55) to C-α (δC 48.0) and from Η-α
(δH 4.58) to CO (δC 173.1). Moreover, the TOCSY
correlations among H-N/H-α, Η-α/Η-β, Η-β/Η-γ, Η-β/Η-δ,
Η-γ/Η-δ, H3-γ/Η-γ, and H3-δ/Η-δ along with the HMBC
correlations from H3-γ (δH 1.08), H3-δ (δH 0.97), and H-N (δH
7.55) to C-β (δC 25.1) suggested that this residue is a DMCG.
The relative configuration of the 2,3-dimethylcyclopropyl unit
was deduced by the NOESY experiment (Figure S11). The
NOESY correlations of Η-α (δH 4.58) with Η-γ (δH 0.78) and
H3-δ (δH 0.97) supported the idea that they oriented on the
same face. On the contrary, the NOESY cross-peak of H3-γ (δH
1.08) with Η-δ (δH 0.20) suggested that they are located on
the same side. Finally, the assignment of the chemical shifts
and relative configurations of DMCG was established by
comparison of its NMR data with those of a similar scaffold
constituted in cycloheptapeptides, isolated from Maytenus
variabilis (Loes.) C. Y. Cheng (Celastraceae family).14
The amino acid sequence was established by analysis of the
HMBC data (Figure 2). The HMBC spectrum of 1 showed
correlations from Ile1-NH (δH 7.87) to FAS-CO (δC 168.2),
from FAS-NH (δH 6.44) to Me-Ala2-CO (δC 172.7), from Me-
Ala2-NMe (δH 3.05) to DMCG-CO (δC 173.1), from DMCG-
NH (δH 7.55) to Me-FAS-CO (δC 165.5), from Me-FAS-NMe
(δH 2.95) to Me-Ala1-CO (δC 171.7), from Me-Ala1-NMe (δH
3.01) to Ile2-CO (δC 170.7), and from Ile2-NH (δH 5.98) to
Ile1-CO (δC 171.6), suggesting that all seven constituent amino
acid residues were connected by six amide bonds. This
assignment was further confirmed by ROESY experiments
(Figures S5 and S12). From the analysis described above,
compound 1 was identified as a cycloheptapeptide with an Ile1-
Ile2-Me-Ala1-Me-FAS-DMCG-Me-Ala2-FAS sequence (Figure
2). Furthermore, the results of the MS/MS analysis of 1
(Figure S14) agreed well with the two-dimensional structure,
as deduced from NMR analysis.
Next, an attempt to address the absolute configuration of the
natural amino acid residues in 1 was performed by acid
hydrolysis, followed by chromatographic purification of
hydrolysate and HPLC analysis (Sumichiral OA-5000L
column, 4.6 mm × 150 mm; mobile phase of 5% IPA in 2
mM aqueous CuSO4) (Figure S33). Co-injection using
commercially available L-Me-Ala revealed that 1 contained L-
Me-Ala. HPLC analysis further indicated that the hydrolysate
of 1 did not contain
L-Ile. Unfortunately, attempts to
distinguish between D-Ile and D-allo-Ile cannot be achieved
by HPLC experiments. Finally, an X-ray crystallographic
analysis (Figure 3 and Figure S2) confirmed the assigned
structure and absolute configurations of 1. Thus, the structure
of mallotumide A (1) was determined to be cyclo-[(2R,3S)-D-
allo-Ile1]-[(2R,3S)-D-allo-Ile2]-[(2S)-L-Me-Ala1]-[(2S,3R)-L-
threo-Me-FAS]-[(2S,4S,5S)-DMCG]-[(2S)-Me-Ala2]-
[(2R,3S)-D-threo-FAS].

Mallotumides A−C (1−3, respectively) were deduced to
contain amino acid constituents with the same absolute
configuration according to their optical rotation values and
similar patterns in the electronic circular dichroism data
(Figure S34).
On the basis of the previous knowledge of the potent
cytotoxicity of VR38488,9 and VR12684,10,11 we revisited the
cytotoxic effects of analytically pure mallotumides A−C (1−3,
respectively) on six cancer cell lines (KKU-M213, FaDu, HT-
29, MDA-MB-231, A549, and SH-SY5Y) and human
cholangiocyte cells (MMNK-1) by the SRB assay. Ellipticine,
a potent antineoplastic agent against several types of cancer cell
lines, was used as a positive control (Table S4). Compounds
1−3 exhibited a potent cytotoxic effect on all tested cancer cell
lines, with the IC50 values ranging from 0.60 to 4.80 nM.
Interestingly, the cytotoxicity of these peptides was approx-
imately 2000 times higher than that of ellipticine, which was
used as a positive control. In comparison with previously
reported cycloheptapeptides I−III (Figure S3) bearing one or
two 2,3-dimethylcyclopropyl residues, isolated from the stem
bark of M. variabilis,14 mallotumides A−C exhibited similar
cytotoxic activity against human lung carcinoma cells (A549),
highlighting the potential importance of the novel 2,3-
dimethylcyclopropyl moiety (Tables S4 and S5). It is worth
mentioning that ternatin, a structurally similar cycloheptapep-
tide (Figure S3)15 that does not have the 2,3-dimethylcycloo-
propyl group in its molecule, is not as potent as cyclo-
heptapeptides I−III and mallotumides A−C.
The cytotoxic activity of 1 and 2 was emphasized in
hypopharyngeal carcinoma (FaDu) and keratinocyte (HaCaT)
cells using an MTT assay. Due to the limited quantity, the
activity of 3 was not included. Compounds 1 and 2 exhibited
dose- and time-dependent cytotoxic effects in FaDu and
HaCaT cell lines (Figure 4). The IC50 values in FaDu cells
were lower than those in HaCaT cells, especially at 24 and 48
h. Moreover, the IC50 values of 1 and 2 in FaDu and HaCaT
cells decreased with an increase in incubation time, indicating
time-dependent cytotoxic activity. The IC50 values of 1 and 2
in FaDu cells are 70, 2, and 0.8 nM and 325, 5.2, and 4.5 nM at
24, 48, and 72 h, respectively. The IC50 values of 1 and 2 in
HaCaT cells are 350, 18, and 3.8 nM and 500, 10, and 1 nM at
24, 48, and 72 h, respectively. Interestingly, 1 and 2 were
markedly more cytotoxic than cisplatin, the standard drug for
treating head and neck cancer.
As mallotumide A (1) and mallotumide B (2) were identical
to VR12684 and VR3848, respectively, we compared
cytotoxicity data to those from previous reports.8−11 The
cytotoxicity of mallotumide A (1) and mallotumide B (2)
against head and neck cancer cells was in the nanomolar range,
comparable to those of VR12684 and VR3848. Modes of
action of VR38488,9 and VR1268410,11 were suggested to
partly go through mitochondrion- and endoplasmic reticulum-
associated apoptosis. We are currently investigating the
molecular target underlying the anticancer activity of
mallotumide A (1) along with structural optimization. The
results will be reported in due course.

products produced by prokaryotic and eukaryotic organisms.

Many of these peptides containing nonproteinogenic amino
acids have important medical uses. In particular, only a few
peptides have been found to bear a nonproteinogenic amino
acid, i.e., a 3-(3-furyl)-containing residue, for example,
rhizonins and bingchamide B. Rhizonins were reported to be
isolated from bacterial endosymbiont Burkholderia endofungo-
rum inside Rhizopus microsporus, a fungus used to prepare
fermented food,16 and bingchamide B was isolated from
Streptomyces bingchenggensis.17 Each of the compounds showed
potent cytotoxicity in various cancer cell lines. Additionally,
while L-cyclopropylglycine has recently been disclosed to be
produced by enzymatic electrosynthesis,18 2,3-dimethylcyclo-
propylglycine is rare. To the best of our knowledge, 2,3-
dimethylcyclopropylglycine was previously reported to be
found in cycloheptapeptides, isolated from M. variabilis (Loes.)
C. Y. Cheng.14 On the basis of these findings, the biosynthetic
pathways of the nonproteinogenic amino acids incorporated in
mallotumides A−C, including Me-β-(3-furyl)-O-acetylserine
(Me-FAS), β-(3-furyl)-O-acetylserine (FAS), and α-(2,3-
dimethylcyclopropyl)glycine (DMCG), might be hypotheti-
cally associated with a plant endosymbiont similar to other
naturally cyclic peptides.20,21 While the the biosynthetic origin
of nonproteinogenic amino acids Me-FAS, FAS, and DMCG
requires further investigation, biosynthesis of a closely related
3-(3-furyl)-alanine was reported by König and co-workers.19
The 3-(3-furyl)-alanine was proposed to be synthesized using
phosphoenolpyruvate and erythrose 4-phosphate as precursors
through the shikimate pathway.
In conclusion, we disclosed three cycloheptapeptides
isolated from M. spodocarpus, mallotumides A−C (1−3,
respectively). Their structures were deduced to contain non-
natural amino acid residues. The absolute configuration of 1
was confirmed through X-ray crystallographic data. Mallotu-
mides A−C (1−3, respectively) exhibited remarkable cytotox-
icity against all tested cancer cell lines with IC50 values in the
range of 0.60−4.02 nM. In addition, 1 and 2 exhibited dose-
and time-dependent cytotoxic effects against FaDu and HaCaT
cell lines, with IC50 values lower than that of cisplatin, the
standard drug for the treatment of head and neck cancer. This
discovery identified natural compounds with remarkable
potential for further development as anticancer therapeutics.
