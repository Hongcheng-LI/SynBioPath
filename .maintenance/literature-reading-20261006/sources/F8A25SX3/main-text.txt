ABSTRACT: Although ketosynthases in bacterial type II fatty
acid biosynthesis have been extensively studied, the human
mitochondrial ketosynthase, OXSM, remains incompletely charac-
terized. Contrary to the assumption that the role of mitochondrial
fatty acid biosynthesis is limited to the production of the lipoic
acid precursor octanoate, recent studies suggest an ability to
produce longer chain fatty acids. Here, we employ covalent, dual
site-selective cross-linkers to trap the interactions between OXSM
and its mitochondrial acyl carrier protein partner, mACP. Two
high-resolution crystal structures that capture OXSM bound to
mACP provide molecular details of the conformational changes
that guide the chain elongation process. We identify key protein •
protein and protein • substrate interactions that regulate the
transacylation and condensation steps associated with this process.
We also observe a conserved gating mechanism previously identified in bacterial type II ketosynthases. Complemented by site-
directed mutagenesis and activity analyses, these findings provide detailed insight into the selectivity of the OXSM substrate. This
study explores how the OXSM can elongate fatty acids larger than eight carbons, mirroring that of its bacterial type II progenitor.
■INTRODUCTION
The fatty acid synthase (FAS) is central to de novo lipid
biosynthesis in all organisms and operates through a highly
conserved reaction sequence.1 Unlike type I FAS, which is a
large multidomain protein, type II FAS houses each activity
within its own discrete protein. For both types, carbon chain
elongation occurs when tethered to an acyl carrier protein
(ACP), which shuttles its fatty acid cargo through iterative
enzymatic steps guided by discrete protein·protein interactions
between the ACP and its partner enzymes.2,3 Within this
process, ketosynthase (KS) serves as the central catalyst for
carbon chain elongation. Elongation at KS domains arises
through a two-step, ping-pong mechanism, as shown in Figure
1. This process begins with transacylation (left, Figure 1),
where a Cys residue in the KS accepts an acyl substrate from
its cognate ACP. This is then followed by a Claisen-like
condensation (right, Figure 1) with a malonyl-ACP to yield a
chain elongated product.4 The mechanistic details of this
pathway have been well-characterized in type II bacterial FAS
through structural and activity studies on the E. coli elongating
KSs FabF and FabB.5 Despite 2 billion years of eukaryotic
evolution, the mitochondrial type II FAS appears to be
remarkably similar to its bacterial ancestor (Figure 2), though
details remain sparse. The mitochondrial FAS is known to play
a primary role in the production of octanoate, the metabolic
precursor of lipoic acid, and longer chain fatty acid production
has been postulated.6−8 Human mitochondrial FAS has gained
considerable attention as a therapeutic target, wherein
dysregulation of lipid biosynthesis has been associated with
metabolic, neurodegenerative, and oncological disorders.9,10
The human mitochondrial KS OXSM (Figure 2) (Uniprot
Q9NWU1, Gene OXSM) is a nuclear-encoded protein
containing a 37-residue N-terminal mitochondrial targeting
peptide. Removal of the targeting peptide results in a 422-
residue protein, which forms an obligate 89 kDa dimer.11 The
only KS in the mitochondrial FAS, OXSM, shares significant
identity with bacterial type II KSs (Supporting Figure S1).12
Following our recent studies on E. coli FAS interactions,13 we
turned our attention to understand how the transient protein·
protein interactions in the complex of human mitochondrial
ACP (mACP, Uniprot O14561, Gene NDUFAB1) and OXSM
serve to regulate carbon chain length during the elongation
process. To date, three high-resolution structures of the OXSM

■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Our studies began by preparing recombinant OXSM and
mACP in E. coli. Like the case for the OXSM, mACP is
naturally a nuclear-encoded protein carrying a cleavable N-
terminal targeting peptide for translocation into the mitochon-
drial matrix. Here, both genes were synthesized for
heterologous expression without the mitochondrial targeting
peptides. While OXSM was achieved using standard
recombinant expression methods, successful production of
mACP could only be obtained through coexpression with the
mitochondrial Fe−S assembly complex protein, ISD11, known
to form a stable complex with mACP.14,15 holo-mACP was
obtained as previously described and subsequently treated with
recombinant ACP hydrolyase (AcpH) to return it to apo-
mACP and prepare it for cross-linker attachment (Supporting
Information).
Structural studies of ACP interactions are often impeded by
the transient nature of these protein·protein interactions.
Applying dual site-selective cross-linkers that enable active site
covalent cross-linking, a snapshot of the interaction is enabled.
Having previously developed cross-linking strategies for
bacterial KS enzymes,16−18 we applied the developed
pantetheinamide cross-linkers to selectively trap the mACP·
OXSM complex for both the transacylation and condensation
half-reactions.
The first cross-linker contained an α-bromoamide warhead,
which has been demonstrated to target the KS transacylation
half-reaction (left, Figure 1).16 We selected a C8 acyl chain,
anticipating that attachment of the C8-α-bromoamide
pantetheinamide cross-linker (C8αBr, Figure 3a) to apo-
mACP would engage nucleophilic attack by the active site
cysteine residue (Cys209) in the OXSM, mimicking the first
transacylation step. In a similar manner, we also chose a C8-β-
chloroacrylate pantetheinamide cross-linker (C8βCl, Figure
3b) to mimic the condensation stage of KS processivity (right,
Figure 1). We selected the Z-olefin isomer in the C8βCl cross-
linker (Figure 3b) to complement the α-bromoamide from the
first step. Prior studies have shown that the selectivity for
configurations among KSs increases with longer chain lengths
due to spatial constraints within the substrate binding
pocket.18
An established chemoenzymatic process was implemented
to mechanistically cross-link mACP to OXSM. It began by
loading the cross-linker onto the conserved serine residue
(Ser44) in apo-mACP. A one-pot, four-step enzymatic reaction
was used to attach the cross-linkers. This started with
enzymatic conversion of the corresponding cross-linker
(C8αBr or C8βCl) to its coenzyme A (CoA) analogue
(Supporting Figure S2) with recombinant CoA modifying
enzymes and then loading it onto mACP with a 4′-
phosphopantetheinyl transferase (PPTase) Sfp, to deliver a
C8αBr or C8βCl loaded crypto-mACP.19 Samples of these
crypto-mACPs were then incubated with OXSM in a 3:1 ratio
(Supporting Figure S3). After 12 h, we observed clear band-
shifts from 45 kDa (OXSM) to 59 kDa, corresponding to the
cross-linked complexes of crypto-mACP = OXSM. We then
screened the cross-linking reaction with probes containing
different chain lengths, from C2−C16. As shown in Supporting
Figure S4, effective cross-linking was observed from C4−C14,
with reduced efficiency observed for short (C2) or longer
(C16) cross-linkers. This observation not only provides an
expanded understanding of the substrate scope in the cross-

Next, we purified samples of cross-linked mACP = OXSM
for structural studies. Using size-exclusion chromatography, we
obtained C8αBr- and C8βCl-mACP = OXSM cross-linked
complexes at >95% purity. Crystallization attempts on
structures with intact N-terminal His6 tags were unsuccessful.
Tag removal was achieved by inserting a TEV protease
cleavage site proximal to the His6 tag and removing the tag
prior to cross-linking (Supporting Information). Size-exclusion
chromatography provided untagged C8αBr- and C8βCl-mACP
= OXSM cross-linked complexes at 99+% purity. We
crystallized the two complexes by hanging drop vapor diffusion
against 0.5 M (NH4)2SO4, 1 M Li2SO4, and 0.1 M sodium
citrate pH 5.6. X-ray crystallographic diffraction of the C8αBr-
mACP = OXSM (Figure 3a) and C8βCl-mACP = OXSM
(Figure 3b) cross-linked complexes provided structures with
resolutions at 2.50 Å (P1211 space group) and 2.31 Å (C121
space group), respectively (Supporting Tables S1 and S2),
after data refinement.
Although the asymmetric units in both structures were
similar (Figure 3), translational noncrystallographic symmetry
(tNCS) was observed in the C8βCl-mACP = OXSM structure.
As a result, we introduced translation/libration/screw (TLS)
and NCS restraints in our refinement.20 This resulted in
C8βCl-mACP = OXSM having two complexes (C8βCl-
mACP1 = OXSM1 and C8βCl-mACP2 = OXSM2), where
complex 1 contained OXSM1 (cross-linking monomer),
OXSM1′ (non-cross-linking monomer), and mACP1; and
complex 2 contained OXSM2 (cross-linking monomer),
OXSM2′ (non-cross-linking monomer), and mACP2.
Structural Validation of the Ping-Pong Mechanism
within OXSM. Both cross-linkers (Figure 4) were visualized
with sufficient electron density stretching from the post-
translational modification on mACP Ser44 through the
reactively captured active site Cys209 within the OXSM. In
most FASs, the active sites of elongating KSs consist of a
catalytic triad formed by Cys-His-His, where the Cys residue,
located at the N-terminus of an extended α-helix, serves as the
nucleophile, and the two His residues are involved in the
decarboxylative elongation process (Figure 1). An oxyanion
hole created by backbone amides of the catalytic cysteine and a
highly conserved phenylalanine provides stabilization of both
tetrahedral intermediates generated during each half-reac-
tion.21
In the C8αBr cross-linked structure (Figure 4a), the
carbonyl that mimics the acyl chain carbonyl (cyan, Figure
4b) points into the oxyanion hole, forming a hydrogen bond
with backbone amides of Phe447 and Cys209 (Figure 4a). In
contrast, substrate interactions in C8βCl-mACP = OXSM
(Figure 4c) comprise His348 and His385 setting the acyl
carbonyl position, while the oxyanion hole remains unoccu-
pied. During the transition between transacylation and
condensation, Phe447 serves as a gate, where it is in the
'propped-open’ position for substrate delivery during the
transacylation half-reaction (Figure 4a) and then switches to
the ‘gate-closed’ conformation during condensation (Figure
4c). At the latter stage, closure of the gate activates the
thioester by coordination of the transferred carbonyl with
OXSM active site histidine residues His348 and His385.
During this process, the 4′-phosphopantetheine (PPant)
moiety is anchored by polar contacts to Thr315 and Thr352.

face between mACP and OXSM. The interface between
mACP and the catalytically active, yet unoccupied, structural
monomer (OXSM′) was larger than that of the cross-linked
monomer (OXSM) in both structures. This suggests a
potential role for negative cooperativity across the KS dimer,
where one monomer acts to conduct the reaction and the
other plays a structural role. When cross-linked with C8βCl
(Figure 3b), the mACP·OXSM interface has buried surface
areas of 421.9 and 456.0 Å2 into OXSM and OXSM′,
respectively (Supporting Table S3). On cross-linking with
C8αBr (Figure 3a), the mACP·OXSM interface has buried
surface areas of 370.5 and 406.6 Å2, respectively. These
interfaces are significantly smaller than the analogous cross-
linked complexes from E. coli FAS, where a direct comparison

Next, we turned our attention to evaluating the side-chain
interactions at these interfaces. The OXSM and mACP
interface shared similarity with type II FAS.16,18 We found
that the OXSM KS domain has basic residues at the interface,
while residues in helices II, III, and loop 1 of mACP are
predominantly acidic (Supporting Figure S6). Although
similarities exist, we also observed several critical interactions
that distinguish this binding interface from those of other type
II FASs. Three regions were identified. In the first region, we
observed electrostatic interactions between Lys113 of OXSM
with Glu49 in helix II of mACP (Figure 5a). The second
region is on the other side of helix II. As depicted in Figure 5b,
unique interactions were identified between Asn173, Lys174,
and Arg252 of the OXSM and Glu55, Asp56, and Glu47 of the
mACP. We also observed interactions between Tyr172 and
Arg250 of the OXSM with Asp64 from helix III of the mACP
(Figure 5c). In the final region (Figure 5d), we observed a
hydrophobic patch sandwiched by the two polar interaction
regions that comprised Leu45, Val48, and Met52 of mACP
and Val175, Ser176, Pro177, Phe178, Ala251, and Ala253 of
OXSM.
Front Gating Loops of OXSM. E. coli type II FAS
elongating KSs regulate substrate entry, binding, and release
through coordinated movements of two loops in front of the
binding pocket. This gating process arises as the loops undergo
conformational changes with a conserved Phe447 residue
shifting significantly to allow the substrate to access the active
site Cys209. Here, the process transits between an open and a
closed gate (Supporting Figure S7). The open conformation
facilitates substrate delivery but disrupts the oxyanion hole,
while the closed conformation is catalytically active for
transacylation and subsequent Claisen-like condensation
(Figure 1).
In OXSM, loop 1 contains a highly conserved GFGG β-turn
motif seen in type II KSs (Supporting Figure S1), flanked by
two conserved asparagine residues Asn443 and Asn451.
Asn443 forms salt bridges with Asn346, Glu359, and Thr453
(Supporting Figure S8). Asn451 interacts with Gly449 at the
end of loop 1 and highly conserved Asp310 at the start of loop
2 (Figure 5e). This coordinated movement of loops 1 and 2,
along with a synchronized hydrogen-bond networking, opens
the gate for substrate delivery and release. In loop 2, His313
interacts with Asp43 of mACP, and Thr315 forms hydrogen
bonds with the hydroxyl group of the PPant arm and the
phosphoserine oxygen of the cross-linker (Figure 5f). This
differs from the equivalent Thr270 and Ser271 in FabF as they
also interact with the nearby carbonyl group of PPant
(Supporting Figure S9). Overall, these conserved residues
suggest that a gating function is a general characteristic of type
II FAS elongating KS enzymes, including OXSM.

Our current understanding of OXSM suggests that substrate
binding and length specificity is regulated by the rotation of
key residues. As mentioned previously, the acyl binding pocket
remains inaccessible prior to ligand binding due to obstruction
by the gating residue Phe447. A previous study proposed that
Phe447 regulates access to the active site by subtle rotational
movement.24 This study also suggested that Met154 under-
went rotational adjustment to accommodate longer chains
(Supporting Figure S10). However, our data shows that the
Phe447 side chain is shifted completely away from this pocket
to accommodate the acyl substrate. As shown in Figure 7a,
direct comparison with the apo structure (PDB: 2IWY)
provides compelling support for such noncanonical tunnel
occupancy. Additionally, Met154 in both cross-linked
structures C8βCl-mACP = OXSM and C8αBr-mACP =
OXSM does not achieve sufficient rotation to allow access to
the downstream extension unit (Figure 7a). To further
evaluate control by Met154, we prepared two mutants,
Met154Gly and Met154Phe. We rationalized that without
steric hindrance, Met154Gly should allow access to the
canonical substrate tunnel, while Met154Phe should block it
completely. Cross-linking experiments with mACPs containing
chain lengths that varied from C10 to C16 revealed no
significant differences between the wild type and these two
mutants (Figure 7b). These data suggest that Met154 does not
play a significant role in regulating access to the substrate
pocket. While our data clearly suggest that two tunnels exist
(Figure 6b), further studies are needed to understand the
structural requirements for their occupancy.

Gating Loop Mechanism Verified by Site-Directed
Mutagenesis. As a means of validation, we conducted further
mutation analyses to verify the front gating loop hypothesis.
We prepared Arg250Glu, Arg252Glu, and His313Ala (Sup-
porting Table S4) mutants to explore the polar interactions
between the OXSM and mACP. Mutants Thr315Ala,
Thr352Ala, His348Ala, His385Ala, and Phe445Ala were also
prepared to test hydrogen-bond stabilization and anchoring of
the ligand in the pocket. Mutants Lys113Glu, Tyr172Ala,
Asn173Ala, and Lys174Glu were designed to test the
significance of polar interactions between OXSM′ and
mACP. Asp310, a highly conserved loop 2 residue, forms a
hydrogen bond with the backbone amide of loop 1 and the
side chain of Asn451 to open the gate. Both Asp310 and
Asn451 were mutated to alanine to further explore their role in
gating. Gly446 and Gly449 were also mutated to alanine to test
the flexibility within loop 1. Finally, we targeted a glycine
residue located at the top of the OXSM pocket, Gly355, and
mutated it to methionine to block the binding tunnel and
abolish the OXSM activity (Supporting Figure S11).
Qualitative cross-linking reactions of OXSM wild type (wt)
and mutants (mts) with C8βCl-mACP were conducted to
explore the accessibility for a C8 substrate. OXSM was
incubated with an excess (3 equiv) of C8βCl-mACP to ensure
a complete reaction. The efficiency of the reaction was assayed
by the ratio of OXSM and cross-linked mACP = OXSM bands
by SDS-PAGE analyses (Supporting Figure S12). Wild-type
OXSM provided a 1:1 ratio of cross-linked mACP = OXSM
and uncross-linked OXSM after 5 h. His385Ala and Phe445Ala
provided comparable efficiency as the wild type. When run
under identical conditions, Tyr172Ala, Asn173Ala, Asn451Ala,
Gly446Ala, and Gly449Ala had weak cross-linking, while the
remaining mutants (Arg250Glu, Arg252Glu, His313Ala,
Thr315Ala, Thr352Ala, His348Ala, Lys113Glu, Lys174Glu,
Asp310Ala, and Gly355Met) showed no cross-linking. Each of
these mutants acted as expected and provide support for the
Phe447 gating model (Supporting Figure S10).
To further explore the effect of these mutations, we applied a
quantitative activity assay to test the entire OXSM reaction
cycle (transacylation to condensation, Figure 1).25 We
developed an activity assay to probe the elongation reaction
by incubation of the donor octyl (C8)-mACP with acceptor
malonyl-CoA to provide decanoyl (C10)-CoA and holo-
mACP (Supporting Figure S13). Quantified by HPLC, we
could precisely monitor the formation of holo-mACP
(Supporting Figure S14). We then optimized this process to
provide a tool for mutational screening. In this study, we found
that transacylation was very fast compared to the condensation
step. Here, product inhibition was excluded as a significant
factor as the observed slow phase remained consistent across
multiple dilutions. Although malonyl-CoA is not the native

Using this condensation assay, we obtained a turnover rate
of 0.174 min−1 for wild-type OXSM (Supporting Table S5).
The two charge swaps on the surface of the OXSM, Arg250Ala
and Arg252Ala, resulted in complete loss of activity. This
observation was similar to Arg206 (Arg252 counterpart) in E.
coli FabF. The interaction between Arg250 and mACP was
unique to this system. The turnover rate of His313Ala is 0.069
min−1, roughly 3-fold lower than that of the wild type, which
was comparable with the same mutation in FabF (His268Ala).
The turnover rates of the two sets of conserved threonine and
histidine residues, Thr315Ala, Thr352Ala, His348Ala, and
His385Ala, were 0.015, 0.011, 0.013, and 0.004 min−1,
respectively. This >90% loss in activity confirmed that residues
that stabilize the substrate in the tunnel greatly impact OXSM
function. Although not as dramatic as the FabF homologue
Phe400Ala, which is only 1−2% as active as wild-type FabF,
OXSM Phe445Ala retained 14.3% of the activity, indicating
that this residue is important, but not essential.
Further studies showed that charge swapping the two
conserved lysine residues on the surface of OXSM′, Lys113Glu
and Lys174Glu, returned 37.2 and 71.7% activity, respectively.
Tyr172 interacts with Asp65 on mACP helix III, an interaction
not observed in AcpP = FabF. Mutation of Tyr172 to alanine
showed 46.1% activity. Combined with the Arg250Ala
mutation, the interaction between the OXSM and helix III of
mACP is particularly important. The destabilization class of
mutants Asp310Ala and Asn451Ala, which coordinate hydro-
gen bonds to open the gate, retained 42.1% (0.073 min−1) and
40.8% (0.071 min−1) activity, respectively. This result strongly
supports the proposed gating mechanism triggered by PPIs.
Mutants Gly446Ala and Gly449Ala, which constrain the
conformational flexibility of the loop 1 GFGG motif, exhibit
relative catalytic rates of 27.0% (0.047 min−1) and 15.7%
(0.027 min−1) compared to the wild type, supporting loop 1
flexibility as an important aspect of the gating mechanism.
Finally, Gly355Met possesses no activity as this mutant
completely blocks substrate access to the active site.
■CONCLUSIONS
By leveraging dual site-selective covalent cross-linkers, we
report high-resolution complexes that provide the first detailed
insights into the protein·protein interactions between the
OXSM and mACP of the human mitochondrial FAS. OXSM
was captured in both transacylation and elongation states while
interacting with substrate-bound mACP, revealing distinct
protein·substrate interaction patterns. Both subunits of the
OXSM, whether cross-linked or not, displayed extensive
interactions with mACP, emphasizing the functional impor-
tance of its dimeric configuration. Importantly, the acyl tail of
the mACP-bound substrate was accommodated not within the
canonical substrate binding site of the OXSM, but within a
perpendicularly positioned tunnel. Mutation of Met154, a
gating residue previously hypothesized to regulate access to the
canonical tunnel, did not impair the ability of the OXSM to
interact with mACP, suggesting flexibility in the selection of
the acyl binding site. Through a combination of structural
analyses and site-directed mutagenesis, our findings refine the
previously hypothesized regulatory model with a comprehen-
sive mechanism involving dynamic structural elements. We
surmise that evolutionary isolation within an organelle allowed
the mitochondrial FAS to retain ancestral features over billions
of years, and it is not surprising that at one point in
evolutionary time, a developing eukaryotic cell found
competitive advantage in access to more than one lipid
biosynthetic modality. Since the cytosolic (type I) FAS in
eukaryotes is designed to produce only a single product, the
type II system could offer a more diverse metabolic menu,
including (but not limited to) octanoic acid for lipoic acid
biosynthesis.
This study demonstrates the structural underpinnings for
human OXSM to accept and facilitate the biosynthesis of
longer chain fatty acids beyond C8 that are important for
mitochondrial regulation. Data collected here enhance our
understanding of fatty acid biosynthesis within human
mitochondria and elucidate how these enzymes interact,
offering pathways for the discovery of new approaches for
the treatment of mitochondrial diseases.
