ABSTRACT: 1,2,4-Triazine ring is a scaffold widely found in biologically active compounds, but how nature makes it remains
enigmatic. In this study, we unveil the complex enzymatic and nonenzymatic cascade reactions that assemble the 1,2,4-triazine
moiety found in the structures of the natural products pseudoiodinine and toxoflavin. Through biochemical studies, isotope labeling,
and the application of substrate analogues, we propose a plausible pathway for the 1,2,4-triazine assembly from a common precursor
in riboflavin biosynthesis. This process involves four two-electron oxidation steps, C−N bond formation, decarboxylation, and the
N−N bond forming step catalyzed by a metal-dependent WD40-repeat (WDR) protein. This study thus not only provides the first
biocatalytic route for the 1,2,4-triazine assembly but also identifies a previously unrecognized catalytic role of a large WDR protein
family.
■INTRODUCTION
1,2,4-Triazine is a six-membered-ring structure with three
nitrogen atoms, two of which are adjacent with an N−N bond
in the heterocycle. This heteroatom-rich and tautomerizable
moiety is highly efficient in forming hydrogen bonds and
interacting with bioactive molecules, making it a key scaffold in
medicinal chemistry with many biological applications.1,2
Examples of 1,2,4-triazine-containing compounds include
synthetic drugs such as the antiviral azaribine,3 the antiepileptic
lamotrigine,4 and the anticancer tirapazamine,5 as well as
natural products such as the antimicrobial pseudoiodinine,6,7
the phytotoxin toxoflavin,8,9 and the anticancer CDMHK
(Figure 1).10 While synthetic methods for the preparation of
1,2,4-triazine derivatives are available, how nature makes this
well-known functional group, particularly the key N−N bond-
forming enzyme, remains enigmatic.
The recent biosynthetic study of pseudoiodinine and
toxoflavin has shed some light on the biogenesis of 1,2,4-
triazine.7,11−15 Bioinformatic analysis and heterologous ex-
pression of the biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) of
pseudoiodinine and toxoflavin led to a proposed biosynthetic
pathway (Figure 2), which starts with generating 1 from GTP
via homologues of RibA and RibD (BthII1285/1281 or ToxB/
E, respectively), like the steps in riboflavin biosynthesis.16 1 or
its dephosphorylated product 2 would then be converted to 3
containing 1,2,4-triazine promoted by BthII1284/ToxC, a two

■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
To probe the activity of WDR proteins in 1,2,4-triazine
synthesis, we overexpressed and purified BthII1284 from
Escherichia coli for in vitro biochemical assays (Figure S2). The
putative substrate, 1 or 2, was initially prepared in situ using a
coupled assay containing purified E. coli RibA and RibD, GTP,
ATP, Mg2+, and NADPH, with or without a promiscuous
phosphatase, calf intestinal alkaline phosphatase (CIP)
(Figures S3B, S4, and S5). Previous isotopic tracer experiments
showed that the C−N bond of Gly was incorporated into
toxoflavin,12,33 suggesting that Gly or its derivative was the
substrate to react with 1 or 2. Considering that several WDR
proteins could interact with tRNA and take part in the tRNA
modification,34,35 we further prepared glycyl-tRNA in addition
to Gly in the coupled reactions. Gratifyingly, we observed a
reaction product with a molecular weight identical to that of 3
based on HRMS analysis, and the utilization of 2-13C,15N-Gly
led to the expected mass spectral shift of +2 Da (Figures S3C
and S6). The presence of tRNA had no effect (Figure S3B),
suggesting that Gly is the direct substrate of BthII1284. The
addition of phosphatase boosted the production of 3, and no
phosphorylated 3 was detected in the absence of phosphatase
(Figure S3D), suggesting that the phosphate group in 1 is not
required for the substrate recognition by BthII1284 and that 2
is the preferred substrate of BthII1284.
To directly investigate the substrate and cofactor require-
ment of BthII1284, we obtained the commercial standard of
compound 2. Previous literature suggested that 2 was highly
unstable and underwent oxidative decomposition upon
exposure to air, producing a multitude of products.36 However,
when converted to its HCl salt, 2•HCl, its stability would be
significantly improved.37 We observed that 2 was most stable
around pH 2; in the assay buffer of pH 7.6, 2 was quickly
degraded with an estimated half-life of 10 min (Figure S7). We
thus conducted most of the reactions with a relatively high
concentration of BthII1284 and a short reaction time. The
incubation of 2, Gly, and BthII1284 successfully produced the
expected product with its UV−vis profile and molecular weight
consistent with that of 3 (Figures 3 and S8). The identity of
the product 3 was further confirmed by the known enzymatic
conversion of 3 to 4 catalyzed by the purified BthII1282 (a
ToxD homologue), and the identity of 4 was confirmed by
comparison with the commercial standard (Figures 3 and S8).
Additionally, 3 was observed to undergo a spontaneous, slow
conversion to 4, consistent with previous studies.12 Interest-
ingly, the addition of EDTA abolished 3 production, indicating
that the reaction is metal-dependent. We then tested the effects
of various divalent metal ions on BthII1284 activity. The
addition of Fe2+ showed no obvious effect. The addition of
Zn2+ or Mn2+ abolished 3 production, possibly due to enzyme
denaturation, as protein precipitation was observed. In
contrast, the addition of Mg2+ or Ca2+ significantly enhanced
the production of 3, with Ca2+ performing slightly better than
Mg2+ (Figure 3). Inductively coupled plasma optical emission
spectroscopy (ICP−OES) revealed that Ca2+, at a concen-
tration of ∼20-fold higher than Mg2+, was copurified with
BthII1284 from E. coli (Figure S9). These residual Ca2+ ions
supported BthII1284 activity without metal supplementation
in vitro, albeit with a much lower catalytic efficiency. These
results unequivocally confirmed the activity of BthII1284 in
generating the 1,2,4-triazine ring of 3 from 2 and Gly in a
metal-dependent manner.
The formation of 1,2,4-triazine is expected to involve
multiple oxidation steps, and molecular oxygen is likely used as
the oxidant. In the absence of enzyme, 2 underwent
spontaneous oxidation and degradation, resulting in the
generation of hydrogen peroxide (Figure S10A). During the
early stage of the enzymatic reaction, we observed that the
concentration of H2O2 was significantly higher than those in
negative controls without enzyme or Gly (Figure S10A),
suggesting that H2O2 was also generated in the 3-producing
reaction. Surprisingly, concentrations of H2O2 decreased over
time in the presence of enzymes as observed in both the 3-
producing reaction mixture and the negative control without
Gly, suggesting that BthII1284 may promote the consumption

to decompose H2O2 into O2, which may prevent the

probe the effect of H2O2 in 3 formation, we added H2O2 or
catalase that decomposes H2O2 to the 3-producing reaction
mixture. The addition of H2O2 slightly inhibited the
production of 3, whereas the addition of catalase slightly
promoted the production of 3 (Figure S12). All of these results
suggested that H2O2 generated during the conversion of 2 into
3 could be decomposed to O2 by BthII1284. Furthermore, the
addition of reductants such as DTT also increased the
production of 3 (Figure S12), likely by slowing substrate
degradation and reducing coproduct H2O2 inhibition.
A complex reaction cascade was proposed in converting 2 to
3, including C−N bond and N−N bond formation, possible
decarboxylation, and multiple oxidations. To shed light on the
sequence of this reaction cascade, we attempted to capture
potential reaction intermediates by time-course assays using
both unlabeled and labeled Gly. At various time points,
enzymatic reaction samples were immediately analyzed by LC-
HRMS to avoid possible spontaneous oxidation after protein
removal via ultrafiltration. Interestingly, 3 was not observed
within the 30 min of reaction; instead, a +4 Da new product 5
compared to 3 was produced gradually over time (Figures S13
and S14). The mass of this product increased by 2 Da when
2,2-D2-Gly was used, and we thus proposed it to be
“tetrahydro” triazine based on the HRMS analysis (Figure
4A). When the ultrafiltered samples containing 5 were left at
room temperature, 5 disappeared, accompanied by the
transient accumulation of 6 (+2 Da compared to 3) and the
corresponding gradual accumulation of 3 (Figure S15). The
mass of 6 also increased by 2 Da when 2,2-D2-Gly was used,
and we thus proposed it to be “dihydro” triazine (Figure 4A).
These observations suggested that during the conversion of 2
to 3, the final four-electron oxidation occurred spontaneously
in two sequential two-electron oxidation steps. Furthermore,
although only one deuterium at the Cα position of Gly was
retained in 3 as expected (Figure 4A), both deuterium atoms
were retained in 5 and 6, suggesting that the C−N bond
formation in the 1,2,4-triazine assembly did not occur before
decarboxylation of Gly.
No earlier intermediates were captured using Gly even at the
1 min interval; we thus attempted to slow down the reaction
using possibly less preferred substrates than Gly. When L-Ala
or D-Ala was used to replace Gly, a product 7 with an expected
14 Da increase in mass compared to 3 was observed after 1 h
of reaction (Figure 4B), suggesting that BthII1284 was not
stringent toward Gly and a methyl substitution was tolerated.
D-Ala supported a slightly higher yield of 7 than L-Ala, but both
were recognized more poorly than Gly (Figures S16 and S17),
raising the possibility of additional intermediate identification.
A careful search revealed compound 8, which showed a 44 Da
increase compared to the methylated 5 (Figures 4B and S18),
suggesting that the carboxylate group was retained in this Ala
adduct. Considering that the C−N bond would not form
before decarboxylation, we tentatively assigned the structure of
8 with a N−N bond. After ultrafiltration to remove BthII1284
in a mixture containing 7 and 8, a gradual decrease in 8 and a
corresponding increase in 7 were observed (Figure 4B), and
the addition of BthII1284 did not promote this process (Figure
S19), confirming the intermediacy of compound 8 and
suggesting that enzymes were not needed for the subsequent
decarboxylation and C−N bond formation.

the 1,2,4-triazine assembly from 2 and Gly (Figure 5). The

oxide, which subsequently combine to form a hydroperoxide
species. Following the elimination of hydrogen peroxide, the
first two-electron oxidation is completed, resulting in the
formation of iminouracil. This intermediate then undergoes
tautomerization, forming an imine at the C6 position. The
amino group of Gly subsequently performs a nucleophilic
attack to form a N−N bond, likely promoted by BthII1284. At
this stage, the structure returns to the oxidizable aminouracil
form, forming 9. The second two-electron oxidation then
occurs, as made evident by the production of 8 using Ala. The
remaining steps can happen spontaneously, starting with
decarboxylation, initiating the formation of a C−N bond to
produce 5, driven by the thermodynamic stability of the
aromatic ring of 5. This is followed by the third two-electron
oxidation, producing 6. The conjugated system of 6 renders
the hydrogen at the C9 position acidic and prone to
deprotonation, facilitating tautomerization back to the amino-
uracil structure. Finally, the fourth two-electron oxidation
completes the biosynthesis of 1,2,4-triazine, yielding the final
product 3. The methyl substitution at the Cα position of Gly
may impact spontaneous decarboxylation and C−N bond
formation, leading to the detection of 8 when Gly was replaced
by Ala.
During the conversion of 2 to 3, we proposed that
BthII1284 catalyzed the N−N bond formation to form a Gly
adduct, while the remaining steps most likely occurred
spontaneously, including multiple oxidations, consistent with
the lack of known oxidoreductase activity of WDR proteins.
Indeed, the WD40 domain is one of the most abundant
protein domains involved in protein−protein or protein-
nucleic acid interactions.39,40 To date, no WDR protein has
been identified with enzymatic activity. Furthermore, the
function of canonical WDR proteins is independent of the
metal ions. In the AlphaFold3-predicted BthII1284-Ca2+
structural model (Figure S20A),41 residues H109 and D130
coordinate with the calcium ion, which is located between the
two WD40 domains, both exhibiting a seven-bladed β-
propeller structure (Figure S20B). Additionally, the side
chain of R107 forms an ionic bond with the side chain of
D130, potentially stabilizing the conformation of D130. To
validate this model and probe the function of these residues,
variants (H109A, D130A, and R107A) were individually used
to replace the wild-type enzyme in the 3-producing reaction
mixture. Both D130A and R107A completely abolished the
production of 3, while H109A exhibited a slight reduction in
yield (Figure S20D). We further conducted a molecular
docking study to investigate the possible binding mode of 9
with BthII1284 (Figure S20C). The results revealed that the
terminal hydroxyl group on the ribityl side chain of 9
coordinates with the calcium ion, while the side chains of
D148 and R107 form hydrogen bonds with the hydroxyl and
carboxyl group of 9. When D148 was mutated to A, the
resulting variant exhibited a significant reduction in catalytic
activity (Figure S20D). These results suggested that calcium
ions might play a role in substrate binding. In addition, in the
binding pocket, the carbon originating from Gly occupies a
cavity, with its size oriented from the D-amino acid side chain
being larger than that of the L-amino acid. This likely explains
the enzyme’s preference for D-Ala over L-Ala. When Gly was
replaced by additional amino acids including L-Val, L-Leu, L-
Ser, L-Cys, D-Thr, L/D-Met, L-Asp, L-Asn, L-Glu, and L-Gln,
only L-Ser and L-Cys were recognized by BthII1284 (Figures
S15, S21, and S22), possibly due to steric hindrance in the
binding pocket. When L-Ser was used as the substrate, the final
product containing the 1,2,4-triazine moiety was detected.
However, when L-Cys was used as the substrate, the reaction
seemed to stop before decarboxylation, likely due to the
interference of the thiol group on spontaneous decarboxylation
and cyclization via the C−N bond formation. It is notable that
we could not readily identify active site residues to facilitate the
proposed nucleophilic attack of Gly to form an N−N bond
based on the docking study, demonstrating the limitation of
this model. A further mechanistic study of BthII1284 is
currently ongoing.
Considering that BthII1284 is the first identified WDR
protein displaying an enzymatic activity, we built a sequence
similarity network (SSN) and genome neighborhood network
(GNN) to explore how extensive the BthII1284 homologues
exist in natural product biosynthesis.42,43 BthII1284 homo-
logues are widely distributed in bacterial species. Notably,
among the 678 homologues, 116 are located in clusters that
also contain BthII1285/1281/1282 homologues (Figures S23
and S24A), suggesting the potential existence of yet
undiscovered 1,2,4-triazine-containing natural products. Addi-
tionally, we identified BthII1284 homologues associated with
gene clusters that are distinctly different from pseudoiodinine
and toxoflavin BGCs, of which three examples are shown
(Figures S23 and S24B). These WDR proteins are located
adjacent to glycosyltransferase, polyketide synthase/nonribo-
somal peptide synthetase (PKS/NRPS), or sulfotransferase,
respectively. They may serve regulatory roles or exhibit
catalytic functions which are yet to be investigated.
In summary, we revealed the first mechanism for the natural
assembly of the 1,2,4-triazine moiety, a scaffold widely found in
various biologically active compounds with pharmaceutical
applications. Complex enzymatic and nonenzymatic cascade
reactions were mapped, including a total of four two-electron
oxidation steps, N−N bond formation, C−N bond formation,
and decarboxylation. A novel metal-dependent WDR protein
was confirmed to be essential for the 1,2,4-triazine assembly,
most likely promoting the key step of the N−N bond
formation. This study thus identified for the first time a
catalytic role of widely spread WDR proteins, expanded the
limited enzymatic inventory for the N−N bond formation,
provided the first biocatalytic route for the 1,2,4-triazine
assembly, and facilitated the additional discovery of 1,2,4-
triazine-containing natural products for drug discovery.
