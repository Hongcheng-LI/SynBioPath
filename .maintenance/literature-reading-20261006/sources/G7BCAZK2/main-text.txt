ABSTRACT: Aromatic polyketides are a vast category of natural products known for their wide-ranging biological activities, with
their structural variety stemming from modifications to their core frameworks. This study reveals two distinct processes that shape
the frameworks of (+)/(−)-anthrabenzoxocinone (ABX) and fasamycin (FAS) from a common biosynthetic precursor, compound
1. FasS protects the carboxyl group of this molecule without altering it, preparing it for FasU, which then crafts FAS’s unique,
nonplanar axial chiral aromatic framework. In contrast, Abx(+)O and Abx(−)O remove the carboxyl group from compound 1,
producing phenyldimethylanthrone (PDA), a key intermediate for (+)/(−)-ABX formation. Structural analysis of AccS (1.65 Å,
FasS homologue) and Abx(+)O (1.99 Å), combined with mutagenesis studies, identifies key residues of AccS and Abx(+)O, providing
insights into the carboxyl-protecting mechanism and decarboxylation mechanism. Evolutionary and functional studies further
connect AccS and AbxO to the N- and C-termini of the long-studied, functionally enigmatic protein family, WhiE-ORFI (resolved in
2.3 Å). This study unveils hidden strategies for terminal carboxyl group editing, providing new insights into the origins of aromatic
polyketide diversity.
■INTRODUCTION
Aromatic polyketides are a large class of natural products with
important biological activities.1−3 They are primary produced
through the decarboxylative Claisen condensation of malonyl-
CoA, catalyzed by type II polyketide synthases (PKSs), which
typically yield carbon chains with even numbers of carbons
ranging from 16 to 30.4,5 Following this, aromatase/cyclase
(ARO/CYC) folds these chains into polycyclic aromatic
frameworks, which then undergo further modifications to
generate a wide variety of polyketide forms.1,4−7 A key factor in
this diversity is the removal of a carboxyl group from the
chain’s end, a step that can occur either before or after the
frameworks are formed, resulting in compounds with an odd
number of carbons.6−15 This process, alongside chain
elongation, plays a central role in determining both the length
and the structural complexity of these molecules.
Although chain elongation is well-understood, the removal
of its linear terminal carboxyl group remains less clearly
defined, often assumed to occur spontaneously rather than
through enzymatic action.8,16−20 (+)/(−)-Anthrabenzoxoci-
nones (ABXs)21−28 and fasamycins (FASs)29−31 are a class of
type II polyketides that strongly inhibit FabF, an essential
enzyme in bacterial fatty acid biosynthesis.32 These com-
pounds possess nonplanar, chiral structures with 25 carbons,
originated from a shared 26-carbons precursor, compound 1
(Figure 1).6,7 This precursor can follow one of two paths: an
enzymatic decarboxylative cyclization step, catalyzed by the
unique ARO/CYC FasU, creates the axially chiral biaryl
structure (E-ring) of FAS,7 or the precursor loses its terminal
carboxyl group to form phenyldimethylanthrone (PDA),6
which then transforms into the oxygen-linked tricyclic
structure (E-ring) of (−)-ABX through a dual-oxa Diels−
Alder reaction.33 The precursor’s instability complicates this

fasamycin F (FAS-F).7 For FAS production, stabilizing the
precursor is essential to avoid unintended carboxyl loss, while
for ABX synthesis, rapid carboxyl removal is necessary to
prevent FAS-F formation. This delicate balance suggests a
sophisticated control mechanism that influences these path-
ways.
To investigate this, we combined both in vivo and in vitro
methods to trace these biosynthetic routes. Our results reveal
two distinct pathways: one preserves the precursor for FAS
synthesis, while the other produces PDA for ABX formation.
We identified two novel proteinsa carboxyl-protecting
protein (FasS) and a decarboxylase (AbxO), both of which
are closely related to the N- and C-terminal domains of a
previously uncharacterized protein family, WhiE-ORFI.34 FasS
and AbxO play crucial roles by either retaining or removing the
terminal carboxyl group, thus steering the precursor toward
FAS or ABX production. This work provides mechanistic
insights into terminal carboxyl editing in type II polyketide
biosynthesis and reveals how these terminal carboxyl
modifications shape the diversity of aromatic polyketides,
highlighting their critical role in directing biosynthetic
outcomes.
■RESULTS
Role of FasS in Protecting the Carboxyl Group. Our
recent work established that ARO/CYC FasU converts the key
precursor, compound 1, into FAS.7 However, another protein,
FasS, belonging to the antibiotic biosynthesis monooxygenase
(ABM) superfamily from the FAS-producer Streptomyces
kanamyceticus CGMCC 4.1441, also appears to determine
the fate of this precursor. Previous studies showed that
removing a homologue gene forS in a related FAS-producing
system reduced FAS output and increased a byproduct,
formicapyridine A, derived from PDA.16 This suggested that
FasS might regulate modifications to the precursor’s terminal
carboxyl group.
To test this, we overexpressed FasS in Escherichia coli and
added it to a reaction system designed to generate compound
1. This reaction system was based on a previously reported
one-pot enzymatic total synthesis of 1, which included the
minimal PKS (FasA, FasB, and FasC), malonyl-CoA ligase
(MatB), acyltransferase (FabD), CYC/AROs (Abx(+)C,
Abx(+)D), methyltransferase (Abx(+)M), and S-adenosylme-
thionine (SAM).7 Analysis showed that FasS can effectively
stabilize compound 1, preventing its spontaneous decarbox-
ylation to form PDA (Figure 2, trace III). When we added a
small quantity of FasU alone, some PDA formed alongside
FAS due to limited FasU activity (Figure 2, trace IV).
However, including FasS with FasU resulted in only FAS
production (Figure 2, trace V), confirming that FasS shields
the carboxyl group of 1, preventing its loss. This protective role
is a novel finding, marking FasS as the first known protein of its
kind to exhibit such a function.
To further validate these findings, isothermal titration
calorimetry (ITC) experiments were performed. The binding
between FasS and compound 1 was measurable, with a Kd
value of 0.49 μM (Figure S1), highlighting the key role of FasS
in protecting the terminal carboxyl group of compound 1.
Furthermore, ITC experiments on other small molecules
confirmed that FasS has a weak binding affinity for PDA (16.9
μM), FAS (36.9 μM), and formicapyridine A (36.3 μM) and
almost no binding affinity for other keto acids (phenylglyoxylic
acid and phenoxyacetic acid) (Figure S1). These results
indicate that FasS is a unique carboxyl-protecting protein

Characterization of the Decarboxylation Function of
AbxO. The losing terminal carboxyl group of the β-keto acid
chain in aromatic polyketide synthesis has typically been
viewed as a spontaneous process, a view supported by our in
vitro enzymatic assays.6,7 Yet, during the natural production of
ABXs, we noticed the absence of an unwanted byproduct,24
FAS-F, which could be generated through the spontaneous
cyclization of 1.7 This suggested that an enzyme in the ABX
biosynthetic pathway actively removes the carboxyl group,
blocking FAS-F formation. By comparing the biosynthetic gene
clusters (BGCs) for (+)-ABX from Actinomycete sp. MA7150
and (−)-ABX from Streptomyces sp. MA6657,24 we identified
two similar genes, abx(+)O and abx(−)O (57.99% identity),
unique to ABX systems and absent in FAS systems (Figure
S2), indicating their exclusive association with the biosynthesis
of ABXs.
To investigate further, we produced these proteins in E. coli
and added them to the reaction that generates compound 1.
Both proteins exclusively produce PDA while eliminating 1
entirely (Figure 3A, traces I−III). When paired with FasU, the
AbxO proteins favored PDA over FAS, showing their dominant
role in this reaction (Figure 3A, trace IV). Adding FasS
restored some FAS production (Figure 3A, trace V), consistent
with its protective effect. These results indicate that AbxO
drives carboxyl removal, steering the pathway toward ABXs.
To further validate these findings, we deleted abx(−)O from
the cosmid 4E1024 responsible for (−)-ABX production and
introduced the engineered cosmid (pJTU109) into Strepto-
myces coelicolor M1154.35 The recombinant strain still
produced (−)-ABX but at a lower level, and FAS-F appeared
in the extractsconsistent with our in vitro results (Figure 3B,
trace II).7 This suggests that without AbxO, compound 1
partially undergo spontaneous cyclization to form FAS-F
instead of completely converting to PDA. Together, these
experiments establish that AbxO actively removes the carboxyl
group from 1, directing the precursor toward PDA, which
ultimately leads to ABX formation. This discovery marks AbxO
as the first known bacterial enzyme that specifically shortens
the β-keto acid moiety of aromatic polyketide via decarbox-
ylation, opening possibilities for crafting new molecular
frameworks through controlled carboxyl modifications. Addi-
tionally, our findings clarify how FasS and AbxO orchestrate
the divergence of FAS and ABX biosynthesis, highlighting their
opposing roles in managing the precursor’s carboxyl group.
Evolutionary Relationship between FasS, AbxO, and
WhiE-ORFI. Protein sequence comparisons revealed that
AbxO is similar to a group of proteins known as the SchA/
CurD-like family,36 while FasS aligns with antibiotic biosyn-
thesis monooxygenase (ABM) superfamily. Interestingly, both
enzymes share similarities with WhiE-ORFI, an uncharac-
terized protein found in BGCs that produce polyketide spore
pigments in various Streptomyces species.34 WhiE-ORFI
contains two distinct domains: its N-terminal ABM domain
(WhiE-ORFI-Nt), which shares 47% identity with FasS, and its
C-terminal SchA/CurD domain (WhiE-ORFI-Ct), which
shares 33% identity with AbxO (Figure S3).
Type II PKS systems are believed to evolve by incorporating
new modifying enzymes to refine their capabilities.5,37
Phylogenetic analysis shows that FasS-like and AbxO-like
proteins likely originated independently before merging into a
protein like WhiE-ORFI over time (Figure S4). This
evolutionary pattern is supported by another type II PKS
system for bipentaromycin,38 which associates more closely
with the ancestral clade than the other three type II PKS
systems.5 This system includes two proteins: Bpa19,
resembling FasS and WhiE-ORFI-Nt (40.62% and 45.63%
identity, respectively), and Bpa29, resembling AbxO and
WhiE-ORFI-Ct (23.73% and 33% identity, respectively).
These results suggest that FasS-like and AbxO-like proteins
operated independently in early evolution and later converged
into WhiE-ORFI, combining their traits.
To further explore this, we produced the two-halves of
WhiE-ORFI from S. coelicolor in E. coli and tested their
functions. The WhiE-ORFI-Nt domain, like FasS, protected
the carboxyl group of 1, while the WhiE-ORFI-Ct domain, like
AbxO, showed the ability to remove it, albeit with very weak

Structural Insights from AccS, Abx(+)O, and WhiE-
ORFI. To understand how these enzymes work, we determined
their structures using X-ray crystallography. We resolved
Abx(+)O at a resolution of 1.99 Å (Figure 4B, Table S1).
Due to unsuccessful crystallization of FasS, we instead solved
the structure of AccS,39 a FasS homologue (65.26% identity)
with identical carboxyl protection activity (Figure S5), at 1.65
Å resolution. AccS adopts a compact homodimeric structure,
with each subunit comprising three α-helices (α1, α2, α3) and
four β-strands (β1, β2, β3, β4) (Figure S7). The active site is
located within a cavity in each subunit and accessible through a
broad entrance formed by β1-4 and α1-3 (Figure 4A). In
contrast, Abx(+)O consists of two domains, which together
form a dimer-like structure, though the enzyme functions as a
monomer. The N-terminal domain, formed by β1-3 and α1-4,
adopts a closed conformation, while the C-terminal domain,
composed of β4-7 and α5-8, adopts an open conformation,
creating a hydrophobic pocket for substrate binding.
Due to the instability of 1, we attempted to obtain the
complex structures of AccS and Abx(+)O with PDA as the
substrate, but crystallization was unsuccessful. As an
alternative, we performed docking simulations of 1 into the
structures of both proteins (Figures 4C,D and S8). We
performed molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of the
complex formed between 1 and AccS to optimize the resulting
complex structures (Figure 4C). The simulations showed
minimal conformational changes with an RMSD of 0.963 Å,
indicating that AccS does not require significant structural
rearrangement to bind its substrate. Interestingly, during the
MD simulations, compound 1, initially positioned at the edge
of the AccS active pocket, gradually migrated toward the
interior of the pocket and tightly bound it by the end of the
simulation (Figure S9). The hydrogen bond time series further
revealed the dynamic changes in the hydrogen-bonding
network during the binding of 1 to AccS (Figure S10). Within
the AccS binding cavity, two key residuesS61 and W63
were identified (Figure 4C). These residues likely stabilize the
terminal carboxyl group of the polyketide chain by forming

Abx(+)O, on the other hand, features a distinct binding cavity
with two key residues (Y118, R162) anchoring the terminal
carboxyl group (Figure 4D). The C1 carbonyl group of 1
forms a hydrogen bond with R162, while Y118 interacts with
the carboxylate group to properly position the substrate. The
residue R162 in the active site of Abx(+)O may donate a proton
to the carboxylate, facilitating decarboxylation, in contrast with
AccS, which primarily protects the carboxyl group. These
differences in substrate binding explain the distinct functions of
the two proteins.
We also determined the X-ray crystal structure of WhiE-
ORFI at a resolution of 2.3 Å (Figure 4E). The N- and C-
terminal domains of WhiE-ORFI show structural similarities
with both AccS and Abx(+)O, with RMSD values of 1.505 and
3.14 Å, respectively (Figure 4F). The key residues S61 and
W63 in AccS and Y118 and R162 in Abx(+)O are conserved in
WhiE-ORFI, aligning with its dual abilities observed in tests
(Figures S3, S5, and S6). These structural insights clarify how
FasS (via AccS) protects carboxyl groups, AbxO removes
them, and WhiE-ORFI blends these traits, offering a molecular
basis for their roles in shaping polyketide diversity.

understand how AccS protects carboxyl groups, we mutated
the key residuesS61 and W63and tested their roles
(Figures 4C and S5). Sequence alignment revealed that the
putative functional residues S61 and W63 are highly conserved
among AccS homologues (Figure S3). We mutated W63 to
leucine, and mutated S61 to glycine, threonine, aspartic acid,
and isoleucine produced soluble mutants. All of these
mutations significantly or nearly abolished the AccS function,
confirming that S61 and W63 are essential for carboxyl
protection activity (Figures 4G and S5). Similary, mutating

In addition to the active site residues forming hydrogen
bonds with the terminal carboxyl group, AccS may utilize
additional residue-mediated interactions to position 1. To
investigate this, we extended our mutational studies to residues
proximal to 1 within the active pocket (Figure 4C).
Hydrophobic residues I32, L81, and I85 are positioned near
the D-ring of 1. Notably, MD simulations indicated potential
hydrogen bond formation between residues L81 and I85 and
the C23 hydroxyl group. The L81A mutant retained 61.66% of
its protective activity, whereas the I32A and I85A mutants
exhibited markedly reduced activities of 23.59% and 8.32%,
respectively (Figures 4G and S5). This indicates that these
three residues may contribute to the stabilization of 1.
Residues F13 is located near the A−B−C rings of 1. The
F13L mutant retained the vast majority of protection activity,
implying that F13 does not play a π−π interaction role in
substrate binding. These results collectively reveal the substrate
binding mode by which AccS protects 1.
Due to the compact substrate-binding mode between AccS
and 1, an external driver is required for dissociation of 1. The
ITC experiment showed a weak protein−protein interaction
between FasS and FasU, with a Kd value of 15.1 μM (Figure
S1), suggesting that FasU may facilitate the dissociation of 1
from FasS (AccS) (Figure S1). To investigate the dissociation
process of 1 and the AccS−FasU interaction mechanism, we
performed MD simulations of the AccS−compound 1−FasU
ternary complex. Interestingly, in contrast with the AccS−
compound 1 MD simulation results, 1 migrated toward the
FasU binding pocket in the presence of FasU (Figures S9 and
S12). Therefore, the FasU−AccS interaction likely plays a
critical role in the dissociation of 1.
For Abx(+)O, which removes carboxyl groups, we focused on
Y118 and R162. Mutating either to alanine abolished the
activity (Figures 4D and S6), confirming essential roles.
However, replacing Y118 with threonine preserved some
function, albeit at a much lower rate, 50 times less efficient,
indicating that Y118 is primarily involved in substrate binding.
In contrast, mutations at R162 (R162Q and R162E)
completely inactivated the enzyme, while R162K retained its
activity. This suggests residue 162 must maintain a positive
charge, likely to donate a proton that triggers carboxyl removal,
a role seen in other enzymes, such as orotidine 5′-
monophosphate (OMP) decarboxylase,41 which catalyzes a
similar reaction. Screening of residues surrounding 1 in the
Abx(+)O active pocket revealed that W164 is adjacent to its A−
B−C ring, while E173 is in close proximity to the C11-
hydroxyl group of 1. Mutants W164A and E173A exhibited
significant reductions in catalytic activity (23.39% and 41.57%,
respectively), supporting their involvement in substrate
binding. Mutational analysis of six hydrophobic residues
(L132, L148, I177, V181, L186, and L190) showed no
substantial decrease in decarboxylation activity, indicating that
AbxO does not utilize hydrophobic interactions to position 1
(Figure S6). Based on these mutagenesis results, we propose
an acid−base mechanism for the decarboxylation catalyzed by
Abx(+)O, where R162 acts as a general acid to facilitate
decarboxylation, while Y118 anchors the substrate through
hydrogen bonding (Figure S13). These findings highlight
distinct strategies: AccS relies on S61 and W63 to shield the
carboxyl group, whereas Abx(+)O uses Y118 and R162 to
remove it. This contrast underlies their opposing roles in
shaping polyketide structures, illustrating how small molecular
differences dictate biosynthetic outcomes.
■DISCUSSION
The terminal carboxyl group significantly influences the variety
and function of aromatic polyketides; yet, its role has often
been underappreciated. In FAS biosynthesis, FasS safeguards
the terminal carboxyl group, preventing unwanted side
reactions and ensuring the correct outcome. Unlike most
proteins in its widespread family, which typically drive
reactions, FasS uniquely stabilizes the carboxyl group without
altering it. Many type II polyketides feature chains with an
even number of carbons, and whether this carboxyl group is
retained or removed ultimately determines the final structure’s
shape.6,7,14,15,17,20 This suggests that protecting the carboxyl
group may be a common need in type II polyketide synthesis.
A BLAST search shows that the carboxyl-protecting proteins
FasS and WhiE-ORFI have at least 10,000 widespread
homologues (>30% identity), whereas the decarboxylase
AbxO has only 948 homologues (>30% identity). This
outcome aligns with the observation that the majority of
type II polyketides likely require carboxyl protection to
generate even-carbon chains, while a minority undergo
terminal decarboxylation to produce odd-carbon chains.
Tests with AccS, a homologue of FasS, showed that it holds
the carboxyl group of 1 in the binding pocket through
hydrogen bonds, avoiding its spontaneous decarboxylation and
preparing it for the next step catalyzed by ARO/CYC FasU. As
the first protein identified to shield this fragile component,
FasS advances our understanding of polyketide formation and
offers new ways to enhance their production.
In contrast, AbxO represents a new group of proteins that
remove the terminal carboxyl group during ABX synthesis.
While even-carbon chains dominate polyketides, certain
polyketides, including those with odd carbon counts, emerge
when this group is removed,6−15 subtly shifting the chain
length and creating diverse frameworks, like murayaquinone.15
Terminal decarboxylation is, therefore, crucial for generating
scaffold diversity. Currently, only decarboxylases for ring-
substituted carboxyl groups have been reported in the
biosynthesis of fungal type I aromatic polyketide anthraqui-
nones and bacterial anthracycline polyketides.42−48 However,
the exact mechanism of decarboxylation in the linear terminal
chain of aromatic polyketides remains unclear. Our work
identified AbxO as a novel bacterial decarboxylase protein to
steer the pathway toward ABXs. This finding deepens our
understanding of odd-carbon type II polyketide diversity and
its underlying mechanisms.

The evolution of polyketide-producing systems involves
changes and combinations that enhance their chemical range.37
Structural and phylogenetic analyses link FasS, AbxO, and
WhiE-ORFI, a protein tied to spore pigment production in
Streptomyces.34 Although WhiE-ORFI’s function has been
elusive since its first discovery in 1990,49 our study reveals it
manages carboxyl groups, either protecting or removing them.
The WhiE BGC, with its key C24 ancestor, has diversified into
systems to produce carbon chains with both longer and shorter
lengths.37 The ancestral WhiE BGC likely underwent enzyme
recruitment and fusion, potentially leading to the combination
of AbxO and FasS, which might have contributed to the
emergence of WhiE-ORFI. This trioFasS, AbxO, and WhiE-
ORFIforms protein families that serve as a guide for spotting
similar hidden reactions in polyketide systems. By controlling
the carboxyl group, these enzymes provide a powerful
mechanism for determining the production of even- or odd-
numbered polyketides and enhancing their structural diver-
sities.
■CONCLUSIONS
In summary, we thoroughly explored the distinct biosynthetic
pathways of ABX and FAS, identifying two novel protein
families: decarboxylases and carboxyl-protecting proteins. We
discovered FasS as the first carboxyl-protecting protein that
preserves the terminal carboxyl group of aromatic polyketides,
ensuring the high-fidelity biosynthesis of the FAS pathway.
Additionally, we identified AbxO as the first linear terminal
polyketide chain decarboxylase that is responsible for rerouting
the FAS pathway toward the ABX pathway. Phylogenetic
analysis of AbxO and FasS has provided valuable insights into
their evolutionary relationship with the WhiE-ORFI family,
shedding light on the mechanisms involved in the generation
of odd-carbon polyketide scaffolds. Based on mutagenesis
studies, we have proposed protecting mechanisms for both
carboxyl-protecting proteins and decarboxylases. This work
significantly advances our understanding of the biosynthesis of
even- and odd-numbered type II aromatic polyketide scaffolds
and offers new approaches for designing novel aromatic
polyketide structures.
