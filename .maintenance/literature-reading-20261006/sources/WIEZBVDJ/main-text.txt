ABSTRACT: Guided by the retrobiosynthesis hypothesis, we charac-
terized a fungal polyketide synthase−nonribosomal peptide synthetase
(PKS−NRPS) hybrid megasynthetase pathway to generate 2-trans-4-
trans-2-methylsorbyl-D-leucine (1), a polyketide amino acid conjugate
that inhibits Arabidopsis root growth. The biosynthesis of 1 includes a
PKS−NRPS enzyme to assemble an N-acyl amino alcohol intermediate,
which is further oxidized to an N-acyl amino acid (NAAA),
demonstrating a new biosynthetic logic for synthesizing NAAAs and
expanding the chemical space of products encoded by fungal PKS−NRPS clusters.
N-Acyl amino acids (NAAAs), featuring an amino acid
covalently linked to a carboxylic acid through an amide
bond, make up an important class of amino acid derivatives
with various biological activities that are extensively used in the
cosmetic, food, and pharmaceutical industries.1 The combina-
tion of hydrophilic amino acid and lipophilic carboxylic acid
moieties leads to the exceptional amphiphilic and biological
properties of NAAAs. Generally, the acyl groups of NAAAs are
from or derived from fatty acids. This type of NAAA is also
known as a fatty acid−amino acid conjugate. For example, N-
lauroyl-L-glutamate serves as a biosurfactant in cosmetic and
personal care formulations,2 N-oleoyl-L-phenylalanine has been
reported to be able to regulate energy homeostasis,3 N-
linoleoyl-L-tyrosine protects against transient cerebral ischemia
via the CB2 receptor,4 and jasmonic acid-L-isoleucine is a plant
defense hormone.5 In contrast, there are also other bioactive
NAAAs with non-fatty acid moieties, exemplified by the
phytotoxin coronatine produced by bacterial plant pathogens6
and the mycotoxin ochratoxin A produced by a number of
fungal species,7 as the acyl groups are polyketides. However, to
date, few polyketide−amino acid (PKAA) conjugates have
been reported in the literature.
Fungi are a rich resource of polyketide−amino acid hybrid
compounds with complex chemical structures and a wide
variety of biological activities, exemplified by the antiangio-
genesis cytochalasin E8 and the immunosuppressive pseurotin
A.9 The backbones of these molecules are assembled by
polyketide synthase−nonribosomal peptide synthetase (PKS−
NRPS) hybrid megasynthetases.10 Typically, fungal PKS−
NRPSs are bimodular enzymes with an N-terminal highly
reducing PKS module and a C-terminal NRPS module. The
PKS module functions iteratively to generate a reduced
polyketide acyl chain, which is then captured and condensed
with an activated amino acid to form an amino-acylated adduct
by the NRPS module (Figure 1). Then, this amino-acylated
adduct intermediate is released from the PKS−NRPS assembly
line by the reductive (R or R*) domain to yield a tetramic acid
through Dieckmann cyclization or a 3,4-dehydrogenated 2-
pyrrolidinone product through thioester reduction and

Knoevenagel condensation (Figure 1). Finally, the associated
tailoring enzymes catalyze various modifications on the
released products to form the mature natural products. Recent
advances in genome sequencing have revealed that a significant
number of PKS−NRPS-containing gene clusters are encoded
in fungal genomes, many of which are uncharacterized,
suggesting a great potential to mine novel bioactive natural
products from these clusters.11
From a retrobiosynthetic view, the fungal PKS−NRPS gene
clusters are ideal candidates for the synthesis of PKAA
conjugates when the amino-acylated adduct intermediates
assembled by the PKS−NRPSs are released not as the classical
processes as mentioned above but directly as a carboxylic acid
or an aldehyde/alcohol. However, as reported, the C-terminal
domain of a fungal PKS−NRPS is generally a reductive
domain, which can catalyze only the reduction or Dieckmann
cyclization release.10 Thus, it would be impossible for a PKAA
conjugate to be directly synthesized by only a fungal PKS−
NRPS enzyme. Therefore, a reasonable pathway for synthesiz-
ing a PKAA conjugate from a fungal PKS−NRPS cluster is that
the amino-acylated adduct intermediate assembled by the
PKS−NRPS is released as an aldehyde/alcohol (Figure 1),
which is then oxidized back to a carboxylic acid by one or two
tailoring enzymes. Hence, in an effort to mine novel NAAAs of
the PKAA conjugate type, we focused on the fungal PKS−
NRPS gene clusters that encode a PKS−NRPS with a
reductive function R domain together with one or two
oxidoreductase tailoring enzymes.
Using CcsA,12 a fungal PKS−NRPS from the cytochalasin
biosynthetic pathway, as a lead, we searched for the potential
PKAA conjugate biosynthetic gene clusters from the
sequenced fungal genomes. In particular, we mined the clusters
that encode minimal tailoring redox enzymes. We identified a
candidate cluster, named the sap cluster, that is conserved in
several different fungal species, including the marine fungus
Scedosporium apiosperum F41-1 (Figure 2A). The sap cluster
encodes only a PKS−NRPS (sapA) and a flavin-dependent
oxidoreductase (sapB) (Figure 2A and Table S1). Detailed
analysis revealed that SapA consists of 10 domains with a C-
terminal putative reductive function R domain, which contains
the intact conserved “GXXGXXG” NADPH binding motif and
the Ser-Tyr-Lys catalytic triad shared by short chain
dehydrogenase/reductase superfamily proteins13 (Figure 2D
and Figure S1), and SapB is an enzyme belonging to the
berberine bridge enzyme (BBE)-like oxidase superfamily
(Figure S3). In addition, the results of phylogenetic tree
analysis of the SapA R domain and SapB also suggested that
the SapA R domain would be a reductive function R domain
(Figure S2) and SapB would be a dehydrogenase (Figure S6).
To investigate the product of the sap cluster, these two genes
were cloned from the gDNA of S. apiosperum F41-1 under the
ADH2 promoter on 2μ plasmids and transformed into
engineered Saccharomyces cerevisiae strain BJ5464-NpgA.15

Analysis of the extract of the co-expression yeast strain showed
that one new metabolite (1) accumulated (Figure 2B, trace ii),
with a molecular weight of 239 (Figure S16). The product, 1
(12.0 mg/L), was isolated and characterized to be N-2-methyl-
sorbyl leucine, a PKAA conjugate type NAAA, based on one-
and two-dimensional nuclear magnetic resonance data (Figures
S9−S15 and Table S5). On the basis of the value of the proton
coupling constant (J = 14.3 Hz for H11−H12) and the NOE
correlations between H10 and H12 and between H11 and
H14, the double bond configurations in the 2-methylsorbyl
moiety of 1 were assigned to be 2-trans-4-trans-2-methylsorbyl.
Interestingly, 1 has a positive optical rotation [[α]25
D = +10.7
(c = 0.30, MeOH)]. Because only the amino acid unit in 1
contains a stereocenter, to further determine the absolute
configuration, we chemically synthesized the analogues 2-trans-
4-trans-sorbyl-D-leucine (5) and 2-trans-4-trans-sorbyl-L-leu-
cine (6). 5 has a positive optical rotation [[α]20
D = +10.6 (c =
0.08, MeOH)], and 6 has a negative optical rotation [[α]25
D =
−17.3 (c = 0.75, MeOH)]. Therefore, the absolute
configuration of the amino acid unit in 1 was assigned as D-
leucine. These results validated our hypothesis that PKAA
conjugate type NAAAs could be achieved from fungal PKS−
NRPS clusters.
On the basis of these results, we then turned to elucidating
the biosynthesis of 1. We proposed that the backbone of 1
could be assembled by the PKS−NRPS SapA and released as
an aldehyde or alcohol, which is then oxidized back to a
carboxylic acid by BBE-like enzyme SapB. To test this
hypothesis, we first removed the sapB gene from the
heterologous expression host. Analysis of the extract of the
yeast strain expressing sapA showed that the production of 1
was abolished and that a new metabolite, 2 (9.0 mg/L),
accumulated (Figure 2B, trace iv). The structure of 2 was
elucidated to be N-2-trans-4-trans-2-methylsorbyl leucinol
(Figures S17−S21 and Table S6). Similar to 1, 2 also has a
positive optical rotation [[α]25
D = +15.6 (c = 0.13, MeOH)].
Thus, we assigned the amino alcohol unit of 2 as D-leucinol. To
further confirm 2 is the product of the PKS−NRPS SapA, a
mutant of SapA was constructed in which catalytic residue
Y3785 (Figure S1), which is an essential residue of the Ser-
Tyr-Lys catalytic triad for the reductive activity of R domain,16
was mutated to alanine. When mutant SapA-Y3785A was
expressed in yeast, the production of 2 was abolished and no
other products were detected (Figure 2B, trace v). Together,
these data confirmed that SapA is responsible for the
biosynthesis of 2 and suggested that the BBE-like oxidase
SapB is responsible for the conversion of 2 to 1, catalyzing the
dehydrogenation of an alcohol to a carboxylic acid.
To further confirm the function of SapB, the recombinant
enzyme was expressed in Escherichia coli for in vitro
characterization. The N-terminal His6-tagged SapB was
purified by Ni-NTA affinity chromatography and migrated as
a single band at the predicted size of 54.9 kDa based on
sodium dodecyl sulfate−polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis
analysis (Figure S4). The purified SapB was yellow as expected,
because it contains the two conserved residues (Figure S5),
His in the “R/KXXGH” motif and Cys in the “CXXV/L/IG”
motif, that are usually found in a BBE-like oxidase for
covalently binding the cofactor FAD.17 To investigate the
function of SapB, the purified enzyme was assayed with 2. The
production of 1 was detected by LCMS analysis (Figure 2C,
trace ii). Additionally, adding exogenous FAD (50 μM), at 5
times the enzyme concentration (10 μM), had no effect on the
activity of SapB (Figure 2C, trace iii). Therefore, we concluded
that SapB is an FAD covalently bound enzyme catalyzing the
dehydrogenation of 2, an N-acyl amino alcohol, to 1, a PKAA
conjugate type NAAA.
On the basis of these findings from both in vivo and in vitro
studies, the biosynthetic route to form 1 was established. As
shown in Figure 2D, the PKS portion of SapA iteratively
catalyzes two rounds of polyketide chain elongation starting
from the acetyl group to yield the 2-trans-4-trans-2-
methylsorbyl chain, of which the methyltransferase (MT)
domain installs a branched methyl group during the second
round of elongation. Then, the polyketide chain is captured by
the condensation (C) domain to acylate the amino group of D-
leucine, which is proposed to be activated and incorporated
into the assembly line directly by the adenylation domain
because there is no epimerization domain found in the SapA
NRPS portion. Then, the amino-acylated adduct is released as
amino alcohol 2 via R domain-catalyzed four-electron
reduction. Lastly, 2 is oxidized back to amino acid derivative
1 by SapB.
Previously, biochemical studies of fungal PKS−NRPS hybrid
enzymes PsoA, involved in the biosynthesis of pseurotin A, and
ApdA, involved in the biosynthesis of aspyridone, revealed a
methylation-dependent programming rule that an α-methyl-
ation step during the polyketide chain synthesis is prerequisite
and a key checkpoint for chain transfer between PKS and
NRPS modules.18 As there is an α-methylation step involved in
the assemble of 2 by SapA, we queried whether this
methylation-dependent programming rule also exists in SapA.
To clarify this, we constructed another mutant of SapA, SapA-
G1416A, where the MT domain was inactivated through the
mutation of residue G1416 involved in the conserved
“GXGXG” SAM binding motif19 to alanine. When mutant
SapA-G1416A was expressed in yeast, the emergence of two
new metabolites, 3 (0.2 mg/L) and 4 (1.0 mg/L), instead of 2
was detected (Figure S8). The elucidated structures showed
that 3 is 2-trans-4-trans-sorbyl-D-leucinol and 4 is trans-2-
hexenoyl-D-leucinol (Figures S23−S34 and Tables S7 and S8).
Therefore, the results indicated that unlike the case for PsoA
and ApdA, the methylation step during the polyketide chain
synthesis of SapA is not critical in chain transfer to the NRPS
module. Interestingly, 4 contains a 2-hexenoyl moiety with a
saturated C−C bond at positions C11 and C12. As there is no
enoyl reductase domain found in SapA, and various acyl-CoAs
could be used by PsoA as the starter unit for polyketide
synthesis,18 a plausible explanation is that in the biosynthesis of
4, butyryl-CoA, instead of acetyl-CoA, was used by SapA-
G1416A as the starter unit. In addition, it is also possible that
an unknown endogenous oxidoreductase may catalyze the
reduction of 3 to yield 4.
Inspired by the PKAA conjugate coronatine, which is a
phytotoxin,6 we evaluated the phytotoxic activity of these
compounds toward Arabidopsis thaliana. In agar plate-based
Arabidopsis root growth assays (Figure 3), compound 1
showed a significant inhibitory impact on root growth at a
concentration of 25 μM, which is comparable to the positive
control that was treated with 25 μM jasmonic acid methyl
ester.20 Interestingly, root growth inhibition was not observed
upon treatment with N-acylated amino alcohols 2−4, or
NAAAs 5 and 6. The results indicated that the carboxylic acid
group and methyl group within the acyl chain of 1 are essential
for its phytotoxic activity.

In conclusion, guided by the retrobiosynthesis hypothesis,
we mined a fungal PKS−NRPS hybrid megasynthetase gene
cluster leading to the discovery of 1, a novel PKAA conjugate
type NAAA, which is identified as a plant root growth
inhibitor. Our work unraveled a new biosynthetic logic for
synthesizing NAAA via a fungal PKS−NRPS enzyme to
generate an N-acyl amino alcohol followed by dehydrogenation
to yield the NAAA. Our study expands the chemical space of
products derived from fungal PKS−NRPS biosynthetic
pathways and paves the way for the generation of novel
PKAA conjugate type NAAAs through fungal PKS−NRPS
pathway engineering in the future.
