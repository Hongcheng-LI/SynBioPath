Conformational Control in Diterpene Synthase TlnA: Elucidating the
Mechanism of 5−8−6 Skeleton Formation through Ring Expansion

ABSTRACT: TlnA produces a distinct cyclohexane-fused 5−8−6
ring system, different from the prevalent 5−8−5 scaffold
synthesized by well-established enzymes. This study identifies
two conformations of a carbocation intermediate, revealing how
the enzyme environment prohibits one conformation due to steric
hindrance, thereby directing the formation of the 5−8−6 system
over the 5−8−5 scaffold. This investigation enhances our
understanding of diterpene biosynthesis and the impact of enzyme
environments on chemical reactions, providing valuable insights
into the formation of complex cyclic structures.
D
iterpenes, a class of compounds known for diverse
structures and biological activities, are synthesized
through intricate pathways involving the cyclization of
geranylgeranyl diphosphate (GGPP), mediated by diterpene
synthases.1 A pivotal step in this process is the formation and
subsequent transformations of a cyclopentyl carbon cation
intermediate, which can undergo various rearrangements,
including hydride shifts, to yield diverse cyclic skeletons.2 In
the realm of diterpene biosynthesis, PaFS emerged as the
earliest reported bifunctional diterpene synthase capable of
producing fusicocca 2,10(14)-diene with a 5−8−5 skeleton;
these bifunctional enzymes are chimeric proteins consisting of
two structural domains, a prenyltransferase (PT) domain at the
C-terminus and a terpene cyclase (TC) domain at the N-
terminus, which can directly use IPP and DMAPP as co-
substrates.3 The crystal structure and cyclization mechanism of
PaFS have been extensively studied, offering deep insights into
the enzymatic facilitation of 5−8−5 skeleton formation.4
Building upon the foundational understanding on PaFS, our
previous research introduced TndC,5 the first diterpene
synthase identified to facilitate the formation of the diterpene
talarodiene with a 5−8−6 skeleton, marking a significant stride
in the field. However, the in vitro functionality and detailed
cyclization mechanism of TndC remain largely unexplored,
opening a vista of opportunities for further research.
In this study, we further investigate the enzymatic landscape
of diterpene biosynthesis, unveiling another diterpene
synthase, TlnA, endowed with the ability to generate a 5−
8−6 skeleton. This research not only confirms the in vitro
activity of TlnA but also embarks on an exploration of its
cyclization mechanism, thereby broadening our understanding
of the biosynthetic pathways leading to diverse diterpene
skeletons.
The second talarodiene [1 (Figure 1)] synthase was
identified from the genome of Talaromyces stipitatus and
designated as TlnA. Heterologous expression of tlnA in
Aspergillus oryzae (AO) NSAR1 afforded the AO-tlnA
transformants. Upon normal-phase high-performance liquid
chromatography (HPLC), two new products (1 and 2) were

major product (1) was identified as talarodiene,5 and the
minor product (2) was confirmed as a new product; their
structures were determined on the basis of the nuclear

On the basis of the established in vitro activity of TlnA, we
tried to delineate the cyclization mechanism catalyzed by TlnA.
A reaction coordinate calculation in the gas phase was
performed to elucidate the cyclization pathway (Figure 2A).
In the ring expansion process from intermediate i to ii, a
notable conformational change of i was observed, from the
initial i-A to i-B. The key differences (Figure 2B) between i-A
and i-B primarily lie in the dihedral angle involving atoms
C13−C15 and C19 (termed dih), the angle involving atoms
C13−C15 (termed ang), and the distance between atoms C13
and C15 (termed dis). The dih changes from −141.5° in i-A to
−93.3° in i-B. This substantial alteration can be attributed to
the rotation of the isopropyl group. Simultaneously, the
decrease in ang and dis allows C13 to approach C15, which
helps form a bond between them, meaning that i-B is better
suited for the subsequent alkyl shift and ring expansion
reaction. The energy barrier from i-A to i-B is extremely low,
merely 1.43 kcal/mol, implying that in the gas phase,
interconversion between i-A and i-B readily occurs.
The subsequent step is a 1,2-alkyl shift, leading to the
formation of a new bond between C13 and C15. The cationic
center was transferred from C15 to C14, yielding intermediate
ii. The energy barrier for this step is ∼8.4 kcal/mol. It is worth
noting that this step is an endergonic reaction of ∼4.9 kcal/
mol compared to that of i-B, as it involves the conversion from
a tertiary carbocation to a secondary carbocation. Subse-
quently, intermediate iii is formed via a 1,4-proton transfer, in
which proton H10 is transferred from C10 to C2, creating a
double bond between C10 and C14, and the cationic center
migrates to C3. The energy barrier for this step is ∼2.9 kcal/
mol. Then a 1,2-hydride shift and ring closure formed
intermediate iv. In this process, hydrogen H2 is transferred
from C2 to C3, thus resulting in the generation of a 5−8−6
tricyclic scaffold. Simultaneously, the cationic center trans-
locates from C3 to C7, with an energy barrier of ∼5.1 kcal/
mol. Overall, the total exergonicity (ΔG = Giv −Gi‑A) from i-A
to iv is calculated to be −14.4 kcal/mol, with the rate-limiting
step being the ring expansion reaction from i-B to ii.
To further validate this computational proposition exper-
imentally, we conducted feeding experiments with [1-13C]-and
[1-13C,2H3] sodium acetate. The former involved analyzing the
13C enrichment pattern of product 1, which demonstrated a
rearrangement of the carbon skeleton during the cyclization
process, namely, the breaking of the C13−C14 bond and the
formation of the C13−C15 bond leading to ring expansion.
The latter analyzed the deuterium-induced splitting of the
carbon signals, revealing a hydride shift at position C2 (Figures
S2−S7). These experimental results support our proposed
cyclization pathway.
To explore the structural aspects of TlnA that govern the
cyclization mechanism proposed above, we constructed a
structural model of the TC domain of TlnA based on I-
TASSER,6 followed by molecular docking, molecular dynamics
(MD) simulations, and quantum mechanics/molecular me-
chanics (QM/MM) calculations to determine the mode of
binding of intermediate i in the active pocket. Figure 3A shows
that, when i is located in the active site of TlnA, dih, ang, and
dis are determined to be −93.9°, 91.0°, and 2.2 Å respectively,
closely resembling those of i-B (Figure 3B). This aligns with
our experiment, yielding a product of the 5−8−6 ring scaffold.

surrounding residues, we observed that the distance between
the aromatic ring of Trp189 and C15 is 4.3 Å, indicating a

The 5−8−5 scaffold synthase PaFS, which shares almost
identical residues with the active site of the TC domain of
TlnA, contains a Trp197 residue that is essential for the
catalysis through strong cation−π interactions.4b Sequence and
structural analyses suggest that Trp189 in TlnA corresponds to
Trp197 in PaFS (Figure S8). Accordingly, we mutated Trp189
to alanine and observed no product formation (Figure S9),
indicating the presence of an essential cation−π interaction
between the ligand and Trp189. Two aromatic amino acid
residues, Phe65 and Phe89, near positively charged C14, C3,
and C7 were observed. These residues were individually
mutated to alanine and were found to be inactive (Figure S9),
thereby corroborating the proposed binding mode.
We then focus on how TlnA accommodates i-A and i-B.
Starting from the QM/MM-optimized i-B in TlnA, a potential
energy surface scan was performed to obtain i-A in TlnA. This
resulted in an energy minimum point with dih, ang, and dis
values of −158.2°, 119.0°, and 2.6 Å, respectively, indicating
the existence of i-A. Refined at the QM/MM (M062X/6-311+
+G**:AMBER) level of theory, the energy of i-A was ∼9.0
kcal/mol higher than that of i-B. This significant difference
indicates that i-A is highly unfavorable and negligible
compared to i-B in TlnA.
To understand the molecular basis of this preference, we
employed the linear interaction energy (LIE) method7 to
calculate the interaction energies between each surrounding
residue and i itself. As shown in Figure 4A, green bars
represent residues favoring i-A while red bars indicate disfavor.
Clearly, no residue strongly favors i-A. In contrast, Trp189
emerges as the most prominent residue that disfavors i-A. We
then intended to explain why i-B is more favored than i-A in
TInA. Notably, the distances from C15 to the center of the
Trp189 aromatic ring were measured to be 4.8 and 4.3 Å
respectively, indicating a stronger cation−π interaction
between i-B and Trp189. Furthermore, the methyl group
adjacent to C15 is seen to be very close to the aromatic ring of
Trp189, where the shortest distance is closer for i-A (3.1 Å)
than for i-B (3.4 Å), meaning stronger steric hindrance and an
inability to form a stronger cation−π interaction as in i-B
(Figure 4B,C).
In addition, as shown in Figure 4D, the isopropyl group
containing C15 in i-A was aligned with that in i-B, allowing
C15 in i-A to form a cation−π interaction that is as strong as
that in i-B. However, this results in i being unable to fit into
the enzyme’s active pocket. Clearly, due to steric hindrance,
positively charged C15 in i-A cannot approach the aromatic
ring of Trp189 more closely, resulting in a significantly higher
energy. Consequently, the probability of observing i-A is
significantly lower than that of observing i-B.
The question of why TlnA does not produce the
cyclopentane-fused 5−8−5 scaffold, a more common scaffold
in diterpene and sesterterpene family, remains. During the
synthesis of fusicoccadiene in PaFS,4a intermediate i is also
formed, but the product adopts a 5−8−5 structure. One can
see that the reaction of intermediate i is crucial in determining
whether the C ring adopts a five- or six-membered ring
structure, shown in Scheme 1. If a 1,2-hydride shift occurs,
then the C ring remains as a five-membered ring, whereas a
1,2-alkyl shift expands the C ring to a six-membered ring. To
investigate why the hydride shift does not occur in TlnA, we
performed a reaction coordinate calculation to study the 1,2-
hydride shift reaction of intermediate i in the gas phase.
Previously obtained conformations i-A and i-B were used as
starting structures for the reaction coordinate calculation.
Interestingly, the hydride shift reaction occurs only from i-A,
while i-B requires conversion to i-A before this reaction. As
shown in Figure 5A, during this shift reaction, atom H14 is
transferred to positively charged C15. When the unoccupied p-
type lone pair orbital of C15 is coplanar with the C14−H14 σ-
bond, the s orbital of H14 can overlap maximally with it. This
indicates that the hydride shift reaction tends to occur when
the isopropyl plane containing C15 (Cplane) is perpendicular
to the plane formed by H14, C14, and C15 (Hplane). We
observed this phenomenon during the hydride transfer process
starting from i-A. The transition state structure (Figure 5B)
shows that the dihedral angle (dih2) formed by H14, C14,

perpendicular to the Hplane, which favors hydride transfer.
The analysis of natural bonding orbitals (NBOs) of the lone
pair*C15 and σC14−H14 orbitals also supports favorable
interaction between the two orbitals. However, when we
forced the hydride transfer reaction to occur starting from i-B,
by keeping ang unchanged and setting dih2 to 95.8°, the C19
in the isopropyl group is very close to C13 (Figure 5C), and
the distance between H19 and H13 is only 1.7 Å. Obviously, in
i-B, the steric hindrance between C19 and C13 prevents the
Cplane from being perpendicular to the Hplane, preventing the
p orbital of C15 from overlapping maximally with the s orbital
of H14. Furthermore, even if the steric hindrance is overcome,
resulting in the perpendicular alignment of Cplane and Hplane,
the interaction between the lone pair*C15 and σC14−H14 orbitals
remains repulsive due to their opposite phases. This explains
why the hydride transfer reaction cannot occur directly from i-
B but can from i-A.
The energy profile of the 1,2-hydride transfer starting from i-
A was calculated (Figure 5D). The energy barrier of this
reaction is ∼8.5 kcal/mol, which is comparable to the energy
barrier (∼8.4 kcal/mol) of the alkyl transfer from i-B.
However, as mentioned above, when intermediate i binds in
TlnA, it adopts i-B. Thus, we delineate a mechanistic pathway
in which the structural features of TlnA create a steric
environment that significantly hinders the formation of a 5−8−
5 skeleton, steering the enzymatic pathway decisively toward
the formation of a 5−8−6 ring system.
In conclusion, this study elucidates the cyclization
mechanism of diterpene synthase TlnA, uniquely generating
a 5−8−6 ring system, and underscores the influence of enzyme
environments on chemical reactions. The successful in vitro
expression of TlnA allowed for the identification of its
cyclization products. By integrating computational and
experimental methods, we identified intermediate conforma-
tions that are conducive to or prohibit ring expansion,
elucidating the mechanism of 5−8−6 skeleton formation.
This highlights the contrast with enzymes like PaFS, which
produce a 5−8−5 skeleton and likely favor conformation i-A
over i-B due to subtle distinctions in the active site
environment. Investigating TlnA’s conformational control
unveils mechanisms guiding its specific production of six-
membered rings, suggesting the potential to manipulate
enzyme-driven reactions for desired outcomes. This calls for
further investigation of the nuances of enzyme environments
and their impact on reaction pathways.
