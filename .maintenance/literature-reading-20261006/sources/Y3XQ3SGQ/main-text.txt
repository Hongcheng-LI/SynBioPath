ABSTRACT: The 3(2H)-furanone unit is observed in many
biologically active natural products, as represented by the
antifungal medication griseofulvin. Setosusin (1) is a fungal
meroditerpenoid featuring a unique spiro-fused 3(2H)-furanone
moiety; however, the biosynthetic basis for spirofuranone
formation has not been investigated since its isolation. Therefore,
in this study we identiﬁed the biosynthetic gene cluster of 1 in the
fungus Aspergillus duricaulis CBS 481.65 and elucidated its
biosynthetic pathway by heterologous reconstitution of related
enzyme activities in Aspergillus oryzae. To understand the reaction
mechanism to aﬀord spirofuranone, we subsequently performed a
series of in vivo and in vitro isotope-incorporation experiments and theoretical calculations. The results indicated that SetF, the
cytochrome P450 enzyme that is critical for spirofuranone synthesis, not only performs the epoxidation of the polyketide portion of
the substrate but also facilitates the protonation-initiated structural rearrangement to yield 1. Finally, a mutagenesis experiment using
SetF identiﬁed Lys303 as one of the potential catalytic residues that are important for spirofuranone synthesis.
■INTRODUCTION
Oxidative rearrangements signiﬁcantly contribute to structural
complexiﬁcation and diversiﬁcation in natural product biosyn-
thesis.1 Biosyntheses of structurally complicated natural
products often utilize oxidative enzymes for the synthesis of
their molecular architectures, including cytochrome P450
monooxygenases and non-heme iron-dependent oxygenases/
oxidases.2 In recent decades, a large number of oxidative
enzymes for chemical transformations have been identiﬁed and
characterized from secondary metabolite pathways, some of
which have been applied as biocatalysts for chemically
challenging reactions.3 Nevertheless, there remain many
natural products with unique structures for which biosynthetic
mechanisms have not yet been elucidated. Therefore,
biosynthetic studies on such molecules may discover enzymes
that could be used for unusual conversions and as potential
biocatalysts for synthetic organic chemistry.
Setosusin (1) is a tremorgenic fungal metabolite that was
originally isolated from the fungus Corynascus setosus and
harbors a characteristic spiro-fused 3(2H)-furanone ring
moiety (Figure 1).4 Biosynthetically, 1 appears to be derived
from the polyketide 5-methyl triacetic acid lactone (5-methyl-
TAL) and geranylgeranyl pyrophosphate (GGPP) and is thus
classiﬁed as a meroterpenoid (or meroditerpenoid).5 5-Methyl-
TAL is often utilized as a non-terpenoid portion of fungal
meroditerpenoids, which display a wide range of biological
activity (Figure 1).6 Biosynthetic and synthetic biology studies

of several 5-methyl-TAL-derived molecules have recently been
reported,7 providing the general molecular basis for their
biosynthesis. However, the 3(2H)-furanone system of 1 is
rarely found in fungal meroterpenoids, having been observed
only in 1 and higginsianins D and E.6a Importantly, 3(2H)-
furanone units are recognized as pharmacophores of many
biologically active natural products and have been challenging
synthetic targets.8 Although there have been reports regarding
the biosynthesis of 3(2H)-furanones,9 the reaction mecha-
nisms to generate the structure remain enigmatic for many
3(2H)-furanone-containing natural products, including 1.
Furthermore, diverse synthetic strategies are used in nature
to construct the 3(2H)-furanone units. Understanding
setosusin biosynthesis would thus reveal a new mechanism
for 3(2H)-furanone formation and provide a biocatalyst to
synthesize this medicinally important structure.
In this study, we identiﬁed the biosynthetic gene cluster of 1
in Aspergillus duricaulis CBS 481.65 and elucidated its
biosynthetic pathway through heterologous reconstitution of
the biosynthesis. Our study revealed that the cytochrome P450
monooxygenase SetF is the key enzyme for spirofuranone
synthesis. To further understand the reaction mechanism for
spirofuranone formation, we conducted isotope-feeding experi-
ments and theoretical calculations on the SetF-mediated
reaction. Finally, we provide the reaction mechanism for the
transformation, in which SetF is responsible for epoxidation of
the substrate and also facilitates the subsequent epoxide
opening and skeletal rearrangement to install the spirofuranone
system of 1.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Discovery and Bioinformatic Analysis of the Setosu-
sin Biosynthetic Gene Cluster. To identify the biosynthetic
gene cluster of setosusin (1), we examined the genome
sequence of one of its producers, A. duricaulis CBS 481.65
(KACC 41137).10 Subsequently, a genomic region encoding
homologues of known fungal meroterpenoid biosynthetic
enzymes was discovered and designated as the set cluster
(Figure 2A and Table S1). The set cluster encodes the
nonreducing polyketide synthase (NR-PKS) SetA, the
geranylgeranyl pyrophosphate synthase (GGPS) SetE, the
prenyltransferase SetD, the FAD-dependent monooxygenases
(FMOs) SetB and SetG, the terpene cyclase SetH, the
cytochrome P450 monooxygenases SetF and SetJ, the short-
chain dehydrogenase/reductase (SDR) SetI, the α-ketogluta-
rate (αKG)-dependent dioxygenase SetK, and the acetyltrans-
ferase SetC. On the basis of the structures of known natural
products and the biosynthesis of the analogous fungal
meroditerpenoids 15-deoxyoxalicins,11 the biosynthetic path-
way of 1 could be roughly predicted as follows (Figure 2B).
Initially, the setosusin biosynthesis generates brevione B6b (2)
in a manner similar to the formation of decaturin D (Figure
S1). The NR-PKS SetA ﬁrst synthesizes 5-methyl-TAL, which
undergoes geranylgeranylation catalyzed by the prenyltransfer-
ase SetD using geranylgeranyl pyrophosphate (GGPP)
provided by the GGPS SetE. The FMO SetG next epoxidizes
the terminal oleﬁn of the prenyl group, which is cyclized by the
terpene cyclase SetH. Subsequently, oxidative ring closure by
the P450 SetJ installs the spiro ether system, and the SDR SetI
performs alcohol dehydrogenation at C-3 to yield 2.
Compound 2 would then be transformed into brevione L10
(3) by the putative Baeyer−Villiger monooxygenase SetB and
the αKG-dependent enzyme SetK. Finally, 3 would be
converted to 1 by the acetyltransferase SetC and the P450
SetF, in which SetF is expected to facilitate spirofuranone
formation.
Heterologous Reconstitution of Setosusin Biosyn-
thesis. To investigate the predicted biosynthetic pathway of 1,
we sought to reconstitute its biosynthesis in Aspergillus oryzae
NSAR1,12 which has been widely used for the heterologous
expression of fungal biosynthetic genes.7b,13 First, we
introduced the seven genes expected to be responsible for
the production of 2 (setA, -D, -E, -G, -H, -J, and -I) to the A.
oryzae strain. The transformant generated one major product,
which is not observed in the host strain (Figure 3A, traces i
and ii). The metabolite was then isolated after large-scale
cultivation and subjected to spectroscopic analyses, which
identiﬁed it as 2. The transformant also yielded two minor
products, 4 and 5; 4, designated as brevione N, was
determined to be an analogue of 2 with a C-3 hydroxy
group as well as its direct precursor (Figure 3C). Meanwhile,
compound 5 was identiﬁed as colletopyrone,14 a dimer of 5-
methyl-TAL with an additional methylene group (Figure 3C)
and a shunt pathway product synthesized by an endogenous
enzyme of A. oryzae.13d Collectively, this indicated that 2 was
synthesized as predicted (Figure S1).
To clarify which enzyme is required next in the biosynthetic
process, we created two eight-gene-expressing transformants in
which either the FMO gene setB or the αKG-dependent
dioxygenase gene setK was introduced along with the
biosynthetic genes for 2. Interestingly, each transformant
produced a new major metabolite, 6 or 7, respectively (Figure
3A, traces iii and iv). Compound 6, which was the product
from SetB and named brevione R, possessed a seven-
membered lactone ring (Figure 3C), indicating that SetB
serves as a Baeyer−Villiger monooxygenase. The strain with
setK yielded 7, designated as brevione S, which contains an α-
hydroxy group at C-1 (Figure 3C). Thus, SetK should be the
C-1 hydroxylase. As expected, coexpression of setB and setK in
the brevione B-producing strain provided 3 as the major

Next, we aimed to establish the complete biosynthetic
pathway for 1. To this end, we further expressed the

(Figure 3B, traces v to viii), suggesting that the acetyl group is
important for substrate recognition by SetF.
Isotope-Incorporation Experiments to Investigate
the Reaction Mechanism of Spirofuranone Synthesis.
Although the experiments mentioned above revealed the
involvement of the P450 SetF in spirofuranone synthesis, the
mechanism to construct this unique structure remains unclear.
To obtain in-depth insight into the reaction mechanism, we
conducted a series of isotope-feeding experiments. Initially, the
A. oryzae transformant that produces 1 was cultivated in the
presence of [methyl-13C]methionine, sodium [1,2-13C2]-
acetate, or sodium [1-13C,18O2]acetate. Compound 1 was
labeled only at C-7′ by [methyl-13C]methionine (Figure 4A),
indicating that C-7′ of 1 is derived from C-7′ of 8, which is
originally installed by the methyltransferase domain of the PKS
SetA. When sodium [1,2-13C2]acetate was fed,
13C−13C
couplings were clearly observed at C-1′/C-2′, C-3′/C-4′, and
C-5′/C-6′ of 1 (Figure 4A), showing that intact acetate units
remains in the polyketide portion of 1. Labeling with sodium
[1-13C,18O2]acetate resulted in the incorporation of 13C at C-
1′, C-3′, and C-5′ (Figure 4A), suggesting that no C−C bond
rearrangement occurs in the polyketide-derived moiety.
Furthermore, 18O-induced isotope shifts were observed at C-
1′ and C-3′ in the 13C NMR spectrum (Figures 4A and S3). A
large isotope shift at C-1′ (5.1 Hz) indicated that the carbonyl
oxygen at C-1′ was labeled with 18O, whereas a relatively small
isotope shift (3.3 Hz) was observed at C-3′. This would not be
caused by the carbonyl oxygen bound to C-3′ and might
instead be attributable to a long-range isotope eﬀect.15
Meanwhile, no isotope shift was observed at C-5′, demonstrat-
ing that the oxygen atom bound to this carbon was not derived
from the oxygen of the acetate unit.
To further investigate the origin of the oxygen atoms in the
polyketide portion of 1, bioconversion of 8 with the A. oryzae
strain expressing setF was performed under an
18O2
atmosphere. The 13C NMR spectrum of the labeled 1 revealed
the presence of isotope shifts at C-2′ and C-5′, conﬁrming that
the oxygen atom between C-2′ and C-5′ is derived from
molecular oxygen (Figures 4B and S3). Finally, we examined
whether an H2O molecule is utilized to synthesize 1. Crude
enzyme extract was subsequently obtained from the A. oryzae
transformant harboring setF, and an in vitro enzymatic reaction
using 8 as a substrate was performed in the presence of H2
18O.

LC-MS analysis of the reaction products revealed the
incorporation of one atom of 18O into 1 (Figure S4). We
also conﬁrmed that incubation of 1 in H2
18O does not lead to
the incorporation of 18O (Figure S4), illustrating that one H2O
molecule is required during spirofuranone formation.
Considering all of the isotope-labeling experiments and the
structures of 1 and 8, we reasoned that the carbonyl oxygen at
C-3′ is derived from an H2O molecule and that the oxygen
atom between C-14 and C-1′ is acetate-derived and originally
bound to C-3′ (Figures 4A and 4C). On the basis of the
obtained labeling patterns, one possible reaction mechanism to
furnish the spirofuranone system could be as follows. Initially,
the P450 SetF oxidizes the oleﬁn at C-2′/C-3′ regio/
stereoselectively to aﬀord an epoxide (IM0; Figure 5);
epoxidation is one of the major reactions catalyzed by
P450s.16 Epoxide IM0 would then undergo a spontaneous
domino-type oxonium cation transportation reaction (inside or
outside the enzyme), including epoxide ring opening,
hydration/dehydration, and skeletal rearrangements, to gen-
erate the 3(2H)-furanone structure.
Theoretical Calculations on the SetF-Catalyzed
Reaction. To check the feasibility of the spontaneous
conversion of the epoxide intermediate IM0 to 1, we next
carried out density functional theory (DFT) calculations
combined with the global reaction route mapping (GRRM)
method. To facilitate the calculations, the seven-membered
lactone A ring, which would have little eﬀect on the skeletal
rearrangement reaction, was omitted in all of the calculation
procedures (Figure S5). Our initial calculations strongly
suggested that the spontaneous epoxy-ring-opening reaction
of IM0 does not occur kinetically under neutral conditions.
However, under acidic conditions, the proton-mediated ring-
opening reaction proceeds smoothly at ambient temperature to
yield protonated oxocarbenium intermediate IM2 with an
activation energy of 12.8 kcal mol−1. Indeed, the model
structure of SetF suggested that one lysine residue (Lys303),
two serine residues (Ser311 and Ser370), and one threonine
residue (Thr371) are present in the vicinity of the substrate-
binding site (Figure S6), which may play an important role in
promoting epoxy ring opening via Brønsted acid catalysis. The
computed pathway for the conversion of IM1 into IM8 under
acidic conditions is illustrated in Figure 5. The SetF-catalyzed
regio/stereoselective epoxidation of 8 aﬀords the key
intermediate IM0. Protonation of IM0 initiates the multistep
oxonium cation cascade leading to 1 and yields IM1 (IM0 +
H3O+). Proton-mediated epoxide ring opening of IM1,
facilitated by the oxygen lone pair of the tetrahydrofuran
ring, proceeds smoothly to form oxocarbenium intermediate
IM2 with an activation free energy of 12.8 kcal mol−1 and a
large overall stabilization energy (38.1 kcal mol−1). After the
interconversion from IM2a to IM2b, the oxocarbenium carbon
is subjected to nucleophilic attack by an H2O molecule to yield
a protonated cyclic hemiacetal intermediate (IM3). From IM3,
the acid-mediated hemiacetal opening reaction occurs
smoothly to cleave the C−O bond of the tetrahydrofuran
ring with an activation barrier of only 1.8 kcal mol−1, aﬀording

tautomerization of IM4a to IM4b with a slight endothermicity,
the C-14 OH group attacks the lactone C-1′ to give protonated
bicyclic hemiortho ester intermediate IM5, which undergoes
translactonization to provide tetracyclic spiro lactone inter-
mediate IM6. IM6 has an eight-membered ring structure with
the lactone carbonyl oxygen and the enol form of the 1,3-
dicarbonyl moiety linked via a proton. After the conforma-
tional change of IM6a to IM6b with a relatively large
exothermicity (+8.9 kcal mol−1), acid-mediated annulation by
1,2-addition of the C-3′ OH group to the oxocarbenium
carbon followed by dehydration of the resultant cyclic
hemiacetal intermediate IM7 assisted by an enol moiety yields
the spiro-fused 3(2H)-furanone ring structure (IM8). Finally,
IM8 is subjected to deprotonation (−H3O+) to yield 1.
The multistep oxonium cation cascade pathway/mechanism
(path A) leading to the double spiro skeleton aligned well with
the results of the isotope-feeding experiments and had a large
overall exothermicity (>35 kcal mol−1). However, the energy
diagram from IM1 to IM8 (Figure 6, dashed line) suggests that
the cascade is unlikely to occur spontaneously at ambient
temperature because of the high barriers (>25 kcal mol−1) for
addition of H2O to the oxocarbenium carbon (IM2a →
TS_2b−3) and the translactonization step (IM4a →TS_4b−
5). However, among several possibilities, we found that the
addition of one more H2O molecule to these steps/TSs
dramatically lowered the barriers. Furthermore, we identiﬁed
the entire reaction pathway of the mechanism involving the
participation of the extra H2O molecule (path B), leading to
IM8′ (IM8 + H2O) from IM1′ (IM1 + H2O) (Figure 6, solid
line). This was ultimately kinetically and thermodynamically
more favorable than path A in most steps. More importantly,
(i) path B was consistent with the peculiar multiple
rearrangements leading to 1, in accordance with the isotope-
labeling experiments; (ii) the extra H2O changed the
sawtoothlike bond rearrangement proﬁle (path A) into a
staircaselike one (path B) with a very large overall
exothermicity (>35 kcal mol−1) along which the entire energy
proﬁle descends as the reactions proceed; and (iii) the
activation barriers were all suﬃciently low for the reactions to
proceed smoothly at ambient temperature. In particular, the
extra H2O played a critical role as a proton donor and acceptor
in the hemiacetal structure formation steps (IM2a →TS_2b−
3 and IM4a →TS_4b−5). Conversely, the steps for the
formation of the spiro-fused 3(2H)-furanone ring (IM6 →
IM7 →IM8) may not require an extra H2O molecule(s). The
current computational study indicated that the unique double
spiro ring formation from epoxide IM0 to aﬀord 1 proceeds
spontaneously under aqueous acidic (but not neutral)
conditions, implying the requirement of SetF to provide an
acidic environment for the spirofuranone formation.
Search for Catalytic Residues in SetF. The calculations
indicated the requirement of acidic conditions for the
formation of 1 from epoxide IM0. If IM0 is released to the
cytosol from the active site of SetF, IM0 will not readily
undergo spontaneous rearrangement. Thus, we reasoned that
SetF is not only responsible for the epoxidation but also
contributes to the subsequent rearrangement. As mentioned
above, several polar/charged amino acid residues exist in the
substrate-binding site of SetF, and we therefore conducted
mutational experiments on these residues to investigate their
importance in spirofuranone synthesis. To this end, Lys303

Thr371 were replaced with Ala. To fairly evaluate their
activities, the wild-type and variant setF genes were integrated
onto the sC locus17 by CRISPR-Cas9-mediated homologous
recombination,18 and the resultant transformants were
incubated in the presence of 8. Subsequently, no conversion
was observed from the K303A variant, whereas the K303R
variant exhibited a reduced conversion rate for the synthesis of
1 (Figure S7). Alternatively, the S311A, S370A, and T371A
variants were all able to produce 1 (Figure S7). Contrary to
our expectation that some mutants could produce the epoxide
intermediate IM0, none of the variants yielded additional
products compared with the wild-type enzyme.
On the basis of the mutagenesis experiment, only Lys303
appeared to be critical for the SetF-catalyzed reaction among
the tested amino acid residues. However, as the K303A variant
did not aﬀord any products, it remains unclear whether Lys303
is simply important for substrate recognition or is also essential
for the rearrangement reaction. Interestingly, a plant P450,
vinorine hydroxylase, is known to catalyze both oxidative and
isomerization reactions, and it was hypothesized that acidic or
basic residues in the substrate-binding site would assist the
isomerization reaction.19 Thus, SetF might also be a bifunc-
tional P450 enzyme capable of catalyzing two types of
reactions. Considering that we were unable to identify an
amino acid residue that was critical only for the epoxide ring
opening, Lys303 could be responsible for both substrate
recognition and initiation of the rearrangement reaction.
Future structural biological studies on SetF might elucidate
the function of Lys303 and how the enzyme performs this
unusual rearrangement reaction.
The 3(2H)-furanone structure is widely distributed in nature
and known to be synthesized in several diﬀerent manners.
Examples include modiﬁcations of sugar molecules20 and
P450-mediated oxidative heterocyclization, as seen in the
biosynthesis of griseofulvin and gregatin A.9b,d Unlike the SetF-
catalyzed reaction, most of the known mechanisms for 3(2H)-
furanone formation do not involve a complicated rearrange-
ment reaction. The furanone synthesis in the scopranone
pathway, for which the detailed reaction mechanism has yet to
be established, is somewhat similar to the reaction by SetF, as
the furanone moiety is formed via the rearrangement of a
pyrone provided by PKS.9c However, SprT, the enzyme for the
furanone synthesis in the scopranone biosynthesis, is an FMO
and might adopt a mechanism distinct from that of SetF.
Collectively, SetF represents a rare example in which 3(2H)-
furanone is synthesized by a highly complicated P450-
mediated rearrangement.
■CONCLUSION
Although setosusin (1) was ﬁrst isolated 25 years ago, its
biosynthesis, including the mechanism for spirofuranone
formation, has never been investigated. In this study, we
identiﬁed the biosynthetic gene cluster of 1 and successfully
elucidated its complete biosynthetic pathway, in which the
P450 SetF is engaged in the generation of the unique
spirofuranone system. Our isotope-incorporation, computa-
tional, and mutational studies suggest an intriguing bifunction-
ality of SetF, which ﬁrst epoxidizes the substrate, followed by
protonation-initiated rearrangement to aﬀord the spirofur-
anone. Our study thus demonstrates the usefulness of
combining experimental and computational analyses to
understand complicated chemical reactions. Setosusin ana-
logues are widespread in nature, and some of them possess
unique chemical structures, such as the unusual cyclo-
heptenone and oxepane ring found in brevione E.6b Hence,
our study might facilitate further biosynthetic and synthetic
biology studies on this class of natural products, potentially
leading to the discovery of many more enigmatic enzymes and
providing new, useful molecules.
