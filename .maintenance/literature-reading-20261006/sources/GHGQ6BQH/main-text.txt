ABSTRACT: Nitrogen-containing heterocycles (N-heterocycles) are essential in drug discovery, with a majority of best-selling
small-molecule drugs containing at least one N-heterocycle. Various enzymes have been characterized or engineered for N-
heterocycle biosynthesis, but these attempts are frequently restricted to specific N-heterocycle skeletons and substitutes.
Multidomain carboxylic acid reductase (CAR) is gaining attention for its ability to reduce amino acids to amino aldehydes, which
can be converted into a variety of N-heterocycle skeletons through nonenzymatic dimerization and rearrangement. This unique
cyclization process could expand the biocatalytic potential of CARs beyond those of specific N-heterocycles. Here, we applied
domain-based genome mining to identify 32,735 CAR enzymes, providing the most comprehensive landscape of CARs to date.
Through in vivo and in vitro assays, we characterized an untapped CAR family that exhibited substrate tolerance on amino acids and
efficiently catalyzed the formation of various N-heterocycles from amino acids. By harnessing the potential of promiscuous CARs for
one-step biosynthesis and diversification of N-heterocycles, we created a library of N-heterocycles for bioassays, identifying multiple
quorum sensing (QS) inhibitors and protease inhibitors. Our discovery of untapped promiscuous CARs offers a promising
alternative for the biosynthesis and diversification of N-heterocycles, making them a valuable resource for chemical and synthetic
biologists.
KEYWORDS: genome mining, carboxylic acid reductases (CARs), N-heterocycles, one-step biosynthesis and diversification, bioactivity
1. INTRODUCTION
Nitrogen-containing heterocycles (N-heterocycles), which are
cyclic molecules featuring a nitrogen atom within the cyclic
skeleton, constitute a prominent subset of heterocycles. Being
ubiquitous in natural and synthetic pharmaceutical products,
these compounds are of immense importance in drug
discovery. The presence of at least one N-heterocycle has
been observed in two-thirds of the top 200 small-molecule
drugs (until 2018) approved by the U.S. Food and Drug
Administration,1−3 exemplified by acipimox4 (a lipid-lowering
agent), dacarbazine5 (an anticancer drug), and pyridostigmine6
(a myasthenia gravis-treatment drug) (Figure 1C). This
highlights the importance of N-heterocycles in drug discovery
and the need to expand structural diversity to facilitate the
development of new drug candidates. The significance of this
compound class has prompted the development of numerous
catalytic methods for their diversity-oriented synthesis, which
could provide diverse natural product-inspired libraries. Both
chemical and biocatalytic synthesis are frequently employed in
diversity-oriented synthesis7,8 ; however, conventional chem-

Biocatalysts are becoming increasingly essential for the
synthesis of N-heterocycles. Specifically, diversity-oriented
biosynthesis offers a superior alternative by modifying scaffolds
or incorporating functional groups using substrate-tolerant
enzymes, which provides benefits such as easy assembly,
synthetic selectivity, and environmental awareness.9,10
Up to date, several enzyme families for N-heterocycle
biosynthesis have been discovered11,12 and repurposed for the
generation of diverse N-heterocyclic compounds,11,12 mainly
including α,ω-diamine transaminases (α,ω-DTAs),3,13 cyclo-
dipeptide synthase (CDPS),14,15 Pictet−Spenglerase
(PSase),16 polyketide synthase (PKS),17 and nonribosomal
peptide synthase (NRPS).18 The α,ω-DTAs have been utilized
for the synthesis of N-heterocycle precursors but have a strict
preference for terminal aliphatic diamines. The multimodular
NRPSs from Clostridium sp. KLE1755 or Ruminococcus sp.
5_1_39BFAA, containing a reductase domain, can produce 17
pyrazinone molecules,18 while the single-module NRPS from
Pseudomonas fluorescens HKI0770 can produce ten pyreudione
molecules.19,20 Additionally, CDPS in Nocardiopsis dassonvillei
can form seven unique diketopiperazine molecules.15 However,
the diversity of their products relies on the substrate tolerance
determined by the A or C domains in NRPSs, or the P1/P2
pockets present in CDPS. Consequently, the attempts aimed at
expanding the chemical space of N-heterocycles by NRPS21 or
CDPS22 are often restricted to specific skeletal structures and a
restricted range of substrates.
Multidomain carboxylic acid reductase (CAR),23,24 is a type
of single-module NRPS-like enzyme that has been gaining
significant attention in the field of biocatalysis. Similarly to
NRPSs, single-module NRPS-like enzymes also utilize
thiotemplated enzymology, and they could generate many
interesting natural products20,25−28 (listed in Table S1). CAR
enzymes contain adenylation (A), thiolation (T), and
reductase (R) domains,29 and they can reduce a wide range
of carboxylic acids into corresponding aldehydes.23,24,30 Except
for the well-characterized CARs with relaxed selectivity on
aromatic acids and fatty acids,23,24,30 several reported CARs
(listed in Tables S2−S3) could reduce amino acids to amino
aldehydes, with strict selectivity.31−33 Of note, the non-
enzymatic dimerization and rearrangement of CAR-reduced
amino aldehydes expand the potential of CAR enzymes to
biosynthesize a variety of N-heterocycles, including dihydro-
pyrazine21,31,32,34,35 and pyrazine21,31,32,34,35 rings derived from
tyrosine, phenylalanine, leucine or valine, as well as recently
discovered pyrazinone32 and imidazole32 rings derived from
leucine or valine (Figure 1A, showing all reported bacterial
CARs converting amino acids into N-heterocycles). Given the

two ways. First, the nonenzymatic dimerization and rearrange-

possible N-heterocycles may be under-explored, containing

(Figure 1A, Tables S2−S3). Overall, despite possessing
immense biosynthesis potential, the complete enzymatic
capabilities of CARs have yet to be fully explored. This is
largely due to the limited number of identified CARs and their
specific substrate limitations (Tables S2−S3).

2. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
2.1. Uncovering Novel CARs through Domain-
Scanning Genome Mining. In our recent studies, we
achieved successful systematic investigations of targeted
biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) or enzymes, including
peptidases and rSAM enzymes, by employing a domain-
scanning-based genome mining strategy.40−43 To discover the
untapped CARs from a vast data set of bacterial genomes, we
employed this domain-scanning genome mining approach that
capitalizes on the shared domain architecture among all CARs.

Our methodology utilized antiSMASH 6.044 to search for
NRPS or NRPS-like enzymes from 223,243 bacterial genomes
in the NCBI database. By examining the absence of the
condensation domain (C domain) and the presence of the
three domains (A, T, and R domains) present in NRPS or
NRPS-like enzymes, we identified potential CARs, resulting in
32,735 hits (Supplementary Data 1). Compared with previous
genome mining approaches for novel CARs, our method
differed in two key aspects. First, we analyzed a larger database
with more genome sequences. Second, we employed a more
precise domain-based genome mining approach (refer to Table
S4) that reduces reliance on sequence similarity. Consequently,
our study identified a significantly larger number of CARs than
previous research,31,45−48 providing a more comprehensive
understanding of the diversity of bacterial CARs. To prioritize
novel CARs with unforeseen enzymatic potential, we next
analyzed the sequence similarity of A and R domains of these
putative CARs (Supplementary Data 1) using the EFI-Enzyme
Similarity Tool (EFI−EST)49 to identify the potential novel
families that may imply different substrate selectivity.
Subsequently, we generated sequence similarity networks
(SSNs) to visualize the sequence space and discovery status
of the CARs, with an e-value threshold of 10−100 and 50%
sequence identity. The SSN analysis based on the A domains
(Figure 2A) generated 149 families, with the largest CAR
family (Family 1, 16,580 proteins) containing well-established
NiCAR50 and MmCAR30 enzymes (Figure S1, Table S2)
capable of reducing a broad range of aromatic or aliphatic
carboxylic acids to aldehydes. The second largest family
(Family 2, 4,350 proteins) includes previously reported
PfCAR,32 PeCAR,33 and PsCAR51 (Figures 1A, S1, Table
S2), predicted to be able to reduce specific aliphatic amino
acids. We also identified BcCAR,52 ScCAR,31 SvCAR,53 and
XiCAR21 families, which reduce aliphatic or aromatic amino
acids (Figures 1A, S1, Table S2). Notably, the majority of the
identified CARs did not cluster with known ones, indicating
significant untapped enzymatic potential. The SSN analysis
based on R domains yielded comparable results (Figure S2).
These findings underscore the significance of our approach in
identifying novel CARs with diverse enzymatic potential.
Numerous uncharacterized families unveiled in our genome
mining provide a good starting point for further biosynthetic
and biocatalytic exploration of CAR enzymes.
We next investigated an uncharacterized CAR family
(Family 4) from Bacillota, which consists of 2,133 proteins
and has significant evolutionary distance from previously
studied CARs belonging to Actinomycetota or Pseudomona-
dota, as shown in the phylogenetic tree of representative CARs
(Figure 2B, Supplementary Data 3). This Bacillota CAR family
had not been previously explored, indicating its potential for
discovering new biosynthetic enzymes or pathways. Although
enzymes within the same family may share similar character-
istics such as substrate preferences, they can still vary. For
instance, NiCAR and MmCAR from Family 1 and PfCAR,
PsCAR, and PeCAR from Family 2 (as depicted in Figures 1A,
S1) exhibit different substrate preferences. To account for
these differences, we reclustered the A domains of enzymes
from Families 1 and 2 (Supplementary Data 2), setting a 65%
sequence identity threshold. This resulted in identifying 20
distinct subfamilies of Family 1, with NiCAR and MmCAR
assigned to separate subfamilies and similar outcomes for
Family 2 (see Figures S3 and S4). We then applied the same
criterion to recluster the Family 4 CARs (Supplementary Data
2) and identified five major subfamilies (Figures 2C and S5).
To gain insight into the biosynthetic capacity of the family, we
selected representative enzymes from each subfamily (BtCAR,
PlCAR, PpCAR, BlCAR, and LfCAR, listed in Table S5) for
biochemical characterization.
2.2. New CARs from Bacillota Biosynthesize Diverse
Natural Products. To investigate the biosynthetic potential
of the five selected CARs, we conducted a comparative
metabolomic analysis to elucidate the chemical diversity of
CAR-synthesized natural products produced by their respec-
tive hosts (Table S5). First, we generated CAR-deletion
mutants in two bacterial strains: Paenibacillus polymyxa ATCC
842, which harbors PpCAR, and Brevibacillus laterosporus
ATCC 9141, which harbors BlCAR. Using liquid-chromatog-
raphy-coupled high-resolution mass spectrometry (LC-
HRMS), we analyzed the metabolic profile of both wild-type
and mutant strains. Our comparative metabolomic analysis
revealed 14 CAR-related products in the wild-type strains, with
compounds 1−6 detected in P. polymyxa and compounds 7−
14 presented in B. laterosporus (Figures 3A, 3B, and 3D). To
gain insight into the chemical diversity of natural products
produced by the five prioritized CARs, we characterized six
compounds (1, 3, 8, 10, 12, and 14) using nuclear magnetic
resonance (NMR) analysis. Previous studies have reported that
CARs can generate imidazole (IDZ),32 pyrazinone (PZN),32
and pyrazine (PZ) heterocycles. Consistent with previous
studies, we identified compounds IDZ-VV (1) (Table S6,
Figures S32−S36) and PZN-VV (3) (Table S7, Figures S37−
S41), which are valine-based imidazole and pyrazinone
analogs, respectively. Additionally, we identified compounds
PZ-AW (12) (Table S10, Figures S53−S54) and PZ-WW (14)
(Table S11, Figures S55−S56) as tryptophan- and alanine-
based pyrazines. While PZ-AW (12) and PZ-WW (14) have
been reported as natural products from Penicillium sp.,54
Cytophaga sp.,55 and B. latersporus,56 the biosynthetic gene(s)
responsible for their production have yet to be elucidated.
Using mass calculations (Supplementary Data 4) and HRMS/
MS analysis, we also identified analogs with the same
heterocyclic skeletons as the NMR-confirmed compounds,
including IDZ-VL/I (2), IDZ-WW (9), PZN-WW (13), PZ-
VV (4), PZ-VL/I (5), and PZ-LL/II (6) (Figures S13, S14 and
Supplementary Data 5).
Compound 10, which was identified as lysiformine,57 was
confirmed to contain a pyridine (PD) ring through 1D and 2D
NMR and thus referred to as PD-WW (Table S9, Figures
S48−S52), with “PD” representing pyridine and “WW”
representing two tryptophan molecules as precursors. Notably,
we identified three compounds (7, 8, and 11) that represent
new N-heterocycles. Compound 8 was also found to share
similar MS/MS fragments with PD-WW (10) and was further
identified as a new N-acetyl glycosylation derivative of PD-
WW (10) via 1D and 2D NMR analysis (Table S8, Figures
S42−S47). As a result, it was given the name G-PD-WW (8).
Additionally, compound 7 was proposed as an analog of PD-
WW (10) derived from alanine and tryptophan and named
PD-AW (Figure S14). Based on HRMS, we detected an
unknown compound, 11, with a molecular formula of
C33H29N5O. However, due to its low concentration, we
could not identify its structure by NMR analysis. Instead, we
identified an analog compound, designated as compound 18,
obtained from the heterologous expression of PpCAR.
Compound 18 showed similar MS/MS patterns to compound
11, as shown in Figure S14 and S15. NMR analysis of

2.4. Exploiting Enzyme Promiscuity of CARs to
Generate a Chemical Library for Bioactivity Screening.
The amino acid substrate versatility of CARs offers the
opportunity for easily generating N-heterocycles harboring
distinct R1 and R2 substituent compositions. We next aimed to
harness the biocatalytic potential of CARs to generate a
chemical library for bioactivity screening (Figure 5A). To
ensure the chemical diversity of our library, we employed a
strategy of heterologous expression of BtCAR and PpCAR.
These two CARs ensured selectivity toward seven different
amino acids for N-heterocycle formation. After successfully
demonstrating the biosynthesis of five different N-heterocycles
from a single amino acid precursor (as presented in Figures 3A,
3B, and 3C), our next step was to investigate the possibility of
generating these skeletons from combinations of two or three
amino acids. We detected five N-heterocycles (PD-FM(39),
PZI-MMF(40), IDZ-FM(41), PZN-FM (42), and PZ-FM-
(43)) based on the combination of two specific amino acids
(Phe and Met), and PZI-MFL/MFI (44) based on the
combination of 3 amino acids (Phe, Met, and Leu/Ile)
(Figures 5B, S25−S26, and Supplementary Data 5, supported
by MS/MS patterns) in B. subtilis JH642::BtCAR. Additionally,
we observed the potential of the CAR enzyme in generating N-
heterocycles from various combinations of seven amino acids,
further supporting the feasibility of CARs in generating diverse
skeletons from combinations of various amino acids. For
example, we successfully detected 28 pyrazine molecules (4-6,
14, 17, 27, 31, 35, and 39-58) (Figures 5C, S27−31, and
Supplementary Data 5, supported by MS/MS patterns) in B.
subtilis JH642::BtCAR and B. subtilis JH642::PpCAR, corre-
sponding to all possible amino acid combinations. Overall, our
findings reveal that through CAR-mediated biosynthesis, the
chemical space of five N-heterocycles can be further expanded
for bioactivity screening, with a theoretical size of 442
molecules (proposing that Val cannot be R2 in the PD
skeleton, R3 in the PZI skeleton, Ile cannot be R2 in PD
skeleton, and Tyr cannot be R1 or R2 in IDZ skeleton) (Figure
5A).
In order to demonstrate the potential of our library, we
conducted a proof-of-principle study by screening the mixture
for potential QS inhibitors. This was based on our assay of
natural products and previous research indicating that N-
heterocycles, such as cyclic dipeptides60−62 or pyrrolones,63
can inhibit QS. Next, we aimed to extend this screening to the
chemical library generated by CARs (Figure 6A). The chemical
library, obtained by combining the crude extracts from B.
subtilis JH642::BtCAR and B. subtilis JH642::PpCAR, were
fractionated into 25 fractions using HPLC, with fractions
collected at 1 min intervals from 15 to 40 min. To further
demonstrate the compound library capacity, the compound
diversity in the each-minute fraction was evaluated using LC-
HRMS analysis, and the identified compounds’ numbers of five
skeletons are depicted in Figure 6B, with Supplementary Data
6 providing a full list of identified compounds. We detected
124 compounds, including 15 pyridine molecules, 50
pyrazinone or imidazole molecules, 24 pyrazine molecules,
and 35 pyrazinium molecules. Sixty percent of the theoretical
size of PD, PZN/IDZ, and PZ skeletons (generated from two
amino acid molecules) could be easily produced and detected,
while the production of PZI (generated from three amino acid
molecules) showed less efficiency (35/294, 12%). The other
compounds not detected in this system may be attributed to
low yields caused by the low selectivity of specific amino acids
or instability or volatility32,64 during the HPLC preparation
process. Additionally, the HPLC profile in Figure 6A-ii verifies
that the chemical library generated through heterologous
expression is not dominated by a small number of high-titer
compounds but rather contains a significant number of
detectable compounds. This feature provides an advantage
and is a prerequisite for applying the chemical library fractions
in prompt bioactivity screening.
We screened the above fractions for bioactivity and
identified the bioactive fractions. Subsequent bioactivity-
guided fractionation led to the identification of four lead
compounds. These compounds were isolated from a 6 L large-
scale fermentation and characterized as pyrazines containing a
benzyl substitute (using phenylalanine as a precursor) (PZ-FF
(17), PZ-FM (43), PZ-FY (47), and PZ-FL (51)), using NMR
(Tables S13−S16, Figures S62−S69) and MS/MS (Figure S25
and S28−S29) analysis. These compounds showed QS
inhibition with IC50 values of 26.8, 7.9, 30.7, and 56.2 μM,
respectively (Figure S9). Interestingly, our findings suggest
that pyrazines with a benzyl substitute have stronger inhibitory
effects on QS than their previously reported analog cyclo-
dipeptides (CDPs).60−62 Molecular docking (Figure S10)
indicated that these compounds might compete with QS
molecules for binding to the QS receptor CviR (PDB 3QP8),
implying their potential to combat other strains that use N-
Acyl homoserine lactone (AHL) as their signaling molecule,
such as Vibrio65 and Pseudomonas.66 Given the protease
inhibition potential demonstrated by amino acid−based N-
heterocycles, including calpain inhibitors PZN-FV and PZN-
YL,67,68 as well as SARS-CoV-2 inhibitor PZ-WW,69 we next
sought to investigate the protease inhibitory activities of
isolated compounds. Our findings revealed that compounds
PZ-WW (14) and PD-WW (10) exhibited inhibitory activities
against SARS-CoV-2 main protease (Mpro/3CLpro),70 with
IC50 values of 65.9 and 38.7 μM, respectively (Figure S11).
Additionally, compound G-PD-WW (8) showed inhibitory
activities against chymotrypsin and trypsin with IC50 values of
98.6 and 145.5 μM (Figure S12). Taken together, we believe
that this chemical library holds great potential for identifying
other biological targets beyond QS inhibition.
While our study highlights the potential of CARs for
accessing the chemical space of N-heterocycles for lead
compound discovery, some limitations need to be addressed.
For instance, the reaction ratio/activity of the biosynthesis of
N-heterocyclic skeletons is still undetermined, making it
difficult to control the production of these compounds and
optimize their yield. Further studies are required to understand
the rearrangements and develop new methods to control and
manipulate biosynthesis. Despite limitations, our study has

3. CONCLUSIONS
Diversity-oriented biosynthesis has emerged as a promising
alternative to traditional chemical synthesis methods for
accessing the vast chemical diversity of N-containing hetero-
cycles. However, the limited substrate selectivity of many
biosynthetic enzymes has hindered their use for the
diversification of N-heterocycles (Tables S2−S3). In this
study, we explore the untapped substrate-tolerant CARs and
propose them as powerful biocatalytic tools for accessing the
chemical space of N-heterocycles. We employed domain-based
genome mining to systematically investigate the untapped
enzymatic potential of CARs in the Bacteria domain, providing
the most comprehensive landscape of CARs to date. Through
in vivo and in vitro assays, we characterized a new family of
CAR enzymes with broad amino acid specificities, including
BtCAR and PpCAR, capable of generating five different N-
heterocycle skeletons. Leveraging this capacity, we next
constructed a chemical library based on BtCAR and PpCAR,
successfully identifying 4 Phe-containing pyrazines with QS
inhibition activity. Our results demonstrate the potential of
CARs for one-step biosynthesis and diversification of N-
heterocycles, enabling efficient access to a wider range of
chemical space and potentially leading to the discovery of
bioactive N-heterocycles. The untapped CARs, particularly
those with substrate promiscuity, offer a significant opportunity
for drug discovery, and our study provides a proof-of-principle
for the use of CARs in accessing the chemical space of N-
heterocycles for bioactive compound discovery. As we
continue to advance our understanding of this CAR-
biosynthesized N-heterocycle library, we are optimistic that
the full potential of it will be unlocked through implementing
more advanced assays.
