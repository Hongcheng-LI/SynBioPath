ABSTRACT: A targeted method for discovering cinnamoyl-
containing nonribosomal peptides (CCNPs), a unique class of
bioactive compounds, was devised by using cinnamoyl isomerase, a
key enzyme in the biosynthesis of the cinnamoyl moiety, as a genome
mining probe. A total of 39 hit strains were obtained, including 35
from polymerase chain reaction-based screening of the in-house
bacterial library (2.5% of 1400 strains) targeting the cinnamoyl
isomerase-encoding gene and 4 from the genome mining of online
databases. Sequence similarity networking and phylogenetic analyses
of the isomerase amplicons (∼530 bp) classified the CCNPs into
three major substructure-based groups (Z-, E-, and M-type CCNPs)
and revealed distinct clade−structure relationships (13 clades). To
overcome the challenge of silent biosynthetic gene clusters, we activated these clusters by overexpressing conserved cluster-situated
LuxR regulators combined with extensive culture optimization. CCNP production was metabolomically detected in the bacterial
extracts by using the characteristic UV absorption and MS/MS fragments of cinnamoyl moieties. CCNP production was observed in
20 of the 39 hit strains, resulting in the isolation of 6 new CCNPs, including oxy-skyllamycin B (2), gwanacinnamycin (3), and
luxocinnamycins A−D (4−7), with high structural novelty. Their structures were elucidated using comprehensive spectroscopic
analyses and multiple-step chemical derivatizations, and the putative biosynthetic pathways were bioinformatically proposed.
Gwanacinnamycin (3) exhibited significant antimycobacterial activity, whereas luxocinnamycin A (4) displayed moderate
antiproliferative activity against stomach cancer cells. Our findings highlight a targeted metabologenomic approach combined with
transcriptional regulator overexpression as a logical and efficient platform for the discovery of bioactive compounds from nature.
■INTRODUCTION
Cinnamoyl-containing nonribosomal peptides (CCNPs) form
a unique class of actinobacterial secondary metabolites that are
biosynthesized by the combined action of highly reducing
(HR) type II polyketide synthase (PKS) and nonribosomal
peptide synthetase (NRPS). This class encompasses a range of
bioactive compounds,1 representatively including WS9326A, a
tachykinin antagonist;2 skyllamycin A, an inhibitor of the
platelet-derived growth factor signaling pathway;3 and
atratumycin, an antitubercular agent (Figure 1A).4 Our
group had also identified several CCNPs including mohanga-
mides,5 WS9326H,6 coprisamides,7,8 nyuzenamide C,9 and
epoxinnamide.10
The biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) of CCNPs
universally contain the following core enzymes: HR type II
ketosynthases (ketosynthase-chain length factor (KS-CLF)
complexes), ketoreductase (KR), dehydratase (DH), acyl
carrier protein (ACP), isomerase (ISO), and a cyclase complex
(CYC) (Figure 1B,C and Table S1).11 Initially, the KS1-CLF1
complex, KR, DH, and ACP catalyze the biosynthesis of triene-
ACP. Subsequently, in conjunction with ISO, further
enzymatic processing yields (2E,4Z,6E)-polyene, which may
terminate with a methyl group or form (8Z)-polyene or (8E)-
polyene depending on the context. Ultimately, the coordinated
action of the KS2-CLF2 complex, KR, DH, and CYC complex
catalyzes the biosynthesis of the cinnamoyl moiety (Figure
1B).11

co-workers performed genome mining using NCBI and JGI

comparison of the wild-type and target gene knockout yielded

Despite the genome mining-based study of kitacinnamycins,
to date, only 18 CCNP families have been reported, mostly by
random chemical searches (Table S2). The limited number of
discovered CCNPs indicates that the potential chemical and
biological diversity of CCNPs is yet to be discovered.
However, a logical strategy for CCNP discovery is still limited:
(1) the gene-structural-type relationship of CCNPs has not
been elucidated, hampering the logical exploration of CCNPs
in nature, even though genome mining for CCNPs relied on
whole-genome data and comprehensive BGC analysis. (2) The
early detection of CCNPs before preparative purification has
not yet been methodologically established. (3) Although
genome mining approaches enable the identification of hit
strains harboring target BGCs, most of these BGCs remain
cryptic, making it challenging to link them to the actual
production of secondary metabolites.
In this study, we classified CCNPs based on their diverse
substructures and employed cinnamoyl ISO, a key enzyme
governing these substructural features, as a genome mining
probe to discover novel CCNPs. We constructed a
phylogenetic tree of CCNPs, showing the clear clade−
structure relationship through a combination of genome
mining, polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-based screening,
sequence similarity networking (SSN), and phylogenetic
analysis of ISO gene amplicon sequences (only ∼530 bp),
which served as a guide for the logical discovery of novel
CCNPs. The detection of CCNPs in bacterial extracts without
preparative purification was achieved in a sensitive way based
on metabolomic hallmarks using ultraviolet (UV) spectroscopy
and mass spectrometry (MS) in liquid chromatography-
tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis. We also
addressed the challenge of cryptic BGCs via genetic
manipulation and comprehensive culture optimization. In
particular, the overexpression of conserved cluster-situated
regulators within target BGCs enabled the production of
structurally novel CCNPs.
Herein, we report a methodological development for the
logical exploration of CCNPs by metabologenomic targeting
and the discovery of six new CCNPs, including their structures,
biosynthetic pathways, and biological activities.

To discover novel CCNPs, we designed primers targeting
the cinnamoyl ISO-encoding genes to screen strains harboring
CCNP BGCs within our in-house actinobacterial library, which
consists of isolates from diverse environments, including
mountain soil, ginseng farm soil, riverine sediments, intertidal
mudflats, and various insect-associated ecosystems. Because
cinnamoyl ISOs belong to the HCCA isomerase/glutathione
S-transferase kappa family (IPR014440), it was necessary to
identify a conserved sequence specific to cinnamoyl isomerases
for their distinction. Most sequences of IPR014440 belong to
the 2-hydroxychromene-2-carboxylate isomerase NahD-like
family (IPR044087), which predominantly originates from
the phylum Pseudomonadota. Detailed sequence analysis of
the isomerases revealed that sequences from the phylum
Actinomycetota possessed highly conserved motifs at residues
6−18 (PRXYFSLRSPYSW) and 176−181 (FGVPXF), which
were absent in NahD-like proteins (Figures S1 and S2). Based
on these conserved motifs, degenerate primers (cinISO-F and
cinISO-R) were designed and used for PCR screening (Figure
S5). A total of 1400 bacterial genomic DNAs were screened
using these primers, resulting in the selection of 35 hit strains
(hit rate, 2.5%). Taxonomic analysis revealed that these strains
comprised 32 Streptomyces and 3 Kitasatospora strains. To
increase the diversity of the hit strains, additional strains
harboring cinnamoyl ISO-encoding genes were selected
through genome mining from online genomic databases and
subsequently obtained. Streptomyces rapamycinicus ATCC
29253 (E-type CCNP-related cluster 6) and Streptomyces
fodineus TW1S1 (M-type CCNP-related cluster 8) were

NPDC044984) were also included in this study.26 A total of
39 PCR amplicon sequences (∼530 bp) were translated into
amino acid sequences and analyzed together with 26 previously
reported cinnamoyl isomerase sequences for phylogenetic tree
construction (Figure S6).
The maximum-likelihood tree constructed using 65
amplicon sequences was divided into three major groups (Z-,
M-, and E-types) based on an 80% bootstrap support
threshold, which was consistent with the SSN results (Figure
3A). Each major group was further subdivided into 3−5 clades
according to branch bootstrap values and the CCNP structures
associated with each clade.
In the largest group Z, clade Z-1 contains the highest
number of strains, including Streptomyces sp. SNM5527 and S.
calvus ATCC 13382,15 which produce the WS9326 family, and
Amycolatopsis cihanbeyliensis DSM 45679, which produces
cihanmycin.20 Both compounds share a 2-((1Z)-pentenyl)-
cinnamoyl moiety backbone, with cihanmycin featuring a cis-
epoxy group formed by the epoxidation of this structure.
Although the peptide skeleton of cihanmycin is more similar to
that of nyuzenamide, its possession of a cis-epoxy group, in
contrast to the trans-epoxy group of nyuzenamide,9 justifies its
classification as a Z-type CCNP, validating the significance of
this study. Clade Z-2 consists of strains that produce
skyllamycin18,28 and oxy-skyllamycin,19 both of which originate
from the 2-((1Z)-propenyl)-cinnamoyl moiety. Clade Z-3
includes strains that produce NC-121 and malacinnamycin,29
both biosynthesized from 2-((1Z,3E)-heptadiene)-cinnamic
acid. Avm15 and Ggm41 belong to clade Z-4 and function as
isomerases in the biosynthesis of atrovimycin30 and grisgeno-
mycin,25 respectively, both of which are derived from 2-
((1Z,3E)-pentadiene)-cinnamic acid) and contain cyclic
decapeptide moieties. The pepticinnamin-producing strains
belong to clade Z-5.16,17,31
In group E, clade E-1 includes strains producing the bicyclic
CCNPs nyuzenamide9,32 and epoxinnamide,10 both of which
contain a trans-epoxy cinnamoyl moiety. The BGC of
kitacinnamycin, an E-type CCNP, contains two isomerases:
Kcn15 (clade E-3) and Kcn33.11 Based on the structural
relevance, Kcn15 was predicted to be crucial for kitacinnamy-
cin biosynthesis.
Group M is further divided into five clades. ISOs belonging
to the BGCs of atratumycin4 and cinnapeptin,22 which include
2-methylcinnamic acid, were assigned to the clade M-1. Clade
M-2 contains the producer of cinnamosyn, a synthetic-
bioinformatic natural product (synBNP),23 along with two
hit strains. Clades M-3, M-4, and M-5 contain no reference
sequences, possibly indicating their potential genetic capacity
to produce structurally new 2-methylcinnamic acid-bearing
peptides.
This phylogenetic tree, for the first time, allows precise
prediction of the chemical substructures of CCNPs based
solely on the approximately 530 bp partial sequences of
cinnamoyl ISO-encoding genes rather than entire BGCs, which
can thereby serve as the genomic hallmarks of CCNPs.
Moreover, this provides the clade−structure relationship for
CCNPs in nature and enables the logical discovery of CCNPs
from numerous strains without whole-genome data.
Production of CCNPs through Culture Optimization
and Overexpression of Cluster-Situated LuxR Regu-
lators. Selected and classified hit strains were cultured and
optimized under diverse conditions using over 20 different
media (Table S5). LC-MS analysis confirmed that these strains
produced compounds identical to those produced by the
reference strains within the same clade, proving the validity of
the clade−structure relationship (Figures 3A and S7).
In clade Z-1, in addition to the WS9326 producer,
Streptomyces sp. SNM55, WS9326A production was also
observed in the four additional strains. In clade Z-2, two
skyllamycin producers and an oxy-skyllamycin producer were
identified. Notably, Streptomyces sp. PMB15, isolated from the
ground beetle Calosoma inquisitor, produced oxy-skyllamycin A
(1) as a minor metabolite and a new derivative as a major
product (Figure S8). In clade Z-3, the Z-type CCNP, NC-1,21
was produced by Streptomyces sp. MA159 from an intertidal
mudflat, the known producer of muanlactam.33 Clade Z-4 did
not have a hit strain that could be addressed by further
chemical study. All five hit strains in clade Z-5 produced
pepticinnamin E possessing a Z-type alkenyl cinnamic acid
(Table S2).
Epoxinnamide, bearing a trans-epoxy cinnamoyl moiety,10
was produced by Streptomyces sp. VNS331 and the rapamycin-
producing strain Streptomyces rapamycinicus ATCC 2925334 in
clade E-1, group E, which was consistent with the clade−
structure relationship (Table S2). Clade E-2 contains only one
hit strain, but it did not produce any compound. We did not
detect any compound from Kitasatospora sp. NDD9, the only
hit, in clade E-3.
In group M, clade M-1 possesses only two reference strains.
Extensive culture optimization enabled the Kitasatospora sp.
GA02 in clade M-3 to produce a detectable compound, which
is possibly a new CCNP that requires further characterization.
However, the other strains in clades M-2, M-4, and M-5, which
were considered potential producers of novel CCNPs, were
silent in various culture media. It indicates that the production
of new CCNPs from the strains belonging to the clades
without reference strains, and thus allegedly with novel
chemistry, was challenging.
According to previous studies, CCNP BGCs commonly
contain cluster-situated LuxR family regulators, and their
overexpression successfully activates cryptic BGCs.25 A
detailed analysis of all reported CCNP BGCs revealed that
most of them contain LuxR family regulators within the cluster
(Table S1), with only two exceptional cases of pepticinnamin
and cihanmycin BGCs lacking homologous LuxR regulators.
Therefore, for the LuxR regulator overexpression, we selected
six hit strains that could potentially produce structurally novel
compounds based on the phylogenetic tree (Figure 3A):
Streptomyces fodineus TW1S1, Streptomyces spp. OID7 and
NPDC021753 (clade M-5), Streptomyces sp. PG17 (clade M-
2); Kitasatospora sp. GA43 (clade M-4); and Streptomyces sp.
NPDC044984 (clade E-2). For each strain, the coding regions
of the two adjacent regulatory genes were amplified by PCR
and cloned into the plasmid pSET152 containing the
constitutive ermE* promoter using the Gibson assembly.
Plasmids carrying LuxR-family regulatory genes were intro-
duced into the wild-type strains by conjugal transfer from
Escherichia coli ET12567/pUZ8002. Overexpression mutants
were selected based on apramycin resistance, and their
genotypes were confirmed by PCR and DNA sequencing.
The mutant strains were subjected to chemical screening under
extensive culture condition optimization (Figure 3B).

Metabolomic Hallmark-Based Identification of
CCNPs. Previously, our group reported spectroscopic
signature-based targeting approaches using nuclear magnetic
resonance (NMR) spectroscopy, which enabled the direct
identification of target compounds (macrolactams, piperazic
acid-containing compounds, and terminal oxazole-bearing
natural products) from crude extracts.33,35,36 However, NMR
is one of the most insensitive spectroscopic methods because it
is ∼1000 times less sensitive than UV spectroscopy. In this
study, we developed a more sensitive detection method using
UV spectroscopy and MS.
The cinnamoyl moiety exhibits a strong absorbance at
approximately 280 nm, yielding a characteristic UV spectrum.
Using the spectrum of authentic 2-methylcinnamic acid and
previously reported CCNPs as references (Figure S9), this
distinctive spectral pattern was employed as a metabolomic
hallmark for CCNP detection. While Z-type and E-type
CCNPs show variable chains containing double bonds
connected to the cinnamic acid moiety, M-type CCNPs
bearing the conserved 2-methylcinnamic acid moiety provide a
reliable MS/MS fragmentation of the b-ion (m/z 145.065) in
LC-MS/MS analysis, which is an unequivocal metabolomic
hallmark.
Wild-type hit strains and LuxR-overexpressed mutant strains
were cultivated in various media on a small scale (200 mL of
culture) and subjected to metabolomic profiling via LC-MS/
MS analysis (Figure 3C). Streptomyces sp. PMB15 in clade Z-2
yielded a major metabolite oxy-skyllamycin B (2) together
with oxy-skyllamycin A (1). The major compound displayed a
UV spectrum (λmax = ∼280 nm) virtually identical to that of 1
and its pseudomolecular ion at m/z 1483, whereas oxy-
skyllamycin A (1) provided an ion at m/z 1497, thus indicating
that the major compound, named oxy-skyllamycin B (2), could
be a new −CH2 (−14 Da) derivative (Figure S8).
In the extract of wild-type Kitasatospora sp. GA02 (clade M-
3) cultivated in GSS broth (Table S5), a major metabolite that
provided the metabolomic hallmarks in the UV (λmax = ∼280
nm) and MS/MS spectra (a distinct fragmentation ion at m/z
145.065) was observed. The compound was later structurally
elucidated and named gwanacinnamycin (3).
In clade M-5, although no metabolites were observed in the
wild-type strains, a common major peak with an identical
molecular ion at m/z 1005 was detected in the mutant strains
of Streptomyces spp. NPDC021753, OID7, and S. fodineus
TW1S1 by overexpressing the cluster-situated LuxR family
regulators. This compound, designated luxocinnamycin A (4),
showed a UV hallmark (λmax = ∼280 nm) and the expected m/
z 145.065 MS/MS hallmark, unequivocally demonstrating that
it is an M-type CCNP.
Moreover, the UV and MS/MS metabolomic hallmarks for a
M-type CCNP, were observed in a LuxR-overexpressed mutant
strain of Streptomyces sp. PG17 in clade M-2. Although it
showed a different mass (molecular ion at m/z 1241) from
cinnamosyn, a synBNP belonging to the same clade,23
unequivocally demonstrating the production of a new M-type
CCNP, this strain was not prioritized because of its low yield,
which was insufficient for further structural characterization.
Therefore, we focused on the large-scale cultivation,
purification, and structure elucidation of the structurally new
oxy-skyllamycin B (2), gwanacinnamycin (3), and luxocinna-
mycin A (4) along with their derivatives, luxocinnamycins B−
D (5−7) (Figure 3D).
Structural Elucidation of New CCNPs. Oxy-skyllamycins
A (1) and B (2) isolated from Streptomyces sp. PMB15 showed
characteristic UV absorption at 280 nm owing to the presence
of a cinnamoyl moiety. 1 and 2 were isolated as white
amorphous powders, and their formulas were assigned as
C75H94N12O21 and C74H92N12O21, respectively, by high-
resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) (Figures S10 and
S11). 1H and 13C chemical shifts of 1 were compared with
previously reported data (Table S8).19 However, the
previously reported NMR data showed slight differences,
because the 13C chemical shifts were imprecisely obtained
solely through a heteronuclear single quantum coherence
(HSQC) NMR spectrum. In contrast, herein, a comprehensive
correlation analysis based on multiplicity-edited HSQC
(eHSQC), correlation spectroscopy (COSY), total correlation
spectroscopy (TOCSY), and heteronuclear multiple bond
correlation (HMBC) NMR experiments was performed
(Figures S22−S25). Based on the analysis, 1 possessed the
same structure as that of previously reported oxy-skyllamycin,
featuring a 10′,11′-epoxy group on the cinnamoyl moiety
attached to the identical peptide chain of skyllamycin A.
Accordingly, 1 was named oxy-skyllamycin A (Figure 4A).
Comparison of the 1H NMR spectra of 1 and 2 revealed that 2
lacked one doublet methyl proton signal compared to that of 1,
which corresponded to the methyl group of the β-
methylaspartic acid in 1. Detailed analyses of eHSQC,
COSY, TOCSY, and HMBC NMR data (Figures S29−S32)
confirmed that 2 contained an aspartic acid residue instead of
β-methylaspartic acid, which is consistent with the peptide
chain of skyllamycin B; therefore, 2 was named oxy-
skyllamycin B (Figure 4A). The vicinal proton−proton
coupling constant between the protons on the epoxy group
was measured to be 4.0 Hz, indicating a cis-epoxy
configuration,37 similar to that observed in compounds with
cis-epoxy such as cihanmycin (3.8 Hz)20 and NC-1 (3.6 Hz).21

Gwanacinnamycin (3) was isolated as a white amorphous
powder from the Kitasatospora sp. GA02 which was derived
from a Gwanak mountain soil sample. The molecular formula
determined by HRMS was C64H88N14O13 (m/z 1261.6721 [M
+ H]+; calcd as 1261.6728) (Figure S12).
Extensive analysis of the 1H, 13C, eHSQC, COSY, TOCSY,
and HMBC NMR spectra (Figures S34−S39 and Table S9)
led to the identification of ten standard amino acid residues:
threonine (Thr), leucine (Leu), valine (Val), alanine (Ala),
glutamine (Gln), glycine (Gly), proline (Pro), arginine (Arg),
and two phenylalanine (Phe) residues. Besides the amino
acids, three-bond HMBC correlations from a downfield singlet
methyl signal (δH 2.31) to carbons C-4′ (δC 133.4), C-8′ (δC
130.2), and C-9′ (δC 136.6), along with correlations from H-6′
(δH 7.14) to C-4′ and C-8′, and from H-5′ (δH 7.74) to C-7′
(δC 128.9) and C-9′, constructed a 1,2-disubstituted benzene
ring. Furthermore, two unassigned protons H-2′ (δH 6.38) and
H-3′ (δH 7.71) exhibited HMBC correlations to the carbonyl
carbon C-1′ (δC 169.5) and nonprotonated sp2 carbon C-4′,
indicating the existence of the 2-methylcinnamic acid (2-
MCA) moiety in 3. This structural feature was corroborated by
UV absorption at λmax = 280 nm and the observation of a
diagnostic MS/MS fragment of 145.065, characteristic for M-
type CCNPs.
The assembly of 2-MCA and amino acid residues in 3 was
elucidated through rotating Overhauser enhancement spec-
troscopy (ROESY) correlations between α-protons and
adjacent amide NH signals (Figure S40). An additional
HMBC resonance between the oxygenated methine H-53
(δH 4.92) of Thr and carbonyl carbon C-1 (δC 170.4) of Phe
(at the C-terminal) indicated the ester bond formation
between these two units, thus defining the cyclic depsipeptide
structure of 3, as depicted in Figure 5. This structure was
supported by MS/MS analysis of the methanolysis product of
3. Finally, bioinformatics prediction using antiSMASH 7.0,38
PRISM 4,39 and SeMPI 2.040 platforms (Figure S92)
reinforced the assignment.
The geometry of the C-2′-C-3′ double bond of 2-MCA was
determined as a trans-orientation based on a large coupling
constant (3JH2′H3′ = 15.5 Hz) and ROESY couplings (H-2′/H-
5′ and H-3′/H3-10′). The absolute configuration of the amino
acid units of 3 was initially determined by complete acid
hydrolysis, followed by Marfey’s method.41 LC-MS analysis of
the D-FDAA derivatization of the hydrolysate and authentic D-
and L-amino acids indicated that 3 contained L-allo-Thr, D-Leu,
L-Val, L-Ala, D-Gln, L-Pro, L-Arg, L-Phe, and D-Phe (Figure
S83). To specifically assign L-Phe and D-Phe units within the
structure, gwanacinnamycin (3) was partially hydrolyzed under
mildly acidic conditions with 4 N HCl:EtOH (1:1) at 65 °C
for 21 h, and the resulting peptide fragments were monitored
by LC-MS. One of the fragment peptides was separated using
reversed-phase LC and identified as 2-MCA-Thr-Phe-Leu-Val-
Ala-COOEt by high-resolution MS/MS (Figure S84).
Subsequent purification, complete acid hydrolysis, and
Marfey’s reaction of this fragment indicated that the Phe
residue was L-configured, thereby assigning the remaining Phe
at the C-terminus as D-configured. The absolute configuration
was consistent with the prediction by the bioinformatic BGC
analysis.
Luxocinnamycins A−D (4−7), isolated from the cultures of
Streptomyces spp. NPDC021753, OID7, and TW1S1, com-
monly exhibited characteristic UV absorption at approximately
280 nm owing to the presence of a cinnamoyl moiety.
Luxocinnamycin A (4) was distinguished by its additional
absorption maxima at 265 and 369 nm, indicating the presence
of additional chromophores in the structure. Its chemical
formula was assigned as C51H56N8O14 (28 degrees of
unsaturation) by HRMS (Figure S16) and 1H and 13C NMR
data (Figures S42 and S43).
A comprehensive analysis of 1H, 13C, and two-dimensional
(2D) NMR data (Figures S44−S47) revealed that 4 contained
serine (Ser), aspartic acid (Asp), two phenylalanines (Phe-1
and Phe-2), threonine (Thr), and glycine (Gly). As predicted
by the classification of the isomerase encoded in the BGC
responsible for luxocinnamycin biosynthesis, 4 possessed a 2-
MCA moiety. In addition to the 2-MCA moiety, 4 contained a
nonproteinogenic amino acid unit, β-hydroxykynurenine (β-
Hky), which provides an additional chromophore with UV
absorption maxima at 265 and 369 nm (see details in the
Supporting Information). To date, a natural product
containing β-Hky has not been reported, and thus,
luxocinnamycin A (4) is the first compound incorporating
this unusual amino acid.
Further detailed analysis of the HMBC correlations enabled
the assembly of the amino acid units, leading to the peptide
sequence Gly-Phe-2-Thr-Phe-1-β-Hky-Asp. Subsequently, the
amide proton 2-NH of Ser (δH 8.02) exhibited an HMBC
correlation to the carbonyl carbon C-1′ of 2-MCA (δC 166.1),
establishing the linkage between 2-MCA and Ser. In addition,
the H-3a of Ser (δH 5.05) showed a correlation to the carbonyl
carbon C-4 of Asp (δC 171.6), indicating that Ser and Asp were
connected by an ester bond. 4 possesses 28 degrees of
unsaturation, accounting for four aromatic rings (4 degrees of
unsaturation each), ten carbonyl groups, one olefinic double
bond, and one macrocycle. The J-resolved HMBC spectrum of
4 confirmed long-range coupling (3JCH) between H-29 of Thr
and C-1 of Ser (Figure S54). Based on these data, the structure
of luxocinnamycin A (4) was determined as a cyclic
depsipeptide featuring two ester bonds and a 2-MCA moiety
(Figure 6).

consistent with a previous J-based configurational analysis,
which indicated the presence of β-erythro-hydroxykynurenine.

Qui17, respectively, which catalyzes the formation of

the quinomycin-type antibiotics.47 In addition, Lxc29 shares a
36% sequence identity with DptJ in the daptomycin BGC,
which functions as a TDO.48 Typically, N-formylkynurenine
produced by TDO is converted to kynurenine by kynurenine
formamidase; however, no clear candidate enzyme for this
conversion is apparent in the lxc cluster. Next, NRPSs Lxc24−

the hexapeptide and luxocinnamycin D (7), resulting in the

for NC-1.21 In this system, a substructure consisting of a

within the BGC remains to be elucidated and requires further
investigation.

■CONCLUSIONS
CCNPs have recently been highlighted, as 15 out of 18 CCNP
families have been reported since 2015. However, because
logical search methods for CCNPs have not been developed,
systematic exploration and expansion of their structural
diversity have been limited. In this study, we developed a
metabologenomic targeting method to identify natural
CCNPs. Using cinnamoyl isomerase, a key enzyme in the
biosynthesis of the cinnamoyl moiety, as a genome mining
probe, we performed bioinformatic analysis through SSN. This
allowed us to classify all CCNPs in nature based on their
isomerase sequences correlated with the cinnamoyl sub-
structures: Z-type (2-((1Z)-alkenyl)-cinnamoyl), E-type (2-
((1E)-alkenyl)-cinnamoyl), and M-type (2-methylcinnamoyl)
CCNPs, depending on the structure of 2-alkyl side chains in
the cinnamoyl moieties.
To select strains harboring CCNP BGCs from the in-house
bacterial library, PCR screening was performed using primers
targeting the conserved sequences of cinnamoyl ISO-encoding
genes, which are the genomic hallmarks of CCNPs. PCR
screening identified 35 hit strains (2.5%) out of 1400 bacterial
strains. The phylogenetic analysis of the ISO gene amplicon
sequences (only ∼530 bp) of 65 strains, including the
reference strains, showed a clear clade−structure relationship
(three groups encompassing 13 clades). It enabled the
prediction of the structural types and novelty of CCNPs and
thus efficiently guided the logical exploration of CCNPs from
numerous strains without whole-genome data.
The early detection of CCNPs is the key to targeted
discovery. The characteristic UV and MS/MS spectroscopic
signatures of cinnamoyl moieties in the LC-MS analysis were
employed as metabolomic hallmarks to rapidly and sensitively
detect the production of targeted CCNPs and circumvented
preparative chromatography in the detection stage.
Cultivation of the hit strains in over 20 different media
confirmed the production of CCNPs. However, the BGCs of
the strains belonging to clades predicted to have high structural
novelty were cryptic. Therefore, using genetic engineering, we
activated cryptic BGCs by overexpressing cluster-situated LuxR
regulators conserved in CCNP BGCs. In combination with the
extensive culture optimization and LuxR overexpression, we
successfully identified the production of CCNPs in 20 of the
39 hit strains, of which the rate (51%) was very high, given that
only 3% of the secondary metabolites encoded by bacterial
BGCs have been experimentally characterized.49
The application of our metabologenomic targeting method
led to the discovery of six CCNPs, including oxy-skyllamycin B
(2), gwanacinnamycin (3), and luxocinnamycins A−D (4−7),
along with the complete NMR assignment of the previously
reported oxy-skyllamycin A (1). Their structures were
elucidated by NMR analysis, and the absolute configurations
were determined by multiple-step chemical derivatizations,
including the advanced Marfey’s method. In particular,
luxocinnamycin A (4) contained the unusual amino acid β-
hydroxykynurenine, which has not been incorporated in a
natural product before and featured a cyclic depsipeptide
formed through a diester bond, exhibiting high structural
novelty. The structure of luxocinnamycin B (5) unprecedent-
edly incorporated the pyrrolo[1,2-d][1,4]benzodiazepine-
dione derived from β-Hky and Asp, providing a novel scaffold.
By the clade−structure relationship-guided targeting of the
clade (M-5), which had unknown chemistry, we successfully
discovered CCNPs with unprecedented structural uniqueness.
The putative biosynthetic pathways of gwanacinnamycin (3)
and luxocinnamycins (4−7) were proposed as NRPS coupled
with HR type II PKS by bioinformatic gene cluster analysis. In
a biological evaluation, gwanacinnamycin (3) exhibited
significant antimycobacterial activity against Mycobacterium
avium and Mycobacterium tuberculosis, indicating a new
antimycobacterial chemotype. Luxocinnamycin A (4) showed
moderate antiproliferative activity against stomach cancer cells.
The discovery of the six new CCNPs validates the
effectiveness of the metabologenomic targeting method in
combination with the overexpression of conserved positive
regulators within the targeted clusters and further induces the
production of targeted compounds by applying extensive
culture optimization. This strategy can also be applied to other
types of BGCs by manipulating cluster-situated regulators
within their clusters. For example, it may be possible to target
secondary metabolites in the macrolide antiparasitic avermec-
tin family by manipulating the conserved positive LAL family
regulators found in their BGCs, polyene macrolides by
targeting the PAS-LuxR family commonly present in their
clusters, or nucleoside antibiotics via the SARP family
regulators encoded in their BGCs.50 Overall, our study
highlights the potential of integrating targeted metabologe-
nomic approaches with genetic manipulation for the logical
exploration of structurally novel and biologically active
compounds.
