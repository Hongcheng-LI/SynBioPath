ABSTRACT: Aminomalonic acid (Ama) is found in various
natural products and protein hydrolysates of multiple organisms,
but the understanding of its biosynthetic origin remains largely
limited. By exploiting a biosynthetic gene cluster for ribosomally
synthesized and post−translationally modified peptides (RiPPs),
which are rich sources of new enzyme chemistry, we identified a
novel two-enzyme pathway for Ama biosynthesis. This biosynthetic
pathway, mediated by an Fe(II)/2-oxoglutarate-dependent oxy-
genase (Fe(II)/2OG), SmaO, and an atypical Fe(II)-dependent
histidine-aspartate (HD) domain enzyme, SmaX, converts
aspartate (Asp) to β-hydroxyaspartic acid (Hya) intermediate
and ultimately to Ama. These tandem enzymatic reactions
hydroxylation of the carbon next to an acid functional group and subsequent four-electron oxidative bond cleavage in α-hydroxy
acidare similar to those associated with other known HD domain oxygenases, PhnZ and TmpB. However, SmaX also exhibits
unique features, such as C−C bond cleavage in α-hydroxycarboxylate using a single Fe cofactor, in contrast to the C−P bond
cleavage using a mixed-valent diiron cofactor in PhnZ and TmpB. Bioinformatic analysis reveals that this two-enzyme cascade may
be present in various biosynthetic pathways for peptide natural products, including RiPPs and nonribosomal peptides (NRPs).
Collectively, our study demonstrates the presence of a novel Ama biosynthetic pathway and suggests its widespread distribution in
peptide natural product biosynthesis.
■INTRODUCTION
Aminomalonic acid (Ama), an analog of malonic acid, is
present in various natural products1−10 as well as protein
hydrolysates of several organisms.11,12 Despite its widespread
detection, Ama biosynthesis has been only partially under-
stood. One well-documented pathway in prokaryotes involves
the oxidation of acyl carrier protein (ACP)-bound serine (Ser-
ACP) to ACP-bound aminomalonate (Ama-ACP).1 Ama-ACP
serves as an extender unit for type I polyketide synthases
(PKSs) to produce zwittermicin,7 guadinomine,8 and col-
ibactin.9,10 The first example of Ama biosynthesis in RiPP
pathways and on peptides involves the overoxidation of
peptidyl serine by radical S-adenosylmethionine (rSAM)
enzymes during cyclophane biosynthesis.2 However, Ama
was observed as only a minor product en route to cyclophane,
suggesting that the Ama-containing peptide is a shunt product
in this pathway. α-Carboxylation of aspartate by a vitamin K-
dependent carboxylase (VKDC)-related enzyme, MloH,
produces an aminomalonic acid moiety in malonomycin,
where the Ama Cα is further derivatized.4 Ama is used as a
substrate in the biosynthesis of sphingolipid inhibitors
sphingofungins, lumiquinone A, and antibiotic GE23077, but
its biosynthetic origin is unknown.3,5,6
Ribosomally synthesized and post-translationally modified
peptides (RiPPs) are a large group of peptide-based natural
products.13 RiPP biosynthesis involves two main steps:
ribosomal synthesis of a precursor peptide and subsequent
structural modifications by various enzymes. A large number of
post-translational modifications (PTMs) in RiPPs have been
identified, with ongoing expansion in their structural and
biosynthetic diversity.14,15 By using genetically encoded
precursor peptides as substrates for PTM enzymes, the RiPP
biosynthetic pathways significantly facilitate the discovery of
novel enzymatic transformations, where substrate identification
often becomes a bottleneck.14 Characterization of enzymes
that are homologous but distantly related to known RiPP
biosynthetic enzymes has successfully revealed unprecedented
modification reactions16,17 and novel linkage formations.18−23
Studies of secondary modification enzymes in RiPP bio-

In this study, we characterized two Fe-dependent enzymes,
SmaO and SmaX, encoded in a putative group 18 graspetide
BGC,24 where ATP-grasp macrocyclases install class-defining
ω-ester or ω-amide linkages.24−26 We found that SmaO and
SmaX form a novel enzyme cascade to produce Ama from
aspartate (Asp) via β-hydroxyaspartic acid (Hya) intermediate.
SmaO functions as a typical Fe(II)/2-oxoglutarate-dependent
oxygenase (Fe(II)/2OG) to hydroxylate β-carbon of two Asp
residues. SmaX is, however, an atypical histidine-aspartate
(HD) domain enzyme carrying only a single Fe cofactor and
cleaves the Cβ−Cγ bond in two Hya residues to produce Ama.
Bioinformatic analysis suggests that this tandem enzymatic
transformation is present in biosynthetic pathways for several
known or uncharacterized RiPP subclasses and, more
interestingly, nonribosomal peptides (NRPs). Our findings
uncover a novel Ama biosynthetic pathway and establish its
versatility in peptide natural product biosynthesis.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
SmaO and SmaX Cooperatively Modify a Precursor
Peptide in a Strict Order. The BGC of our interest contains
genes encoding a precursor (SmaA), an ATP-grasp enzyme
(SmaB)s, and several uncharacterized proteins including SmaO
and SmaX (Figure 1A and Table S1). The precursor peptide,
SmaA, displays a canonical double glycine motif (GG motif),
which is known to be cleaved for core peptide secretion by
peptidase-containing ATP-binding cassette transporter
(PCAT), further supporting that this BGC generates a RiPP
(Figure 1B). The HHPred27,28 search for SmaO homologues
returned ten distinct protein domains, of which four were
indicated as Fe(II)/2OG (Table S2). The same analysis for
SmaX homologues only showed two proteins: DUF6817
(PF20680) and HD_4 (PF13328; Table S3). Using the
AlphaFold329−predicted SmaX structure as an input in the
DALI server,30 which searches for structurally similar proteins,
we obtained an output in which all the top 10 proteins belong
to the HD domain protein superfamily (Table S4). While HD
domain proteins (IPR003607) are known to mediate highly
diverse reactions using various metal ions as cofactors, none of
them are known to be involved in RiPP biosynthesis.31 Thus,
we assumed that SmaX mediates a novel modification reaction
on a precursor in a pathway similar to, yet distinct from, HD
domain enzymes.
To test the reactivities of SmaO and SmaX, we constructed a
gene encoding SmaA fused to Escherichia coli maltose binding
protein (MBP) with an intervening TEV protease cleavage site
and heterologously coexpressed it with or without SmaO or
SmaX in E. coli. We analyzed TEV protease-cleaved SmaA or
its modified variants with matrix-assisted laser desorption
ionization-time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF-
MS). SmaA coexpressed with SmaO, hereafter referred to as
SmaA(O), showed a mass change of +32 Da from SmaA,
suggesting SmaO doubly hydroxylates SmaA (Figure 1C).
Although SmaA coexpressed with SmaX, SmaA(X), did not
show any mass change, SmaA coexpressed with both SmaO
and SmaX, SmaA(OX), displayed −148 Da change from
SmaA(O) in the MALDI-TOF-MS analysis (Figure 1C).
These results indicate that both SmaO and SmaX function as
modification enzymes in a sequential manner, where SmaX
requires SmaO-modified SmaA, SmaA(O), as a substrate.
SmaO is an Fe(II)/2-Oxoglutarate-Dependent Aspar-
tate Hydroxylase. To further characterize the SmaO-
mediated reaction, we used heated electrospray ionization-
high resolution mass spectroscopy (H-ESI-HRMS). SmaA(O)
was initially cleaved with the LahT150 peptidase,32 which
recognizes and cuts after the “GG” motif, resulting in the core
fragment, SmaA(O)31−57 (Figure 1C). The tandem mass
spectrometry (MS/MS) analysis showed that SmaA(O)31−57
presented the +32 Da mass shift at the N-terminal
“D31GD33G” sequence (Figures 1C and 2A). We also tested
the SmaO modification of the shorter SmaA variants
SmaA16−57(O), SmaA28−57(O), and SmaA30−38(O)and
found the same +32 Da mass change (Figure S1). In particular,
SmaA28−57(O) and SmaA30−38(O) data also showed that the
+32 Da change was localized in the “DGDG” motif, indicating
that the SmaO reaction requires only the N-terminal eight
residues in the core peptide (Figure S2). The in vitro
reconstitution of the SmaO-mediated reaction confirmed that
SmaO induced +32 Da mass change on SmaA28−57 only in the
presence of both 2-oxoglutarate (2OG) and Fe2+ (∼97%
conversion, based on peak heights; Figure 2B). Considering
that several aspartyl β-hydroxylases are known in RiPP
biosynthesis,33−36 we speculated that SmaO hydroxylates at
β-carbons of Asp31 and Asp33. The kinetic parameters for the
SmaO-mediated hydroxylation of a single Asp residue in
SmaA28−57 were kcat = 0.09 ± 0.02 s−1 and KM = 300 ± 100
μM (Figure S3).
To obtain more information about the SmaA(O) structure,
we conducted various nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR)
spectroscopic analyses. We used a truncated product,
SmaA30−38(O) (1) for NMR analyses (Figures S4−S11 and
Table S5).

Collectively, we have shown that SmaO is an Fe(II)/2OG-
dependent oxygenase that hydroxylates the β-carbons of Asp31
and Asp33 in the “DGDG” core sequence of SmaA with high
specificity.
SmaX is a Novel Fe-Dependent Enzyme that
Generates Aminomalonic Acid from β-Hydroxyaspartic
Acid. Next, we characterized SmaX and, based on the
following evidence, suggest that SmaX is a novel Fe-dependent
enzyme that produces Ama by oxidatively cleaving the Cβ−Cγ
bond in the two Hya residues of SmaA(O) (Figure 3A).
First, in contrast to the −148 Da mass change of SmaA(OX)
from SmaA(O) in MALDI-TOF-MS, H-ESI-HRMS analysis
resulted in only −60 Da mass change of SmaA(OX)31−57 from
SmaA(O),31−57 indicating a loss of C2H4O2 (Figure 1C). The
−60 Da change was also observed with shorter SmaA variants
upon coexpression with SmaO and SmaX, SmaA28−57(OX)
and SmaA30−38(OX) (Figure S2). This 88 Da mass difference
between two different ionization methods is reminiscent of the
collision-induced dissociation (CID)-mediated decarboxyla-
tion of Ama in HR-MS/MS, reported by Qi Zhang group.2
Our results are consistent with the SmaX-mediated generation
of two Ama residues from two Hya residues (loss of C2H4O2),
and the decarboxylation of two Ama residues in the high-
energy ionization/fragmentation methods (Figure 3A).
Accordingly, we also observed that SmaA(OX)31−57 and the
two shorter variants, SmaA28−57(OX) and SmaA30−38(OX),
presented a species doubly decarboxylated between Asp31 and
Gly34 in HR-MS/MS (Figures 3C and S2).
Second, homologous HD domain-containing oxygenases
show similar oxidative bond cleavage reactions involving α-
hydroxy acids. The HD domain protein superfamily consists of
a large number of metalloproteins harboring a histidine−
aspartate (HD) dyad that coordinates various transition metal
ions, such as Fe, Co, Mn, Mg, Ni, and Zn.31 Although they can
function as oxygenase, phosphatase, phosphodiesterase, and
lyase, only three Fe-utilizing HD domain oxygenases are
known so far; MIOX(myo-inositol oxygenase), PhnZ, and
TmpB (Figure S13A−C). Interestingly, PhnZ and TmpB
mediate oxidative C−P bond cleavage in α-hydroxyphospho-
nate and are coupled with preceding enzymes, PhnY and
TmpA, respectively, which belong to the Fe(II)/2OG family
and introduce a hydroxyl group at α-carbon (Figures 3A and
S13D).39,40 The sequential activities of Fe(II)/2OG and HD
domain oxygenase in the PhnY−PhnZ and TmpA−TmpB
pairs are very similar to the suggested reaction pathway of
SmaO−SmaX (Figure 3A). However, SmaX cleaves a C−C
bond, instead of a C−P bond, suggesting that SmaX represents
a distinct HD domain oxygenase (Figure 3A).
To confirm that SmaX is an Fe-dependent metalloenzyme,
we reconstituted the in vitro reaction with various metal ions.
Purified SmaX was treated with EDTA to remove residual
metal ions, and further purified through gel filtration. Out of
nine tested metal ions (Fe2+, Fe3+, Co2+, Mn2+, Ni2+, Ca2+,
Cu2+, Zn2+, and Mg2+), only Fe2+despite potential partial
oxidation to Fe3+ in airshowed significant reactivity (∼97%
conversion, based on peak heights); the others exhibited no
detectable product peaks (Figure 3B). While 1,4-dithiothreitol
(DTT) was used in the reaction solution to maintain Fe2+ and
the enzyme in a reduced form, the removal of DTT made Fe3+
partially active as a cofactor (Figure S14). Although further
mechanistic studies may be needed to understand the activity
recovery with Fe3+, these results demonstrate that SmaX is
fundamentally an Fe(II)-dependent enzyme. The kinetic
parameters for the SmaX-mediated reaction of a single Hya
residue in SmaA28−57(O) were kcat = 0.93 ± 0.23 s−1 and KM =
140 ± 86 μM, which are 10-fold lower and similar to those of
PhnZ, respectively (kcat = 11 ± 1 s−1 and KM = 170 ± 50
μM)44 (Figure S14).

SmaX is an HD Domain Enzyme with Unique
Features. While SmaX displays similar reactivity with PhnZ
and TmpB, the comparison of their structures (SmaX,
AlphaFold3-predicted; PhnZ and TmpB, experimentally
obtained) and sequences differentiates SmaX as an atypical
HD domain enzyme. Previously reported HD domain
oxygenasesPhnZ, TmpB, and MIOXcontain a mixed-
valent Fe(II)/Fe(III) cofactor for four-electron oxidative bond
cleavage, defining the archetypal mixed-valent diiron oxygenase
(MVDO).40,42,43 Typically, the conserved four His residues
and two Asp residues coordinate two Fe ions, which bind to a
substrate and O2, as shown in the PhnZ structure (Figures
4A,B, and S13B).40,44,45 The AlphaFold329-predicted structure
of SmaX presented a similar core domain to PhnZ with higher
variance in some peripheral regions (root-mean-square
deviation of 112 Cα atoms, 3.1 Å; Figure 4A). Interestingly,
the AlphaFold3-predicted SmaX structure revealed only two
His residues (His39 and His63) and one Asn residue
(Asn143), instead of Asp, for the coordination of one Fe ion
(Figure 4C). No amino acids commonly known to coordinate
to metal ions46,47 were found near the SmaX sequence aligned
with the PhnZ residues coordinating to the second Fe cofactor
(Figure S22) or near the SmaX site in the predicted 3D
structure, superimposed with the second Fe cofactor in PhnZ
(Figure S23). These analyses suggest that, in contrast to the
other three HD domain oxygenases, SmaX contains only a
single Fe ion. Indeed, the quantitative Ferene assay48 of the
anaerobically reconstituted49,50 SmaX indicated that the
enzyme has 0.8 ± 0.1 equiv of Fe, further suggesting that
SmaX has only one Fe ion in its active site (Figure S24).
To identify active site residues critical for SmaX activity, we
selected seven residues for mutational studies that were
predicted to bind to either the Fe cofactor (H39, H63, and
N143) or the substrate (H31, T68, R104, and Q147), based
on the AlphaFold3-predicted structure of the SmaA−SmaX
complex (Figures 4D and S25). The 1 h in vitro reactions with
SmaX mutant variants showed very low (H39A, ∼3%
conversion to a singly modified product) or almost no
conversion of substrates (no detectable products), while that
of wild-type SmaX displayed high conversions (∼93%; Figure
4D). Extended reactions (20 h) with SmaX variants yielded
only singly modified products, with overall conversion of 0
∼25% (Figure S26). The His80-to-alanine mutant of PhnZ,
which coordinates with Fe, retains approximately 10% of the
wild-type activity.44 Circular dichroism analyses of WT and six
variants indicated no major conformational changes (Figure
S26). These results suggest that these seven residues are
important for the SmaX activity.

coordination, MetalPDB indicates that only 0.7% (23 out of
3510) of Fe-binding ligands in representative Minimal
Functional Sites (MFSs) acquired from the 3D structures in
Protein Data Bank (PDB) are Asn.47 Among the 18
representative structures, only one, Bd1817, belongs to the
HD domain protein family.51 In Bd1817, Asn265 coordinates
the bimetal center, but the HD dyad is still present as His183
and Asp184. Therefore, to our knowledge, SmaX is the first
HD domain protein lacking the Asp metal-coordinating
residue. Collectively, we suggest that SmaX represents an
atypical HD domain enzyme where a single Fe cofactor
activates both molecular oxygen and the substrate Hya residue.
Homologous Two-Enzyme Cascade is Prevalent in
Other Biosynthetic Pathways. Secondary modification
enzymes are often shared among unrelated RiPP subclasses.52
Therefore, we envisioned that the oxidative conversion of Asp
to Ama involving two iron-dependent enzymes might be
utilized in the biosynthesis of various peptide natural products.
To explore the evolutionary distribution of the SmaO−SmaX
pairs, we first retrieved SmaX homologues through Position-
Specific Iterative Basic Local Alignment Search Tool53 (PSI-
BLAST). PSI-BLAST10 was conducted on February 2024
against nonredundant protein database on NCBI. Next, we
assessed if any of the neighboring genes encode Fe(II)/2OG
enzymes. We also searched for known RiPP primary
modification enzymes or PCAT54,55 encoded nearby. We
identified 43 putative BGCs that are expected to produce
RiPPs in known subclasses, including graspetides, lanthipep-
tides, triceptides, linear azol(in)e-containing peptides (LAPs),
and thioamitides (Figure 5A−E). We also found 24 putative
RiPP BGCs encoding a putative GG motif-containing
precursor and a PCAT, but not any known RiPP class-defining
modification enzymes (Figure 5F). Thus, we suggest that these
BGCs may either have novel types of class-defining enzymes or
use the SmaO−SmaX pair as class-defining enzymes.
Some BGCs encode Fe(II)/2OG enzymes that are
annotated as other known Fe(II)/2OG Asp/Asn hydroxylases,
YhhC34 or CinX36 (Figure 5C,E; Experimental Section in
Supporting Information). Although many putative precursors
contain aspartates in glycine-rich regions, suggesting that these
residues may be modified, precursors associated with YhhC or
CinX lack “GD” or “DG” motifs, indicating that they recognize
different substrate sequences. In some cases, the SmaO and
SmaX homologues exist as a single fusion protein (Figure
5C,F), further supporting that SmaO and SmaX function in the
two-step cascade reactions.
Intriguingly, we identified 52 BGCs that encode SmaX
homologues, nonribosomal peptide synthetases (NRPSs)
featuring the Asn/Asp adenylation domain, and TauD-like
Fe(II)/2OG oxygenases (Figures 5G and S27).56 This result
strongly suggests that the similar enzymatic pathway for Asp-
to-Ama conversion is also utilized in NRP biosynthesis,
although it is unclear whether the conversion occurs in an
amino acid or in a part of a peptide substrate. Collectively,
these bioinformatic analyses suggest that the Asp-to-Ama
conversion pathway is widespread among RiPP and NRP
biosynthesis.
■CONCLUSION
By dissecting two enzymes found in a putative graspetide BGC,
we identified a novel biosynthetic pathway for Ama. Although
the two known pathways for Ama biosynthesis involve Ser
oxidationeither in a Ser−ACP form1 or in a ribosomal
peptide2our biosynthetic pathway entails β-hydroxylation of
Asp followed by oxidative cleavage of the Cβ−Cγ bond in Hya.
This two-step enzyme cascade results in the removal of a single
hydrocarbon chain (CH2) from Asp. Contrary to the first
report of the Ama production as a minor product in a
ribosomal peptide,2 our study reveals a genuine Ama
biosynthetic pathway in RiPPs as a major product. Our

bioinformatic analysis also indicates that these tandem

RiPPs and, more interestingly, NRPs. Previously, the genes

NRPS-encoding genes, suggesting that Ama−ACP is also used

may represent an alternative pathway for Ama production in

Our study also reports a novel Fe(II)-dependent HD
domain enzyme, SmaX, involved in C−C bond cleavage.
Previously known HD domain oxygenases, PhnZ, TmpB, and
MIOX, use a mixed-valent Fe(II)/Fe(III) cofactor to cleave
the C−P bond in α-hydroxyphosphonate or the C−C bond in
vicinal diol. In particular, PhnZ and TmpB form a two-enzyme
cascade for hydroxylation and oxidative bond cleavage. SmaX
also features a domain homologous to HD domain proteins
and mediates Fe-dependent oxidative bond cleavage in the
second step of the two-enzyme cascade. However, SmaX
differs by using only a single Fe(II) cofactor and cleaving the
C−C bond in α-hydroxycarboxylate. Although additional
structural and mechanistic studies are needed to understand
the SmaX catalysis, the four-electron oxidative C−C bond
cleavage in an α-hydroxy carbonyl group with a single Fe
cofactor is reminiscent of the reactions catalyzed by NovR/
CloR-like nonheme iron oxygenase TqaM58 and 2,4′-
dihydroxyaceptophenone dioxygenase (DAD),59,60 although
SmaX displays a different protein domain.
Remaining questions include how conserved the two-
enzyme cascade reactions are beyond what is shown in Figure
5, and whether they are involved in biosynthesis of novel RiPP
subclasses. Additional characterization of various SmaX
homologue-containing BGCs, which is currently underway in
our laboratory, will enhance the understanding of the diverse
SmaX-associated biosynthetic pathways and their products.
Further studies, including experimental structure determina-
tion and various spectroscopic characterizations, are also
needed to understand the mechanism of SmaX. Our study
paves the way for new enzyme chemistry associated with RiPP
biosynthesis.
