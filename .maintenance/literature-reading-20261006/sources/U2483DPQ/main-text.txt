Enzymatic Stereoselective Nucleophilic Cyclization Governs Atypical
Spirotetronate Assembly in Lucensimycin A Biosynthesis

ABSTRACT: Lucensimycin A is a structurally unique spirotetronate
polyketide featuring a rare spiro[tetronate-hydrophenanthrene] tetracyclic
core, distinct from the classical spiro[tetronate-cyclohexene] scaffolds
formed via intramolecular Diels−Alder (IMDA) cyclizations. Here, we
identified and characterized the luc biosynthetic gene cluster from
Streptomyces fagopyri NAX0062, revealing a divergent biosynthetic logic.
The pathway begins with type I PKS assembly of a linear polyketide,
followed by tetronate ring formation by a canonical tetronate cassette. A
flavin-dependent Diels−Alderase (LucM) then catalyzes an IMDA reaction
to form a decalin intermediate. Unusually, the Diels−Alderase homologue
LucK catalyzes a stereoselective intramolecular nucleophilic cyclization
rather than a pericyclic reactionto generate the spiro[tetronate-hydro-
phenanthrene] core, following acetylation by LucN. Oxidative cleavage of a
terminal alkene (by LucO3) completes the pathway. Structural and mutational analysis of LucK revealed that Glu16 and Glu85
function as general acid/base catalysts to drive the nucleophilic cyclization reaction, highlighting LucK as a mechanistically distinct
cyclase. This work uncovers a previously unrecognized enzymatic strategy for spirocyclic construction and expands the catalytic
repertoire of β-barrel enzymes in polyketide biosynthesis.
■INTRODUCTION
Tetronate antibiotics represent a prominent class of bacterial
polyketide natural products characterized by the presence of a
tetronic acid moiety.1,2 These compounds exhibit a broad
spectrum of potent biological activities, including antimicrobial,
antiviral, and anticancer effects, which have garnered consid-
erable scientific interest.3 Among them, spirotetronatessuch
as abyssomicin, chlorothricin, kijanimicin, and lobophorinare
distinguished by their macrocyclic structures formed through a
spirocyclic linkage that connects a cyclohexene ring to a
tetronate segment (Figure 1A).3−6
The biosynthesis of spirotetronates typically begins with the
assembly of a linear polyketide chain containing a terminal
tetronate unit, catalyzed by Type I polyketide synthases (PKSs).
Following dehydration, this linear intermediate undergoes
enzymatic intramolecular Diels−Alder (IMDA) reactions,
which are critical for constructing the complex cyclic frame-
works characteristic of these molecules. For example, in
abyssomicin biosynthesis, AbyU catalyzes an IMDA reaction
to form the characteristic spirotetronate unit (Figure S1A).7 In
the case of the larger macrocyclic spirotetronate chlorothricin,
two sequential Diels−Alder (DA) enzymes are involved. First,
the linear polyketide is cyclized via an IMDA reaction catalyzed
by ChlE3 to produce a decalin ring; followed by ChlLa
homologue of AbyUwhich promotes a second DA cyclization
to form the spiro-center (Figure S1B).8,9 Similar biosynthetic
logic is observed in spirotetramates, nitrogen-containing analogs
of spirotetronates, such as pyrroindolmycin.9
However, variations from this conserved biosynthetic path-
way are evident in the biosynthesis of tetronasin, a linear
polyether tetronate (Figure 1A).10,11 Although its gene cluster
shows high homology to the chlorothricin pathway (Figure 1B),
the enzymatic transformations differ significantly. The enzymes
Tsn11 and Tsn15homologous enzymes to ChlE3 and ChlL
catalyze reactions that diverge from classical Diels−Alder
mechanisms. Specifically, Tsn11 mediates a hetero-Diels−
Alder reaction, while Tsn15 likely facilitates a pericyclic
rearrangement rather than a typical [4 + 2] cycloaddition
(Figure S1C).10 These findings underline the evolutionary
plasticity of homologous biosynthetic enzymes and their
capacity to adopt novel catalytic roles that generate structural
and chemical diversity.

In this study, we elucidate the complete biosynthetic pathway
of the atypical tetronate polyketide, lucensimycin A. Despite
sharing high genetic similarity with the chlorothricin gene
cluster, the involved Diels−Alderase enzyme, LucK, operates via
a fundamentally different mechanism. Instead of catalyzing a
classical pericyclic cycloaddition, LucK facilitates a stereo-
selective nucleophilic substitution, leading to the formation of a
distinctive four-ring spirocyclic core interconnected by a
tetronate and a highly hydrogenated phenanthrene moiety.
Structural analysis of LucK combined with site-directed
mutagenesis pinpointed key catalytic residues, providing new
insights into its enzymatic mechanism and emphasizing the
remarkable diversity of catalytic strategies employed in tetronate
biosynthesis.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Identification of Gene Cluster for Lucensimycin

B. Singh and colleagues at Merck from Streptomyces through
bioassay-guided screening.12−14 We reisolated lucensimycin A
(1) and lucensimycin C (2) from the fermentation broth of S.
fagopyri NAX0062 (Figure 1A, Tables S5−S6). Interestingly, 1
spontaneously hydrolyzed in aqueous solution at room
temperature, gradually converting into 2 (Figure S2), suggesting
that 1 is likely the authentic natural product encoded by the gene

Despite the skeletal differences, the identified luc gene cluster
shares high sequence homology with those of known
spirotetronates such as chl, kij, and lob (Figure 1B). It contains
all the conserved genes required for assembling the character-
istic pentacyclic structure typical of spirotetronates, including
type I polyketide synthases (PKS; LucA−E), a tetronate
formation cassette (LucL1−L5), a Diels−Alderase enzyme
(LucM) for decalin ring formation, and a spirotetronate-specific
Diels−Alderase (LucK) (Table S4). To verify whether this gene
cluster directs lucensimycin biosynthesis, we performed gene
knockout experiments targeting lucA. The resulting lucA mutant
abolished production of 1 and 2, confirming that the gene cluster
is essential for lucensimycin biosynthesis (Figure 2A, trace (ii).
LucL1-LucL5 Coordinate the Tetronate Ring Forma-
tion. To investigate whether specific modifications occur during
early biosynthesisparticularly in the formation of the tetronate
ringthat could lead to the final atypical spirotetronate
structure, we systematically knocked out each of the five genes
(lucL1−L5) involved in tetronate formation. Deletion of lucL1
resulted in a complete loss of the final product 1 (Figure 2A,
trace iii), while individual knockouts of lucL5 and lucL2 led to
accumulation of compounds 3 and 4, respectively (Figure 2A,
trace iv-v). Analysis by NMR and MS revealed that 3 is a linear
polyketide featuring all trans-double bond and a terminal γ-
hydroxymethyl-tetronate group, consistent with the collinearity
rule observed in Type I PKSs, and is most likely the nascent
product of the PKS assembly line (Table S7, Figure S3). 4, which
has a molecular weight 42 Da higher than 3, was confirmed as its
acetylated derivative (Table S8), indicating that LucL5
functions upstream of LucL2 by catalyzing the acetylation of
the tetronate hydroxyl group.
Furthermore, we heterologously expressed and purified
LucL2 in Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3). Co-incubation of 4
with LucL2 yielded a new product 5, which is 60 Da less than 4
(Figure 2B, trace iii). Structural analysis revealed that 5 features
an exocyclic double bond at the tetronate moiety, resulting from
the elimination of an acetyl group (Table S9). Based on both
bioinformatic predictions and experimental data, we conclude
that the biosynthesis of the tetronate moiety in 1 proceeds via
the classical pathway characteristic of tetronate natural
products.15−17
LucM Catalyzes the Trans-Decalin Ring Formation. We
next examined the roles of LucM and LucK, which show
homology to known tandem Diels−Alderases PyrE3 (39%/
100% identity/coverage) and PyrI4 (22%/62% identity/cover-
age) in pyrroindolmycin biosynthesis, respectively.9 Gene
knockouts of lucM and lucK abolished the production of 1,
demonstrating their essential roles in biosynthesis. The ΔlucM
mutant accumulated the linear precursor 5 (Figure 2A, trace vi).
To confirm LucM’s function, we heterologously expressed and
purified the enzyme in E. coli BL21 (DE3). The bright yellow
enzyme, which is confirmed to contain FAD by LC-MS (Figure
S6), catalyzed the conversion of 5 to a new product, 6, without
the need for additional cofactors (Figure 2B, trace v). Structural
elucidation of 6 indicated it is a bicyclic intermediate featuring a
trans-decalin system, consistent with an endo- selective DA
reaction (Table S10). These results strongly suggest that, similar
to PyrE3 in pyrroindolmycin biosynthesis, LucM promotes an
endo- IMDA reaction on the linear precursor, facilitating the

LucK Constructs the Atypical Spiro Tetracyclic
Scaffold. Notably, the ΔlucK mutant strain led to the
production of three new metabolites 7, 8, and 8′ (Figure 2A,
trace viii). Large-scale fermentation of the ΔlucK strain allowed
us to isolate these metabolites, which were characterized by LC-
MS and NMR. 7 was elucidated as a 2-hydroxy acetylated
derivative of 6 (Table S11). Both 8 and 8′ exhibited identical
molecular weights, corresponding to an 18 Da reduction
compared to 7. Comprehensive NMR characterization revealed
that 8 and 8′ share the same planar structure featuring an
atypical spirotetronate frameworka structural motif consis-
tent with the final biosynthetic product 1 (Tables S12−S13).
To elucidate the stereochemical configurations of 8 and 8′, we
performed NOE analysis. In 8, distinct NOE correlations were
observed between the methyl group Me-30 and protons H-2/H-
4, as well as between H-10 and H-2/H-4. The spatial proximity
was further supported by a NOE correlation between H-3 and
H-5. Further NOE analysis was identified between Me-31 and
H-5/H-8, and between H-8 and H-13. These NOE observations
collectively demonstrate that Me-30, H-2, H-4, and H-10
occupy one face of the cyclic system, while H-3, H-5, Me-31, H-
8, and H-13 reside on the opposite face (Table S12, Figure S76).
Intriguingly, 8′ exhibited a similar NOE correlation pattern to
that of 8 (Figure S83), strongly suggesting that the structural
difference between the two compounds most likely lies at the
quaternary carbon center C-12.
Since the protons adjacent to C-12 are absent, direct NOE
analysis cannot reliably determine its stereochemistry, and
efforts to obtain suitable crystals for X-ray diffraction have failed.
Given that the terminal alkene tetronate part can function as an
effective Michael acceptor, we hypothesized that compounds 8
and 8′, upon reaction with N-acetylcysteamine (SNAC), would
yield four possible stereoisomers: 12R,25R; 12R,25S; 12S,25S;
12S,25R. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations revealed
that only the 12R,25S isomer exhibits a close H-1/H-25 distance
(∼2.5 Å), consistent with an NOE signal, while other isomers
have distances exceeding 4 Å, showing no NOE (Figure S7A).
Guided by this prediction, we derivatized 8 and 8′ with SNAC,
separated the products, and analyzed their NOESY spectra
(Tables S19−S22). The derivative from 8 displayed a dignostic
NOE correlation of H-1 and H-25, confirming its 12R
stereochemistry (Figure S7B). In contrast, the derivative from
8′ lacked this NOE, indicating the 12S configuration.
Feeding experiments with compounds 8 and 8′ in the ΔlucA
mutant strain demonstrated that 8 exclusively rescued the
biosynthesis of 1(Figure S8). This result unequivocally identifies
8 as a bona fide biosynthetic intermediate and establishes
stereochemical congruence between 1 and 8 (Tables S5, S12).
Our stereochemical reassignment of 1 raises a key discrepancy
with the previously reported structure, particularly in the relative
configurations at C-8 and C-13.12 This misassignment likely
arises from the nearly identical chemical shifts of H-8 and H-14,
which led to the misinterpretation of the actural H-14 and H-10
correlation as an H-8 and H-10 correlation in previous report
(Figure S9).12 In addition, the configuration proposed in the
earlier report is inconsistent with the biosynthetic logic
underlying the formation of the decalin ring. Specifically, the
cyclohexene moiety in 1 originates from an enzyme-catalyzed
IMDA reaction (Figure S10). The substrate for this step,
compound 5, has been isolated and confirmed to feature an all-
trans alkene system (Table S9, Figure S54). Mechanistically, the
IMDA reaction proceeds via a suprafacial−suprafacial overlap
between the HOMO of the trans-diene and the LUMO of the
trans-configured dienophile, leading to a stereospecific syn
cycloaddition.18 As a result, the substituents at the newly formed
stereocenters, C-5 and C-8, should reside on the same face of the
ring. Our revised structure of 1guided by experimental NOE
datashows that H-5 and H-8 adopt the same orientation, fully
consistent with the stereochemical outcome expected from an
IMDA reaction and supporting a biosynthetically coherent
structural model (Figure S10).
We found that 7 could rapidly convert into 8 and 8′ in acidic
solution, with a product ratio of approximately 3:1, consistent
with the proportions observed in the ΔlucK mutant strain
(Figure 2C, trace ii). This strongly indicates that 7 is the
authentic biosynthetic intermediate. We propose that 8 and 8′
are generated from 7 via a single-step 6-exo-tet intramolecular
nucleophilic cyclization. Although the hydroxyl group is a poor
leaving group, protonation under acidic conditions prompts its
departure as water. Attack by the α-carbon of the tetronate
moiety from the Si face leads to the stereoselective formation of
spiro compound 8, while attack from the Re face yields its
diastereomer 8′ (Figure S11).
To clarify the catalytic role of LucK in this reaction, we
performed in vitro incubation with 7 under neutral phosphate
buffer. The reaction rapidly yielded 8 as the major product with
excellent diastereoselectivity (>90% de, Figure 2C, trace iii).
This result demonstrates that, despite its homology to Diels−
Alderases involved in spirotetronate biosynthesis, LucK does
not mediate a Diels−Alder cycloaddition. Rather, it catalyzes an
unusual, enzyme-guided, stereoselective intramolecular nucleo-
philic substitution that constructs the critical spirocyclic scaffold
of lucensimycin.
Given LucK’s unusual catalytic function compared to its
homologues in spirotetronate and spirotetramate pathways, we
investigated its substrate specificity and mechanism. Six
homologous enzymes (AbmU,19 AbyU,7 PyrI4,9 PloI4,20
Tsn15,10 and VstJ,21 associated with spirotetronate or polyether
tetronate biosynthesis, were expressed in E. coli and tested for
their ability to convert compound 7 into 8 or other IMDA-type
products. Despite 7 containing both a diene and a dienophile, no
transformation was observed (Figure S12). Likewise, LucK
failed to act on 14 (the native substrate of AbmU) or 15 (the
native substrate of PyrI4) (Figure S13), indicating high substrate
specificity of LucK.
Compound 7 is the O-acetylated derivative of 6, suggesting
that LucM and LucK do not act sequentially. To identify the
acyltransferase responsible for this modification, we focused on
LucNthe only remaining candidate enzyme in the gene
cluster. Knockout of lucN resulted in the accumulation of two
major metabolites, 9 and 10, as well as three minor products: 6,
6a, and 6b (Figure 2A, trace vii). Structural analysis confirmed
that compounds 9 and 10 are the nonacetylated analogs of 1 and
2, respectively (Tables S14−S15). Although the quantities of 6a
and 6b were insufficient for full NMR characterization, their
molecular weights based on LC-MS data are consistent with
spontaneous intramolecular nucleophilic cyclization products of
6analogous to the conversion of 7 to 8/8′ observed in the
ΔlucK mutant. The accumulation of compound 6 in the ΔlucN
mutant supports that LucN functions downstream of LucM,
catalyzing acetylation at the 2-OH position of 6.

To investigate their roles in the final steps of lucensimycin A
biosynthesis, we individually knocked out lucO3, lucI, and the
two additional monooxygenases in the cluster (lucO1 and
lucO2), annotated as luciferase-like enzymes. Deletion of lucO1

(Figure 2A, trace xi).
To validate LucO3′s function, we expressed and purified the
protein in E. coli BL21 (DE3). Upon incubation of 8 with
LucO3, a new product, compound 11, was detected (Figure 2B,
trace vii). An upscaled enzyme reaction allowed us to isolate 11,
and structural elucidation via LC-MS and NMR confirmed the
presence of a conjugated heptatrienal moiety (Table S16). The
generation of the other product, acetaldehyde, was also
confirmed through 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (DNPH) deri-
vatization analysis. (Figure S14). These results demonstrate that
LucO3 catalyzes selective oxidative cleavage of the terminal
alkene, generating two aldehyde products. To our knowledge,
this is the first functionally characterized CCD enzyme involved
in noncarotenoid natural product biosynthesis.

the alcohol derivative of 11 (Table S17), while 13 differs from 1
by reduction of the terminal double bond (Table S18). Although
we were unable to obtain soluble LucI protein for direct
biochemical validation, the data strongly suggests that LucI
functions as the aldehyde dehydrogenase responsible for
oxidizing the terminal aldehyde into a carboxylic acid,

The Structure of LucK. To gain further insight into LucK’s
catalytic mechanism, we solved the crystal structure of its apo-
form at 1.9 Å resolution (Figure 3A). LucK adopts a symmetric
dimeric conformation typical of β-barrel proteins. Each
monomer comprises an 11-stranded β-barrel core (β1−β11),
with a short α-helix (α1) inserted between β7 and β8. The dimer
exhibits an intermonomer angle of approximately 20°,
resembling the quaternary structure observed in AbyU, but
sharply contrasting with the parallel or perpendicular dimer
arrangements seen in PloI4 and PyrI4, respectively (Figure S15).
Due to poor electron density, the N-terminal loop region
(residues 27−36 between β3 and β4) was not resolved. This
flexible segment, located near the putative substrate entry site, is
hypothesized to form a lid-like α-helix that encapsulates the
substrate within the active site cavitya conformation
previously reported in PyrI4 (Figure 3A).26
Notably, unlike other β-barrel enzymes such as PyrI4,26
AbyU,7 AbmU,19 and Tsn1510which promote IMDA or
pericyclic rearrangement through hydrophobic substrate pre-
organizationLucK appears to catalyze an intramolecular
nucleophilic substitution reaction via general acid/base catalysis.
Such mechanisms are more reminiscent of natural product
methyltransferases27,28 and glycosyltransferases,29 which utilize
polar residues to activate nucleophiles and stabilize leaving
groups.30,31
Molecular docking of substrate 7 into LucK’s active site
revealed a polar catalytic environment composed, of residues
E16, T18, D50, Y48, Y83, E85, and R129 (Figure 3B-E). It
showed the 13-OH of 7 resides in close proximity (2.6 Å) to the
protonated guanidinium group of R129, while E85 and E16 are
positioned 4.4 Å and 3.3 Å from the 13-OH, respectively.
Alanine substitutions at either E16 or E85 completely abolished
enzymatic activity, whereas substituting E85 with aspartate
(E85D) nearly restored activity, suggesting the essential acidic
nature of this residue (Figure 3G). Although expression of
R129A led to insoluble protein, the R129H variant retained only
∼30% catalytic activity (Figure 3G). Collectively, these results
confirm E16, E85, and R129 as critical residues likely involved in
proton transfer, aiding in hydroxyl group departure.
Further inspection of the active site identified hydrophobic
contacts between residues L100 and A105 and the exo-
methylene group of the tetronate moiety, while residue T111
forms a hydrogen bond with the tetronate lactone carbonyl
(Figure 3F). These interactions are hypothesized to contribute
to the enzyme’s stereoselectivity. Although catalytic activity was
reduced in L110T/T111L and L110D/T111V double mutants,
substrate selectivity remained unchanged, suggesting these
positions are important for efficiency but not for stereocontrol
(Figure 3G).
Additionally, we observed two aromatic residuesY48 and
Y83situated near the cyclohexene ring and polyene tail of the
substrate (Figure 3E). The Y83A variant retained ∼45% of wild-
type activity, whereas the Y48A mutant was insoluble. Notably,
replacement of Y48 with serine (Y48S) abolished enzymatic
activity, while conservative substitution with phenylalanine
(Y48F) preserved activity (Figure 3G). These findings suggest
that the aromatic ring of Y48 is crucial for substrate engagement,
likely through π−π interactions and hydrophobic packing, while
Y83 may serve a more secondary structural or positioning role.
Based on these insights, we propose a mechanism for LucK-
catalyzed spirocyclization (Figure 3H). Upon binding of
substrate 7, its C-13 hydroxyl group undergoes protonation
facilitated by the E16−E85−R129 triad, enabling water
elimination. Meanwhile, the C-12 carbon of the tetronate
moiety, oriented by interactions with L100 and T111, performs
a nucleophilic attack on the carbon center from the Si face,
forming the stereoselective spirocyclic product. This distinct
mechanism, involving acid/base catalysis and intramolecular
nucleophilic substitution, sets LucK apart from its IMDA-
catalyzing homologues, highlighting a previously unrecognized
catalytic paradigm within the β-barrel enzyme superfamily.
■CONCLUSION
This study elucidates the biosynthetic pathway of the atypical
spirotetronate natural product lucensimycin A. Although its
biosynthetic gene cluster closely resembles those of canonical
spirotetronates, the pathway diverges remarkably at a key step:
the homologous enzyme LucK catalyzes a stereoselective
intramolecular nucleophilic cyclization, rather than the classical
pericyclic reaction. This unprecedented transformation leads to
the formation of a rare spiro[tetronate-hydrophenanthrene]
tetracyclic scaffold, significantly broadening the chemical space
accessible to spirotetronate biosynthetic enzymes.
Crystallographic analysis of LucK, combined with molecular
docking and site-directed mutagenesis, reveals that this β-barrel
enzyme carries out its unique catalytic function via a general
acid/base mechanismhighlighting a new reaction paradigm
within this enzyme family. In addition, we identified LucO3, an
olefin-cleaving enzyme that acts in the final step of lucensimycin
biosynthesis. Remarkably, LucO3 is the first carotenoid cleavage
dioxygenase (CCD) reported to mediate post-tailoring
modifications in a noncarotenoid natural product biosynthetic
pathway. Altogether, this work provides new insights into the
intricate link between biosynthetic logic and structural diversity
in tetronate-containing compounds. These findings open new
avenues for exploring the hidden catalytic potential within
established enzyme families.
