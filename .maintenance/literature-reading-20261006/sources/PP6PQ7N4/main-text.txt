ABSTRACT: Emodin-6-O-β-D-glucoside (E6G) and Emodin-8-O-β-D-glucoside (E8G) are promising plant-derived bioactive
compounds with significant pharmaceutical potential. However, their large-scale production is hindered by low natural abundance
and complex extraction processes. In this study, the biosynthesis of emodin glucosides in filamentous fungi was comprehensively
investigated using synthetic biology approaches. We evaluated ten highly efficient emodin UDP-glucosyltransferases (EUGTs) and
individually integrated these EUGTs into an emodin-accumulating Aspergillus terreus strain. Among these, the ΔgedA-Ft73BE variant
achieved the highest E6G titer of 217.5 mg/L. However, further optimization was impeded by challenges in expressing plant-derived
proteins in filamentous fungi. To address this, we developed a whole-cell catalytic system in Escherichia coli harboring Ft73BE,
achieving a high conversion rate of 78.9%. Sequence, structural, and phylogenetic analyses provided key insights into the enzymatic
mechanisms of EUGTs, revealing OsUGT’s dual-site glucosylation activity. This work advances our understanding of emodin
glucosylation and plant protein expression in microbial hosts while establishing a sustainable platform for the large-scale production
of these valuable compounds.
KEYWORDS: emodin glucosides, emodin UDP-glucosyltransferases, synthetic biology, Aspergillus terreus, whole-cell catalysis
1. INTRODUCTION
Rhubarb is an important medicinal herb used in traditional
Asian medicine and European cuisine.1 Its most significant
bioactive components are anthraquinones, including emodin
and its derivatives.2 Emodin, a key lead compound, exhibits
diverse pharmacological properties, such as anti-inflammatory,
antibacterial, antioxidant, and anticancer effects.3,4 However,
its limited water solubility hinders its development as a
pharmaceutical product, necessitating chemical modification to
improve its properties.5
Emodin glucosides, with glucose moieties linked at the C1-
OH or C3-OH positions of emodin, give rise to E6G and E8G,
respectively. These compounds are naturally found in the
Polygonaceae family, such as Rheum and Reynoutria japonica.6
Glucosylation enhances emodin’s pharmacological potential by
improving solubility, stability, and bioavailability.7
Among these derivatives, E6G has shown promising effects
in vascular inflammatory diseases, diabetic complications, and
atherosclerosis.8,9 It can also help uphold barrier integrity and
inhibit HMGB1-mediated inflammatory responses, suggesting
its potential in treating sepsis.10 Meanwhile, E8G displays
neuroprotective, antibacterial, antiviral, and bone regeneration-
promoting activities.11,12
Traditional production methods involving planting, harvest-
ing, extraction, and purification are inefficient and costly,
requiring the development of a sustainable biosynthetic
pathway for these compounds. Recent advances in synthetic
biology have revolutionized plant-derived bioactive compound
production, with microbial cell factories and whole-cell
catalysis replacing traditional methods. These approaches
offer high efficiency, cost-effectiveness, sustainability, and
scalability. Representative examples include cannabinoids,13
breviscapine,14 taxadiene,15 artemisinic acid,16 resveratrol,17
and tropane alkaloids.18
Despite significant advancements, microbial cell factories for
plant-derived natural products have predominantly relied on
Escherichia coli and Saccharomyces cerevisiae, with relatively
limited research utilizing filamentous fungi as hosts. For
example, Sun et al. successfully constructed a biosynthetic
pathway for emodin in S. cerevisiae, demonstrating yeast as a
viable platform for anthraquinone biosynthesis.19 In contrast,
Yao et al. achieved de novo production of physcion in the
filamentous fungus Aspergillus nidulans, yet studies leveraging
filamentous fungi for efficient emodin biosynthesis remain
scarce.20
Among filamentous fungi, A. terreus stands out as a robust
industrial strain, widely used for producing primary metabo-
lites such as itaconic acid21 and trans-aconitic acid,22,23 as well
as secondary metabolites like lovastatin.21 Its mature genetic
modification system and resilience under industrial conditions
make it an excellent platform for large-scale manufacturing. In
a previous study, we developed a high-efficiency emodin-

Based on this, we hypothesized that constructing an A.

conversion efficiency under optimized conditions. Additionally,
sequence, structural, and phylogenetic analyses of EUGTs

work provides insights into emodin glucosylation and plant
protein expression in fungi, offering a sustainable platform for
large-scale production of emodin glucosides.
2. MATERIALS AND METHODS
2.1. General Materials. A. terreus HXN301, a statin-producing
strain stored in our laboratory, was used as the parent strain (Table
S1). Oligonucleotide synthesis and DNA sequencing services were
provided by TsingKe Biotech (Qingdao, China). All oligonucleotide
primers are listed in Table S2. Authentic emodin, UDP-glucose and
E8G standards were purchased from Solarbio (Beijing, China). The
standard E6G was obtained from the in vitro reaction product of
emodin, which was catalyzed by FmUGT, and was verified through
liquid chromatography-high resolution mass spectrometry (LC-
HRMS) (Figure S1) and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR)
analyses (Table S3). The T4 DNA ligase and PrimeSTAR GXL
DNA Polymerase utilized in this study were obtained from Takara Bio
(Dalian, China). Fast-digest restriction endonucleases were purchased
from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Vilnius, Lithuania). A Pro ligation-free
cloning kit from Abm (Richmond, Canada) was employed for the
construction of vectors following the manufacturer’s instructions. The
Fungal DNA Kit, Plasmid Mini Kit I, Gel Extraction Kit, and Cycle
Pure Kit from Omega Bio-Tek (Norcross, USA) were used for the
extraction of fungal genomic DNA, plasmids, agarose gel, and PCR
products, respectively. E. coli DH5α and BL21(DE3) were utilized for
gene cloning and protein expression, respectively.
2.2. Gene Synthesis, Expression, and Purification of EUGTs.
Eleven candidate EUGT genes, including FmUGT, UGT75, OsUGT,
RyUGT3A, RyUGT12, RpUGT1, AlUGT, NsUGT, Ft73BE,
Ct73AE, and MhGT1 (Table S4), were synthesized with codon
optimization for E. coli and A. terreus and cloned into the pET28b
vector by BGI Genomics (Beijing, China). The resulting expression
vectors were then transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3) for
heterologous expression of N-terminal His6-tagged recombinant
proteins. Transformants were cultured overnight at 37 °C on LB
agar supplemented with 50 mg/L kanamycin. Positive clones were
screened using PCR with primers EUGTs-F/EUGTs-R.
Positive clones were selected and cultured in LB broth
supplemented with 50 mg/L kanamycin. When the OD600 reached
0.6−0.8, protein expression was induced with 0.2 mM isopropyl β-D-
thio-galactopyranoside (IPTG), following previously established
protocols.25 Protein concentrations were determined using a Bradford
Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) with a
standard curve of bovine serum albumin.
2.3. In Vitro Enzymatic Assays of EUGTs. The standard assay of
EUGTs was conducted using 2 μM of protein, 100 μM of emodin, 2
mM of UDP-glucose, and 2 mM of MgCl2 in a reaction buffer of 100
μL (50 mM Tris-HCl, 20% glycerol, pH 7.4) incubated at 30 °C for
20 min. Finally, all reactions were quenched by adding 300 μL ethyl
acetate, followed by extraction. The upper organic layer was collected,
dried under a nitrogen stream, and redissolved in 200 μL of methanol.
Following centrifugation at 13,000 rpm for 10 min, a portion of the
sample (20 μL) was analyzed by HPLC using a ThermoFisher
Scientific C18 column (5 μm, 4.6 mm × 250 mm). The analysis
method is as follows: solvent A (100% ACN), solvent B (100% H2O
+ 0.1% TFA), gradient elution (0−10 min 0%−35% A, 10−20 min
35%−100% A, 20−24 min 100% A, 24−25 min 100%−35% A, 25−30
min 35% A), the flow rate was 1 mL/min, and the detection
wavelength was 440 nm. The titer of emodin and emodin glucoside
were evaluated based on the HPLC peak area relative to their
authentic standards.
2.4. LC-HRMS Analysis. The LC-HRMS analysis was performed
using a Waters Symmetry C18 column (5 μm, 2.1 × 150 mm) with
positive or negative-mode electrospray ionization. The biphasic
solvent gradient system used was as follows: 0−1 min with 40% A,
1−18 min with a gradient from 40% to 0% A, 18−24 min with 0% A,
24−25 min with a gradient from 0% to 40% A, and 25−30 min with
40% A. The flow rate was set at 0.2 mL/min, and the column
temperature was maintained at 30 °C. Solvent A was 100% ddH2O
with 0.1% TFA, while solvent B was 100% ACN. The HRMS data
were acquired using a Dionex Ultimate 3000 system coupled to a
Bruker Maxis Q-TOF.
2.5. Construction of the Emodin Glucoside-Producing A.
terreus Cell Factory. A. terreus strain ΔgedA (Table S1) is a variant
of HXN301 that produces emodin.24 Subsequently, an emodin-
producing, uridine-deficient strain (ΔgedA-ΔpyrG) was constructed
by knocking out pyrG (Table S1). Further deletion of P450-6231
using pyrG as a selective marker generated the ΔgedA-Δ6231 strain
(Table S1), which served as a negative control in the fermentation of
all mutant strains. These strains were constructed following the
methodology described in a previous study.26,27
The EUGT genes (FmUGT, UGT75, OsUGT, RyUGT3A,
RyUGT12, RpUGT1, AlUGT, NsUGT, Ft73BE, and Ct73AE) were
individually amplified from codon-optimized, synthetically con-
structed plasmids using the following primer pairs: FmUGT-1F/
FmUGT-1R, UGT75-2F/UGT75-2R, OsUGT-3F/OsUGT-3R,
RyUGT3A-4F/RyUGT3A-4R, RyUGT12-5F/RyUGT12-5R,
RpUGT1-6F/RpUGT1-6R, AlUGT-7F/AlUGT-7R, NsUGT-8F/
NsUGT-8R, Ft73BE-9F/Ft73BE-9R, and Ct73AE-10F/Ct73AE-
10R, respectively. Additionally, the 6231-PgpdA-Tef-pyrG fragment
was amplified from 6231-PgpdA-H5468-Tef-pyrG plasmid (labora-
tory storage) using the primer pairs: PgpdA-1F/PgpdA-1R, PgpdA-
2F/PgpdA-2R, PgpdA-3F/PgpdA-3R, PgpdA-4F/PgpdA-4R, PgpdA-
5F/PgpdA-5R, PgpdA-6F/PgpdA-6R, PgpdA-7F/PgpdA-7R, respec-
tively. Subsequently, EUGTs fragments and 6231-PgpdA-Tef-pyrG
fragments were individually ligated together using a Pro Ligation-Free
Cloning Kit (ABM, Canada) to generate corresponding bifunctional
H6231 knockout/EUGTs knockin plasmids. Finally, all constructed
plasmids were confirmed by DNA sequencing and the H6231
knockout/EUGTs knockin cassettes were individually amplified with
the C6231-F/C6231-R primer pair from the bifunctional plasmids
and transformed into the uracil auxotrophic A. terreus variant ΔgedA-
ΔpyrG by polyethylene glycol (PEG)-mediated transformation as
described previously.28 The transformants were selected on potato
dextrose agar with sorbitol (PDAS) (4 g/L potato starch, 20 g/L
dextrose and 15 g/L agar) plates supplemented with 1 M sorbitol
(Solarbio, China) and verified by colony anchor PCR using a Phire
Plant Direct PCR Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to
the manufacturer’s instructions with the upstream primers U6231-F/
U6231-R, downstream primers D6231-F/D6231-R and internal
primers N6231-F/N6231-R for all transformants.
2.6. UDP-Glucose and Citric Acid Feeding Assays. Approx-
imately 3 × 107 spores of ΔgedA-OsUGT and ΔgedA-RyUGT3A
were inoculated into 250 mL of LPM medium and incubated at 28 °C
with rotary shaking at 220 rpm. After 24 h, 0.1 mM UDP-glucose was
added, and fermentation products were collected daily for HPLC
analysis.
For the citric acid feeding assay, citric acid was supplemented to
the culture at final concentrations of 0, 5, 10, 15, and 20 g/L. The
cultures were maintained under the same conditions for 7 days, and
fermentation products were analyzed by HPLC on the final day.

H8706-F/H8706-GGGGS-R were used to amplify the UDP-glucose

2.8. Construction of Four GFP Fusion Protein Variants. The

development of engineered H6231 knockout strains expressing fusion
proteins. Finally, the four recombinant H6231 knockout/fusion
protein expression cassettes were amplified using C6231−F/C6231-
R primers from the corresponding plasmids and transformed into the
uracil auxotrophic A. terreus ΔgedA-ΔpyrG strain following the
method described above.
2.9. Fermentation Analysis of All Variants. The ΔgedA-
EUGTs variants, H8706-EAAAK-OsUGT, H8706-GGGGS-OsUGT,
and four GFP fusion protein variants were first cultured on PDAS
plates at 28 °C for 7 days. Conidia were then harvested and
suspended in sterile water, and approximately 3.0 × 107 conidia were
inoculated into 250 mL LPM fermentation medium (70 g/L glucose,
40 g/L sucrose, 10 g/L nestle milk powder, 5 g/L fermented soybean
meal, 1 g/L yeast extract, 1 g/L peptone, 1 g/L sodium acetate, 2 g/L
sodium citrate, 0.04 g/L KH2PO4, 0.5 g/L PEG-400, 0.1 g/L MgSO4·
7H2O, 1.5 g/L CaCO3, pH 6.8). Fermentation was carried out at 28
°C for 7 days with 220 rpm rotary shaking. On the last day, 2 mL of
culture was collected from each strain and extracted overnight with 20
mL dichloromethane/methanol (1:1, v/v). The upper organic phase
(500 μL) was then evaporated under nitrogen flow, redissolved in 500
μL methanol, filtered through 0.22 μm organic membranes, and
analyzed by HPLC. To further evaluate the intracellular and
extracellular distribution of E6G, the fermentation broth of the
ΔgedA-Ft73BE variant, treated as described previously,24 was
analyzed by HPLC.
2.10. Fluorescence Microscopy of GFP Fusion Variants and
In Vitro Enzymatic Reconstitution Assays Using EUGT Over-
expression Strains. Spores of the four GFP fusion protein variants
were inoculated into 250 mL of IPM medium (150 g/L glucose, 2 g/
L NH4NO3, 1 g/L corn steep powder, 0.01 g/L (NH4)2HPO4, 0.01
g/L FeSO4, 0.4 g/L MgSO4, pH 4.0) and cultured at 28 °C with
rotary shaking at 220 rpm for 30 h. A small amount of culture medium
was taken, and the mycelium was observed under the confocal
microscopy (FluoView FV1000).
Meanwhile, spores of four GFP fusion protein variants and six
ΔgedA-EUGT variants (FmUGT, UGT75, OsUGT, RyUGT3A,
RyUGT12, and RpUGT1) were inoculated into 250 mL of IPM
medium and cultured at 28 °C, 220 rpm for 3 days. Mycelia were
harvested by filtration, washed with ddH2O, frozen in liquid nitrogen,
and ground into powder. Approximately 150−200 mg of mycelium
was suspended in buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, 10% glycerol, pH 7.4) and
centrifuged at 12,000 rpm, 4 °C for 10 min. SDS-PAGE was used to
assess the quality of the extracted protein.
For in vitro enzyme assays, a 100 μL reaction mixture was prepared,
containing supernatant (10 μL), emodin (200 μM), UDP-glucose (1
mM), and reaction buffer. Negative controls were set up by omitting
UDP-glucose, supernatant, or emodin, while a positive control used
purified enzyme. The reactions were incubated overnight at 30 °C,
220 rpm, then quenched by adding an equal volume of methanol.
After centrifugation at 13,000 rpm for 10 min, 20 μL of the
supernatant was collected for HPLC analysis.
2.11. Whole-Cell Biotransformation of Emodin into Emodin
Glucoside. Based on the results of in vitro and in vivo experiments,
several active EUGTs were selected for in vitro whole-cell catalytic
experiments, revealing that RyUGT12 and Ft73BE exhibited higher
conversion rates. Thus, E. coli BL21(DE3) harboring RyUGT12 or
Ft73BE was individually cultured overnight in LB medium with
kanamycin at 37 °C and 220 rpm. When the culture reached OD600 =
0.6−0.8, 0.2 mM IPTG was added to induce protein expression,
followed by incubation at 16 °C and 220 rpm for 20 h. Cells were
harvested by centrifugation (5000 × g, 20 min, 4 °C), resuspended in
1 mL of fresh M9 medium (6.8 g/L Na2HPO4, 3.0 g/L KH2PO4, 0.5
g/L NaCl, 1.0 g/L NH4Cl, 1.2 g/L MgSO4, 1.1 g/L CaCl2)
containing 2% glucose and antibiotics for recovery, and adjusted to
OD600 = 1.0, 2.0, 3.0, or 4.0 to assess the effect of cell density on
biotransformation. A final concentration of 0.7 mM emodin was
added, and the culture was incubated at 30 °C and 220 rpm for 24 h.
After incubation, 1 mL of reaction mixture was extracted with an
equal volume of ethyl acetate, dried under nitrogen, redissolved in 200
μL methanol, filtered (0.22 μm organic membrane), and analyzed by
HPLC. Using the optimal cell density, the effect of different
exogenous emodin concentrations (0.1, 0.2, 0.4, and 0.6 mM) on
catalytic efficiency was further evaluated using the same procedure.
To assess the effect of emodin on cell growth, Ft73BE-expressing E.
coli cells were induced as described above, harvested, and resuspended
in fresh M9 medium to an OD600 of 1.0. Emodin was added to the
experimental group at a final concentration of 0.1 mM. Two control
groups were included: one with an equivalent volume of DMSO and
one without any addition. All cultures were incubated at 30 °C and
220 rpm for 24 h, and OD600 values were measured every 3 h using a
microplate reader.
2.12. Structure Prediction and Comparison of OsUGT and
Ft73BE. The three-dimensional structures of OsUGT and Ft73BE
were predicted using CHAIDiscovery, a deep learning-based protein
structure prediction tool.30 The predicted models were evaluated
using the ipTM (interface predicted TM-score) and pTM (predicted
TM-score) metrics, which assess the global and local accuracy of the
predicted structures. For OsUGT, the ipTM and pTM scores were
0.8913 and 0.9493, respectively, indicating high confidence in the
predicted structure. Similarly, for Ft73BE, the ipTM and pTM scores
were 0.9182 and 0.9499, respectively.
The predicted structures of OsUGT and Ft73BE were compared
using PyMOL (Version 4.6.0, Schrödinger LLC). The structures were
aligned using the align command, and the Root Mean Square
Deviation (RMSD) of the Cα atoms was calculated to assess
structural similarity. The RMSD value between OsUGT and Ft73BE
was 1.931 Å, indicating a high degree of structural similarity between
the two proteins.

3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
3.1. Screening of EUGTs and Analysis of Enzyme
Activity In Vitro. To construct an A. terreus cell factory
capable of producing emodin glucoside, we selected candidate
EUGTs through a combined-literature and homology-guided
approach. Specifically, five UGTs previously reported to
glucosylate emodin in vitro were chosen, including
RpUGT1,33 RyUGT3A,34 RyUGT12,34 Ft73BE,35 and
Ct73AE.36 Using their protein sequences as BLAST queries,
four additional homologues (FmUGT, UGT75, AlUGT, and
NsUGT) were identified. We also included MhGT137 and
OsUGT,38 two UGTs known for broad activity toward
flavonoid scaffolds, based on the structural similarity between
flavonoids and anthraquinones such as emodin. All 11 enzymes
were tested in vitro for their emodin glucosylation activity
(Tables S4 and S5).
The coding genes of all candidate EUGTs were codon-
optimized, synthesized, and subcloned into the pET28b vector
for heterologous expression in E. coli BL21(DE3). Recombi-
nant enzymes with N-terminal His6 tags were successfully
purified using Ni-NTA chromatography, as confirmed by SDS-
PAGE analysis (Figure 1A). Among the 11 candidates, all
plant-derived EUGTs had molecular weights of approximately
53 kDa, while the only fungal UGT (MhGT1) was smaller, at
36.6 kDa. Subsequently, the enzymatic activities of the 11
candidate EUGTs were evaluated in vitro using UDP-glucose
as the glucosyl donor and emodin as the substrate. HPLC
analysis revealed that all candidates, except fungal UGT
(MhGT1), catalyzed the formation of emodin glucoside, which
was identified based on retention time comparison (Figure
1B). The large-scale preparation of the product using FmUGT
was further characterized by HR-LCMS (Figure S1) and NMR
spectroscopy (Table S3), confirming it as E6G. Furthermore,
comparison with authentic standards showed that OsUGT
exhibited moderate emodin-8-O-glucosyltransferase activity,
producing trace amounts of E8G (Figure 1B).
The emodin transformation rates of most EUGTs ranged
from 60% to 70% after a 20 min reaction with 2 μM of purified
enzyme and 100 μM emodin. Notably, RyUGT3A demon-
strated the highest activity, achieving a conversion rate of
83.5%, followed by Ft73BE at 81.8% (Figure 1C), indicating
their strong catalytic efficiency for emodin glucosylation.
Among the 11 candidates, 10 plant-derived enzymes catalyzed
the formation of E6G, among which OsUGT additionally
produced trace amounts of E8G.

overexpression of the EUGTs (Figure 2A). Consequently,
ten variants were successfully constructed and designated as
ΔgedA-FmUGT, ΔgedA-UGT75, ΔgedA-OsUGT, ΔgedA-
RpUGT1, ΔgedA-RyUGT3A, ΔgedA-RyUGT12, ΔgedA-
AlUGT, ΔgedA-NsUGT, ΔgedA-Ft73BE, and ΔgedA-
Ct73AE, respectively (Table S1).
The spores of these mutants were inoculated into LPM
medium and cultured for 7 days. Fermentation products were
extracted and analyzed using HPLC. Notably, seven out of ten
ΔgedA-EUGT variants, including ΔgedA-OsUGT, ΔgedA-
RyUGT3A, ΔgedA-RyUGT12, ΔgedA-AlUGT, ΔgedA-
NsUGT, ΔgedA-Ft73BE, and ΔgedA-Ct73AE, successfully
produced detectable amounts of emodin glucosides (Figure
2B). Among these, ΔgedA-Ft73BE demonstrated the highest
production, yielding 217.5 mg/L of E6G. This is consistent
with the in vitro assay, where Ft73BE also showed second-
highest conversion efficiency. Further quantification of E6G in
the cell extract and culture medium revealed that E6G was
primarily accumulated in the intracellular fraction, with only
3.3% detected in the supernatant (Figure S2).
Other variants produced emodin glucosides at titers ranging
from 12.9 mg/L to 40.5 mg/L. Consistent with the in vitro
results, the ΔgedA-OsUGT mutant produced 35.3 mg/L of
E6G and 9.3 mg/L of E8G (Figure 2C). However, significant
amounts of emodin residue were observed, with ΔgedA-
Ft73BE and ΔgedA-OsUGT retaining 2.6 g/L and 1.6 g/L,
respectively. Moreover, A. terreus can accumulate emodin up to
∼10 mM over 7 days, providing a high in situ substrate
reservoir that could be leveraged for downstream biotransfor-
mation. This observation, together with its ability to express
functional EUGTs, highlights A. terreus as a promising basis for
developing a dual-function microbial chassis capable of both de
novo emodin biosynthesis and whole-cell glucoside production.
3.3. Addressing Challenges in Enhancing Emodin
Glucoside Production. Despite the successful establishment
of an A. terreus cell factory for emodin glucoside production,
the transformation rate still leaves significant room for
improvement. This limitation may stem from (1) insufficient
availability of UDP-glucose, (2) inefficient expression or
degradation of plant-derived UGTs, and (3) compartmental-
ization restricting substrate access to EUGTs (Figure 3A).39
To address the potential limitation of UDP-glucose, both
exogenous UDP-glucose supplementation and citric acid-
mediated carbon flux redirection were tested;40 however,
neither approach improved emodin glucoside production
(Figure S3). Subsequent KEGG and transcriptome analyses
(Figure S4; Table S6) identified UDP-glucose pyrophosphor-
ylase (H8706) as a key enzyme. At the time of fusion and
coexpression experiments, Ft73BE had not yet been identified
or synthesized. Therefore, OsUGT, an early candidate with
relatively good activity, was selected for initial testing. Thus,
H8706 was then coexpressed with OsUGT using either a
flexible (GGGGS) or rigid (EAAAK) linker. However,
fermentation and HPLC analyses showed no enhancement in
emodin glucoside titers (Figure S5), ruling out UDP-glucose
insufficiency as a limiting factor.
Subcellular localization analysis revealed that most EUGTs
in plants are localized to the cell membrane or chloroplast
(Table S4). To facilitate proper folding and localization in A.
terreus, the dual-mutation inactivated native O-methyltransfer-
ase, GedA-Y173A-R434A (GedAM),29 was fused with OsUGT
using either a flexible or rigid linker. Additionally, Green
Fluorescent Protein (GFP) was fused to the C-terminus to
monitor expression and localization (Figure 3B). However,
none of the four fusion variants (Table S1) improved emodin
glucoside production (Figure 3C). Fluorescence microscopy
detected weak green fluorescence in the merged field only in
the mycelia of OsUGT-GFP, while the other variants exhibited
no detectable fluorescence (Figures 3D and S6). Furthermore,

3.4. Establishment of a whole-Cell System for
Emodin Glucoside Production. Although the identified
EUGTs exhibited high catalytic efficiency in in vitro assays,
their introduction into emodin-accumulating A. terreus failed to
achieve satisfactory transformation rates in vivo, as more than
92% of emodin remained unconverted in the variant ΔgedA-
Ft73BE, which exhibited the highest emodin glucoside titer,
indicating low substrate utilization efficiency. Additionally,
using purified enzymes for emodin glucoside production
presents significant challenges, including enzyme instability,
high preparation costs, high UDP-glucose cost, and operational
complexity.

To overcome these limitations and improve process
feasibility, we established a whole-cell glucosylation catalytic
system. Since E. coli can synthesize UDP-glucose endoge-
nously, we leveraged this capability to establish a whole-cell
biocatalysis system.41 Emodin was directly supplied as a
substrate to fermentation cultures of BL21(DE3) strains
harboring EUGTs.
Among the ten tested EUGTs, RyUGT12 and Ft73BE
showed the highest catalytic efficiency, as confirmed by feeding
assays and HPLC analysis (Figure 4A). To maximize glucoside
production, we systematically optimized key parameters,
including cell density (OD600) and substrate concentration
(Figure S8). Under optimized conditions (OD600 = 1.0, 0.1
mM emodin), RyUGT12 and Ft73BE achieved conversion
rates of 77.5% and 78.9%, respectively, within 24 h (Figure
4B). Cell growth analysis during fermentation revealed that
emodin addition caused moderate inhibition of E. coli growth,
with final OD600 values reduced by ∼35−40%. Nonetheless,
cells maintained active proliferation throughout the 24-h
reaction period, indicating that the whole-cell biocatalytic
system remains robust and operational under substrate-fed
conditions (Figure S9). This corresponds to a productivity of
approximately 34.11 mg/L/day for Ft73BE, which is slightly
higher than the 31.1 mg/L/day achieved by A. terreus
overexpressing the same enzyme under comparable conditions
(Table S7). This strategy provides a scalable and practical
alternative to the use of purified enzymes, making it promising
for industrial applications.
3.5. Sequence, Structure, and Functional Character-
ization of EUGTs. Given the varying glucosylation efficiencies
of EUGTs in both in vitro and whole-cell systems, we sought to
investigate their molecular characteristics to understand the
structural basis underlying their catalytic properties. Sequence
alignment of the 11 UGT genes revealed that nearly all
sequences contained the highly conserved catalytic His-Asp
dyad, key amino acids essential for glycosyl transfer reactions,42
except for FmUGT and UGT75, in which the Asp residue was
replaced by Thr. Additionally, the 44-amino acid Plant
Secondary Product Glycosyltransferase (PSPG) motif, recog-
nized for its critical role in UDP-sugar donor recognition and
binding,43 was well conserved in the C-terminal regions of
these enzymes (Figure S10).
Most of the 10 candidate EUGTs displayed strict
regioselectivity, targeting exclusively the C6 hydroxyl group.
However, OsUGT demonstrated dual-site glucosylation
activity, modifying both the C6 and C8 hydroxyl groups,
setting it apart from other enzymes. Meanwhile, both in vitro
enzymatic assays and whole-cell systems consistently demon-
strated that Ft73BE exhibits higher catalytic efficiency.
To elucidate the underlying enzymatic mechanisms, protein
structure prediction and substrate docking analyses were
performed for OsUGT and Ft73BE. Structural superposition
revealed significant differences in their N-terminal regions,
which are critical for substrate binding, particularly in α-helices
(α2, α3, α4, α6, and α7), while the overall fold of the C-
terminal region remained conserved (Figures 5A and S11).
Emodin binding site analysis identified four relatively
conserved residues (His-22/His-19, Phe-136/Phe-120, Ser-
157/Ser-141, and Glu-401/Glu-394) that likely stabilize the
anthraquinone core through π-π stacking and hydrogen
bonding (Figures 5B and S10). In contrast, OsUGT-specific
residues (Trp-135, Trp-212, Val-19, and Glu-103) may reduce
spatial constraints, enabling C8 hydroxyl glucosylation
(Figures 5C and S12). These structural differences likely
underlie their distinct regioselectivity, future mutagenesis
studies and in vitro enzymatic characterization will be
conducted to validate these findings.
Phylogenetic analysis revealed that emodin glucosylation
activity is predominantly found in the UGT73, UGT70/71,
and UGT75 families, with Ft73BE and OsUGT clustering into
distinct clades (Figure 5D). This evolutionary divergence
aligns with their structural differences, particularly in the N-
terminal region, which may contribute to their regioselectivity
and catalytic efficiency. These findings suggest that different
UGT families may have independently evolved to accom-
modate anthraquinone substrates.
Elucidating the enzymology of these EUGTs will provide
guidance for future protein engineering modifications. For
example, while E8G is more prevalent in nature, most EUGTs
identified in this study and in previous reports exhibited
emodin-6-O-UDP-glucosyltransferase activity.33−38 Among
them, only OsUGT exhibited detectable emodin-8-O-glucosyl-
transferase activity. This discrepancy may arise from differ-
ences in N- or O-glycosylation patterns between plant-derived
EUGTs and those expressed in filamentous fungi, potentially
altering the enzymes’ binding pockets. If a dedicated emodin-
8-O-UDP-glucosyltransferase cannot be identified, OsUGT
could serve as a suitable candidate for protein engineering,

To improve emodin glucoside production in A. terreus,
future strategies could include coexpressing plant-derived
chaperones, deleting endogenous protease genes, or construct-
ing hybrid plant-fungal proteins to enhance EUGT expression
and stability. Advances in protein engineering and synthetic
biology could further optimize enzyme activity and specificity.
While E. coli and S. cerevisiae are widely used chassis for
natural product biosynthesis due to their genetic tractability,
they may be suboptimal for compounds like emodin, which are
natural intermediates in fungal natural product biosynthesis.
Here, we demonstrate for the first time the use of A. terreus as a
filamentous fungal chassis for in vivo emodin glucoside
productionmarking a conceptual advance in host selection.
This system supports both native emodin biosynthesis and
selective glucosylation via heterologous plant UGTs. Among
these, Ft73BE showed high conversion efficiency (78.9%) and
strong specificity toward E6G, outperforming Bacillus lichen-
iformis YjiC (72% conversion, multiple byproducts, Table S7).7
This is also the first systematic evaluation of plant UGT
expression in a filamentous fungal host, revealing both
challenges and promise. Despite modest titers, the observed
productivity (31.1 mg/L/day, Table S7) already surpasses that
of some bacterial systems.7
Together, our work establishes filamentous fungi as a viable
chassis for de novo biosynthesis and in vivo modification of
fungal-type natural products. Importantly, the catalytic
efficiency of E. coli-based whole-cell systems supports the
development of hybrid strategiesranging from coculture and
lysate-based coupling to tandem fermentation setupsthat
integrate the metabolic strengths of fungi with the catalytic
advantages of bacterial hosts. These strategies highlight the
practical and conceptual value of our approach, and lay the
groundwork for scalable and sustainable production of emodin
glucosides, paving the way for their broader industrial and
pharmacological applications.
