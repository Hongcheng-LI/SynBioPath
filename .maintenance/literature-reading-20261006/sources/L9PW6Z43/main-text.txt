ABSTRACT: Chalcone is a privileged natural product skeleton for
drug discovery, and retrochalcone represents a group of non-
classical chalcones with a distinctive oxygen substitution pattern.
Echinatin, a hepatoprotective agent, is a retrochalcone derived
from Glycyrrhiza inflata. Despite their initial discovery half a
century ago, the biosynthetic mechanisms of retrochalcones have
remained elusive. In this work, we identified a ketoreductase,
GinKR1, which selectively catalyzes the reduction of the 1″-
carbonyl group of the dibenzoylmethane precursor 2′-O-methyl-
licodione, followed by spontaneous dehydration to form the
retrochalcone skeleton. Our findings reveal that the A and B rings
of retrochalcones are derived from the shikimate and polyketide
pathways, respectively, which are reversed to normal chalcones. In addition, 18O isotope labeling verifies that the carbonyl oxygen of
retrochalcones is derived from the hydroxyl group introduced by a flavanone 2-hydroxylase. The complete biosynthetic pathway of
echinatin was elucidated by identifying six enzymes from G. inflata. Moreover, we determined the crystal structure of GinKR1 and
identified a critical α10 helix responsible for its regioselectivity. With this α10 helix as a marker, we further discovered homologous
genes of GinKR1 from 185 plant species. This study elucidates the biosynthetic pathway and underlying mechanisms of
retrochalcones.
■INTRODUCTION
Chalcones represent a significant class of phenolic natural
products.1 They play important roles in plant physiology as
phytoalexins, environmental responders, and color contrib-
utors.2−4 On the other hand, the chalcone scaffold is recognized
as a privileged structure in drug discovery, with several
derivatives including metochalcone and sofalcone having been
developed into clinical drugs.5−9 Structurally, chalcones feature
a C6−C3−C6 skeleton comprising two aromatic rings
(designated as the A and B rings) connected by an α,β-
unsaturated carbonyl moiety.1 From the biosynthetic perspec-
tive, the A ring is derived from the polyketide pathway through
decarboxylative Claisen-type condensation of three malonyl
coenzyme A (malonyl-CoA) units, while the B ring and the C3
chain is derived from the shikimate pathway with p-coumaroyl-
CoA as a precursor.10,11 This conserved biosynthetic pathway
typically results in chalcones possessing hydroxyl groups at C-2′,
4′, and 6′ positions of the A ring, as exemplified by naringenin
chalcone.12,13 In certain plant species, chalcone reductase
(CHR) collaborates with chalcone synthase (CHS) to produce
6′-deoxychalcones such as isoliquiritigenin from Glycyrrhiza
species.14,15 The structural diversity of chalcones is further
enhanced through various postmodifications, including pre-
nylation, glycosylation, methylation, and Diels−Alder reac-
tions.16−18 These modifications contribute to the various
pharmacological properties of chalcones.
Retrochalcones are a group of nonclassical chalcones
characterized by a distinctive oxygen substitution pattern.19,20
Unlike typical chalcones, retrochalcones generally lack hydroxyl
groups at the C-2′ and C-6′ positions and contain a methoxyl
group at the C-2 position. These compounds are present in a
limited number of plant species belonging to the Leguminosae,
Annonaceae, Dioscoreaceae, Asparagaceae, and Eucommiaceae
families.21−26 Among the well-known retrochalcones are
echinatin and its prenylated derivative, licochalcone A, which
are abundant (exceeding 0.2% of dry weight) in the medicinal
plant Glycyrrhiza inflata.27 They exhibit significant hepatopro-
tective effects in CCl4-induced liver injury mice.28−30
Although the biosynthetic pathway of normal chalcones has
been well established, very few reports are available on the
biosynthesis of retrochalcones.10,31,32 The first investigation was
reported in 1975. Afterward, several Japanese research groups
investigated the biosynthesis of echinatin using cultured cells of
Glycyrrhiza echinata through isotope labeling experiments and

GinKR1 is the key enzyme in forming the retrochalcone
skeleton. It selectively reduces the 1″-carbonyl group of 2′-O-
methyllicodione (4), resulting in a swap in the biosynthetic
origins of the A and B rings in retrochalcones: the A-ring
(designated as A′ in Figure 1A) is from the shikimate pathway,
and the B-ring (B′ in Figure 1A) is from the polyketide pathway.
It could also reduce the 3″-carbonyl group to produce normal
chalcone 9 as a minor product. In contrast, GinKR2 selectively
catalyzes the reduction of the 3″-carbonyl group to obtain 9. The
expression level of GinKR1 in the roots of G. inflata was
remarkably higher than that in the leaves, which was consistent
with the high abundance of echinatin in the roots. This
expression pattern was exactly distinct from that of GinKR2.
According to the biosynthetic pathway, the carbonyl oxygen
of retrochalcones should be derived from the hydroxyl group
introduced by F2H (Figure 2A). To prove this hypothesis, we
conducted three groups of enzyme catalysis experiments (Figure
2D). Liquiritigenin (6) could be converted to 4 by GinF2H and
Gin2′OMT. Next, GinKR1 was added to group 1, and the
reaction took place in 18O2 gas; GinKR1 was added to group 2,
and the reaction took place in regular 16O2 gas; GinKR2 was
added to group 3, and the reaction took place in 18O2 gas.
GinKR1 and GinKR2 converted 4 into 3/1 and 9/10,
respectively. The catalytic reaction products were analyzed by
HPLC (high-performance liquid chromatography) and LC/MS
(Figure 2E,F). The mass spectra of product 4 in groups 1 and 3
showed an [M −H]−ion at m/z 287.08, 2 Da greater than group
2, indicating the 18O isotope was incorporated into 4 through
GinF2H catalysis (Figure 2F).40 Similar results were observed
for 3 and 9. However, product 1 in group 1 showed an [M −H]−
ion at m/z 271.09, while product 10 in group 3 showed an [M −
H]−ion at m/z 269.08. The 18O isotope was eliminated during
dehydration of group 3. This result was consistent with the
different regioselectivity of GinKR1 and GinKR2 and confirmed
the biosynthetic pathway of echinatin.
Validation of the Biosynthetic Pathway In Vivo. RNA
interference (RNAi) and overexpression (OE) hairy root
systems were established to verify the functions of the identified
biosynthetic enzymes in G. inflata (Figure S25). We determined
the contents of echinatin (1) and its derivative licochalcone A
(LCA, 2), being the major retrochalcones in the hairy roots
(Table S5). In the RNAi lines, the expression levels of GinKR1
and GinKR2 decreased by 84% and 86%, respectively. In
accordance, the contents of echinatin and LCA decreased by
73% and 88%, respectively, in the GinKR1-RNAi line but
changed little in the GinKR2-RNAi line. Likewise, the contents
of echinatin and LCA increased by 247% and 84%, respectively,
in the GinKR1-OE line but changed little in the GinKR2-OE line
(Figure 3A,B). These results indicate that GinKR1 indeed
participates in the biosynthesis of echinatin in G. inflata, while
GinKR2 is unlikely to be involved. We also noticed the content
of the normal chalcone (3-deoxysappanchalcone, 10) was very
low in the control group and the GinKR2-RNAi group, but
increased remarkably in the GinKR2-OE group (Figure S26).
Although 10 could be directly synthesized from isoliquiritigenin
(7) by Gin2′OMT, the above results indicate that GinKR2 may
play a role in its biosynthesis (Figure S27).
In a similar manner, we verified the in vivo functions of
GinF2H and Gin2′OMT. In the GinF2H-RNAi line, GinF2H
showed 85% lower expression than the EV group, decreasing the
content of echinatin and LCA by 75% and 88%, respectively. In
the Gin2′OMT-RNAi line, Gin2′OMT showed 80% lower
expression than EV, and it decreased the content of echinatin
and LCA by 49% and 67%, respectively. Conversely, in the
overexpression lines, GinF2H and Gin2′OMT exhibited 51- and
19-fold higher expressions, respectively. In accordance, the
contents of echinatin and LCA increased by 267% and 84% in
the GinF2H-OE line, respectively, while they increased by 180%
and 71% in the Gin2′OMT-OE line (Figure 3C,D). Moreover,
the expression levels of GinF2H and Gin2′OMT could be
remarkably affected when GinKR1 was knocked down or
overexpressed, and no significant effects were observed in the
GinKR2 groups (Figure S28). Meanwhile, the expression levels
of GinF2H and Gin2′OMT could also affect the expression of
GinKR1 but not that of GinKR2 (Figure S29). These results
indicate that GinKR1, GinF2H, and Gin2′OMT may be
involved in the same biosynthetic pathway.
Next, we reconstituted the biosynthetic pathway to produce
echinatin in tobacco (Figure 3E).41,42 Isoliquiritigenin (7) could
be synthesized by infiltrating AtPAL, AtC4H, At4CL, GinCHS,
and GinCHR into tobacco. The coexpression of GinCHI
increased the production of liquiritigenin (6). Then, GinKR1
was cotransformed to Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV2260
and infiltrated into the leaves of Nicotiana benthamiana. After 7
days of growth, echinatin (1) could be detected in tobacco
leaves (Figure S30).
Structural Mechanisms of GinKR1. To elucidate mech-
anisms for the regioselectivity of GinKR1, we obtained its
crystals, and solved the apo structure (PDB ID: 9M3K) at a
resolution of 2.25 Å by molecular replacement with the help of
AlphaFold3 simulations.43 The structure contains four highly
similar molecules with a root-mean-square deviation (RMSD)
of 1.1 Å. Similar to reported short-chain dehydrogenases/
reductases, each molecule contains an N-terminal domain
(NTD, residues 1−190 and 231−253) and a C-terminal domain
(CTD, residues 191−230 and 254−338), which are responsible
primarily for donor and acceptor binding (Figures 4A, S31, and
S32 and Table S8).
Then, we simulated the GinKR1/NADH complex structure
by AlphaFold3 (Figure S33). Molecular docking yielded the
structural model for GinKR1/NADH/4, which included two
binding modes favoring the reduction of 1″- and 3″-carbonyl
groups, respectively, which were named 1″-O-pose and 3″-O-
pose (Figure 4B). The binding free energies of 4 in GinKR1 for
the 1″-O- and 3″-O-poses are −24.61 ± 4.40 and −21.79 ± 3.78
kcal/mol, respectively, indicating that the 1″-O-pose is more
stable. Thus, GinKR1 is more possible to reduce the 1″-carbonyl
group to form the retrochalcone skeleton. In the 1″-O-pose,
S128, Y163, A129, G130, T208, and Q227 could form hydrogen
bonds with 4, and S205, I222, G226, F164, F292, L192, P190,
P191, and P204 could further stabilize the acceptor through

Next, we simulated the GinKR2/NADH complex structure

Considering that the substrate tunnel may influence the
substrate specificity of enzymes, we predicted the substrate
tunnels of GinKR1 and GinKR2 using CAVER 3.0.44

1″-O-pose tunnel and 3″-O-pose tunnel, while GinKR2 showed

pathway for 4 to enter the pocket at the 3″-O-pose.

K-G/A-M-Q-x-x-S-G) (Figures 5B and S38). Then, we
simulated structures for all the proteins by AlphaFold3 and
confirmed that KR2-type enzymes have a longer α10 helix than
KR1-type enzymes (Figures 5C, S39, and S40).
We further characterized the functions of candidate genes
from three species: Cannabis sativa (CsKR1 and CsKR2),
Hypericum perforatum (HpKR1 and HpKR2), and Humulus
lupulus (HlKR1 and HlKR2). CsKR1, HpKR1, and HlKR1 all
preferred to generate 3, which could be further dehydrated to
retrochalcone 1. Meanwhile, CsKR2, HpKR2, and HlKR2
preferred to produce 9, which was dehydrated to normal
chalcone 10 (Figures 5D, S41, and S42). These results further
confirmed the significance of α10 helix in determining the
regioselectivity of KR1- and KR2-type enzymes.
■DISCUSSION
The concept of retrochalcone was first introduced in 1975, as the
A and B rings of the skeleton were considered to have reverse
biosynthetic origins from those of normal chalcones. They are
empirically recognized according to a special oxygen sub-
stitution pattern. While the biosynthetic study of retrochalcones
could date back to a half century ago, and a possible pathway was
proposed based on isotope labeling experiments, key functional
enzymes have not been reported due to limited understanding
on the inversion mechanisms of the A and B rings. The present
work highlights discovery of the key ketoreductase GinKR1
from G. inflata, and verification of the complete biosynthetic
pathway of echinatin. GinKR1 selectively catalyzes reduction of
the 1″-carbonyl group of 2′-O-methyllicodione to form the
retrochalcone skeleton.
According to our investigations, retrochalcones should
contain no C-2′ and 6′-hydroxyl groups at the A ring, which is
derived from p-coumaroyl-CoA of the shikimate pathway, and
no free hydroxyl groups at the C-2 and 6 positions of the B ring,
which is derived from the polyketide pathway. Moreover, the
carbonyl oxygen of retrochalcones is not derived from the
shikimate pathway or the polyketide pathway but is derived from
the oxygen introduced by the F2H enzyme.
Aside from GinKR1, at least three key enzymes are
indispensable for the biosynthesis of echinatin. GinCHR
removes 6′-OH of naringenin chalcone to generate isoliquir-
itigenin, and GinF2H catalyzes 2-hydroxylation of liquiritigenin
to form the dibenzoylmethane intermediate licodione (5).
Gin2′OMT is critical in methylating the 2-OH of 5. Otherwise,
the open-ring form of 5 would cyclize to form the 2-
hydroxyflavanone tautomer, which will further dehydrate
spontaneously to produce flavones, whose pathway is popular
in higher plants to produce flavone C-glycosides. This is the
reason most retrochalcones contain a 2-OCH3 group. While the
above three enzymes are popular, and we have discovered
GinKR1-like genes in numerous plants, it may be rare that all
these key genes are present in the same species. This deduction
may interpret why retrochalcones have only been reported from
very limited plants.
Although only a limited number of retrochalcones have been
reported from nature, thus far, some of them show noticeable
potential as drug candidates. Echinatin and licochalcone A are
both potent Nrf2 activators and exhibit promising hepatopro-
tective effects. Licochalcone A also possesses significant
antiparasitic, anti-inflammatory, and antitumor activities. This
study paves the way for manufacturing echinatin, licochalcone A,
and other derivatives through de novo biosynthesis and will
improve drug development of these retrochalcones in the future.
