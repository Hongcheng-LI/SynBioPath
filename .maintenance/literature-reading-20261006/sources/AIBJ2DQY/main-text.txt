Efficient Mutasynthesis of “Non-Natural” Antitubercular Ilamycins

ABSTRACT: Ilamycins are a type of cyclopeptide exhibiting
potent antitubercular and cytotoxic activities, which make them
promising leads for antitubercular and antitumor drugs with unique
mechanisms. Mutasynthesis, a strategy utilized to generate natural
product analogues by intercepting the biosynthesis of natural
products with analogues of biosynthetic precursors, offers a distinct
advantage in the eco-friendly and powerful synthesis of “non-
natural” natural products. This study developed the mutasynthesis
of “non-natural” ilamycins by constructing three genetically
engineered strains. Feeding four types of tyrosine-derived
mutasynthons to two biosynthetically simplified mutants resulted
in the production of 18 new ilamycin analogues (7−24), including 16 halogenated and dinitro-containing ilamycin variants (7−22),
along with two previously unreported ilamycin congeners (23 and 24). Most of the generated compounds, particularly the
halogenated ilamycins (13, 16−18), exhibited potent antitubercular activity (MICs ca. 0.30 μM) while showing minimal or no
cytotoxicity (IC50 > 50 μM). Furthermore, the bromine-containing ilamycin derivatives (16−18) demonstrated enhanced
antitubercular activity and lower cytotoxicity compared to those with chlorine, fluorine, or nitro groups, making them promising
candidates for further investigation in antitubercular drug development.
KEYWORDS: cyclicpeptide, halogenated Ilamycin, mutasynthesis, antitubercular activity, non-natural natural product, cytotoxicity
■INTRODUCTION
Tuberculosis (TB) remains a significant infectious disease,
having been a leading cause of adult mortality from infectious
diseases prior to 2020.1 Moreover, the escalating prevalence of
multidrug-resistant TB underscores the urgent need for the
development of novel antituberculosis drugs with distinct
mechanisms. Ilamycins, also known as rufomycins, are a class
of cyclic heptapeptides containing unusual amino acids,
exhibiting selective inhibitory activity against mycobacteria
through binding to a distinct target ClpC1, which makes
ilamycins as potential candidates for a new family of anti-TB
drugs.2−4 Ilamycins were originally isolated from a Streptomyces
strain in 1960s.5 Then, they were reisolated by our group and
other groups from the deep-sea-derived Streptomyces atratus
SCSIO ZH16 in 2017 and S. atratus MJM3502 in 2020 and
2022, respectively.6−8 The biosynthetic gene cluster (BGC) of
ilamycins was independently identified by our group (ila,
MIBiG ID: 1620) and Ohnishi’s group (ruf, MIBiG ID: 1763)
in 2017.6,9 Moreover, our group characterized the functions of
six tailoring enzymes in 2017 and two regulatory genes in 2021
in the biosynthesis of ilamycins.6,10 Among the six tailoring
enzymes, IlaL, a P450 monooxygenase, oxidizes the terminal
methyl group of the N-methyl leucine unit to the carboxylic
acid moiety, and IlaR converts the isopentene double bond
into an epoxy group.6 During the ilamycin biosynthetic
mechanism elucidation, three ilamycin analogues, named
ilamycin E1, ilamycin E2, and ilamycin F, showing strong
antitubercular activity, were discovered from the S. atratus
ΔilaR mutant. Subsequently, a 200 L scale fermentation of the
same mutant strain yielded additional 12 new ilamycin
analogues.11
To pursue ilamycin derivatives with improved antitubercular
activity and drug-like properties, chemical modifications of
ilamycin were systematically carried out by Eli Lilly and
Company12 and our group.13 Among these derivatives, the 3-
nitro-L-tyrosine moiety was mostly retained and no halo-
genated tyrosine-containing ilamycins were reported. More-
over, it is noteworthy that about a quarter of the top 200 best-
selling drugs contain halogen atoms,14 which invoked us to
introduce halogen atoms into the ilamycin structure to

Although total chemical synthesis of ilamycins E and F has
been reported,15 their high cost and low yields resulting from
multistep synthetic routes highlight the need for alternative,
environmentally friendly, and efficient synthesis methods, such
as mutasythesis, for structural modification of ilamycins.
Mutasynthesis involves feeding precursor analogues to a
mutant strain,16,17 representing an eco-friendly approach for
modifying natural products, which has been successfully
applied to diversify various natural products and to generate
thereof serial derivative, such as eight rapamycin analogues, 16
vancomycin analogues, 30 four platensimycin analogues, 20
one prodigiosin analogues, three nosiheptide analogues, and
ten actinomycin D analogues obtained from their respective
mutants.18−23 Our previous study revealed that the biosyn-
thesis of 3-nitro-L-tyrosine is a pretailoring step synergistically

the peptidyl-S-PCP in the assemble line (the giant NRPS IlaS)

Herein, we report (I) the construction of two double-gene
(ΔilaMN, ΔilaLM) and one triple-gene knockout mutant
(ΔilaMNR) strains with simplified metabolic background,
followed by independent feeding of three different 3-
halogenated tyrosine and 3,5-dinitrotyrosine precursors to
these mutants; (II) the isolation and structure elucidation of
16 “non-natural” ilamycin analogues (7−22) and 2 new shunt
products (23 and 24), resulting from precursor feeding to the
genetically engineered mutants; and (III) the evaluation of
antitubercular and cytotoxic activities of these new analogues,
leading to obtain three halogenated antitubercurlar drug leads
with low cytotoxic activities.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Bioinformatic Analysis Indicated that IlaS-A3 Might
Have Relaxed Substrate Specificity. The binding pocket of
adenylation domain (“A” domain) determines its substrate
specificity. To predict the substrate specificity of IlaS-A3, we
aligned its amino acid sequence with those of previously
reported “A” domains that accept either canonical or
noncanonical aromatic amino acids as substrates in non-
ribosomal peptide (NRP) biosynthesis. The amino acids in the
binding pocket of those selected “A” domains were predicted
by the PKS/NRPS Analysis web server.24
The sequence alignment revealed that IlaS-A3 shares
conserved signatures AA1−AA9 (the amino acid residues in
NRPS that are critical to amino acid recognition and
activation) with those in the “A” domains that recognize and
activate 3-chrolo-L-tyrosine, 4-nitro-L-tryptophan, and O-
methyl-L-tyrosine in the biosynthesis of keratinimicin,
thaxtomin, and marformycins, respectively (Figure S1).
Moreover, by comparing the structure of the IlaS-A3 domain
predicted by AlphaFold software with the structure of the
GrsA-A1 domain, the key amino acid-constituted substrate-
binding pocket of IlaS-A3 has a little difference with those in
GrsA-A1 (Figure S2a). And our further analysis found that the
key amino acids in the A3, A5, and A9 motifs of IlaS-A3 are
serine, alanine, and glycine, whereas the respective amino acids
in the A3, A5, and A9 motifs of GrsA-A1 are tryptophan,
isoleucine, and cysteine. The substitution of tryptophan,
isoleucine, and cysteine to small amino acids serine alanine,
and glycine in the respective motif in IlaS-A3 may provide a
larger substrate recognition cavity for 3-nitro-tyrosine (Figure
S2b, Table S4). In the future, mutating the large amino acids in
the substrate-binding pocket of the A-domain to the small
amino acids will help to increase the generality of the substrate
recognition of the A-domain.
Measurement of Substrate Promiscuity of the IlaS-A3
Domain by Feeding Different Tyrosine Analogues to
the ΔilaM Mutant Strain. In our investigation into the
biosynthesis of ilamycins, we observed that feeding of 3-nitro-
L-tyrosine to S. atratus ΔilaM mutant restored the production
of ilamycins.6 The results motivated us to independently feed
four L-tyrosine derivatives (3,5-dinitro-L-tyrosine, 3-fluorine-L-
tyrosine, 3-chlorine-L-tyrosine, and 3-bromine-L-tyrosine) to
the ΔilaM mutant. HPLC analysis of the extracts obtained
from the mutants fed with various 3-substituted-L-tyrosine
analogues indicated that these precursors were utilized by the
mutants to generate new ilamycin analogues, albeit with
reduced efficiency compared with the natural ilamycin
production in S. atratus SCSIO ZH16 (Figure S3). Initially,
we upscaled the fermentation of the fed mutants to 200 mL
media in 1 L flasks in an attempt to isolate the new ilamycin
analogues. However, this approach led to a notable reduction
of the yields. Moreover, the complexity of the metabolite
profiles and the relatively low yields of the new derivatives
posed challenges in their following isolation. Thus, we applied
an alternative strategy to facilitate the isolation of ilamycin
analogues by decreasing the metabolite background of the
producing strain as described below.
Construction of Engineering Strains with a Simplified
Metabolite Background for Efficient Mutasynthesis.
According to our previous studies on the biosynthesis of
ilamycins, we found that there are two peaks in the HPLC
analysis of ΔilaL strain and there are four peaks in the HPLC
analysis of ΔilaR strain compared to at least six peaks in their
wild strain S. atratus SCSIO ZH16. Further isolation and
structural elucidation of the compounds in ΔilaL strain and
ΔilaR strain led us to assign the function of IlaL, oxidizing the
terminal methyl group of the N-methyl leucine unit to the
carboxylic acid moiety, and IlaR, converting the isopentene
double bond into an epoxy group6 (Figure 1D). In addition,
our previous studies on the biosynthesis of the 3-NO2-Tyr unit
of ilamycin also indicated that it was biosynthesized by IlaM
and IlaN in a pretailoring manner, which implied that deletion
of either of the two genes abolished the production of
ilamycins, and the production of ilamycins could be restored in
those mutants by the supplied 3-NO2-Tyr precursor6 (Figure
1B). Thus, based on the function of IlaL, IlaR, and IlaM in the
biosynthesis of ilamycins, we hypothesized that the con-
struction of ΔilaLM and ΔilaMNR mutants would enable
efficient generation of novel ilamycin derivatives by feeding
distinct precursors to these double- or triple-gene knockout
mutants. To validate this hypothesis, we constructed a ΔilaLM
mutant (Figure S4), taking advantage of the closely adjacent
arrangement of ilaL and ilaM genes to facilitate the efficient
production of analogues of ilamycins B1 and B2. To obtain
other “non-natural” ilamycin analogues similar to those in
ΔilaR mutants, we first constructed a ΔilaMN mutant by in-
frame deletion to block the production of 3-nitro-L-tyrosine in
the pretailoring step of ilamycin biosynthesis (Figure S5).
Subsequently, we knocked out ilaR using λ-red recombination,
introducing an apramycin gene cassette to generate the
ΔilaMNR mutant (Figure S6). This mutant was designed
specifically to produce the congeners of ilamycins B1, E1/E2,
and F by feeding various L-tyrosine analogues.

Different Precursors to ΔilaLM and ΔilaMNR Mutants.

metabolite background available, we aimed to provide a
proof of principle. We initially fed 0.5 mM 3,5-dinitro-L-
tyrosine to both ΔilaMNR and ΔilaLM mutants independ-
ently, the aim of which was to test the efficiency of precursor
utilization by the mutants during fermentation in Am2ab
medium at 30 °C for 7−8 days. HPLC analysis showed that
the fermentation extracts of the ΔilaMNR mutant exhibited

Isolation and Structural Elucidation of the “Non-
Natural” Ilamycin Analogues. Based on the above
observations of precursor-feeding experiments, upscale fer-
mentations were performed using a plus feeding method for
precursor feeding, which was fed at two different times (60th
hour and 72nd hour for this study) to reach a final
concentration of 0.5 mM. Each mutant was individually fed
the aforementioned four types of precursors and then
incubated at 30 °C for 7−9 days to produce the ilamycin
derivatives. After upscaling the fermentation and extraction,
chromatography of the mutant extracts yielded 16 new
ilamycin derivatives (7−22), including those with C-3
chlorine, bromine, and fluorine-substituted tyrosine moieties,
along with two new pathway byproducts (23 and 24) and one
known compound ilamycin O (25) (Figure 3).11
The molecular formulas of compounds 7, 12, 16, and 20
were determined to be C54H76N10O12, C54H77ClN8O8,
C54H77BrN8O8, and C54H77FN8O8 on the basis of the positive
or negative HRESIMS ions (Figures S8−S12), respectively.
Comparing the 1D NMR data (Tables S5−S8) of compounds
7, 12, 16, and 20 with that of ilamycin B1 (1) revealed
significant differences in the aromatic resonance region.
Specifically, a pair of chemically equivalent aromatic protons
(δH‑42/H‑46 7.79, δC‑42/C‑46 125.1) was observed in 7, suggesting
the substitution of C-45 with a nitro group, as deduced from
the molecular formula. This was further supported by the
identical chemical shift of the quaternary carbons C-43 and C-
45 (δC‑43/C‑45 133.3), indicating the presence of a dinitro-
substituted tyrosine residue. The aromatic proton splitting
pattern in compounds 12, 16, and 20 resembles that of 1, but
with varying chemical shifts, implying distinct substitutions at
C-43. Through 2D NMR analysis (Figures S37−S40, S61−
S64, and S85−S88), HRMS data, and consideration of the fed
precursors, we assigned 12, 16, and 20 as having chlorine,
bromine, and fluorine atoms at C-43, respectively. This
assignment was reinforced by the 13C chemical shift at C-43,
measuring 121.7 ppm for chlorine, 111.0 ppm for bromine, and
152.2 ppm for fluorine substitutions. These shifts align with
the electronegativity of the respective halogen atoms. The
structures of the other compounds generated in the precursor-
fed fermentation batches were also determined by HRESIMS
and 1D and 2D NMR analyses (Figure 3, Tables S5−S8, and
Figures S8−S100). Diagnostic COSY and HMBC correlations
of the halogenated tyrosine residues in compounds 12−22 can
be seen in Figure 4.
Compounds 23 and 25 were isolated from the ΔilaMNR
mutant fed with different precursors (3-fluorinated-L-tyrosine,
3-chlorinated-L-tyrosine, and 3-brominated-L-tyrosine), while
compound 24 was isolated from the ΔilaLM mutant fed with
aforementioned different precursors. Their molecular formulas,
determined by HRESIMS analysis, are C54H76N8O9 for
compound 23 and C54H78N8O9 and C54H76N8O10 for
compounds 24 and 25 (Figures S24−S26), respectively.
Compound 25 was identified as ilamycin O through a
comparison of its HRESIMS and NMR data (Table S9,
Figures S113 and S114) with our previously reported data.11
Comparing the 1D NMR data of compounds 23 and 24 with
compound 25 revealed that they also featured a tyrosine
residue instead of the 3-nitro-L-tyrosine residue (Table S9,
Figures S101, S102, S107, and S108). The complete structures
of 23 and 24 were deduced through 2D NMR data analysis
(Figure 4) and designated as ilamycin S and T, respectively
(Figures 3, S103−S106, and S109−S112).
Since compounds 23−25 all contain L-tyrosine, we inferred
that IlaS-A3 can utilize L-tyrosine to form ilamycin analogues
when the biosynthesis of 3-nitro-L-tyrosine was disrupted. A
similar phenomenon was observed in the de novo biosynthesis
of “non-natural” thaxtomin phytotoxin, in which a new
thaxtoxin containing unmodified tryptophan was detected in

It is noted that the production of ilamycins O (25), S (23),
and T (24) as well as rufomycin NBZ4 highlighted substrate
promiscuity of IlaS-A3. The utilization of various precursors by
IlaS-A3 suggested the potential of the IlaS-A3 domain for
generating novel cyclic peptides by combinational biosynthesis
or synthetic biology approaches. In addition, the discovery of
new ilamycin derivatives containing a carboxyl group (e.g., 10,
14, 18, 22, and 25) revealed the substrate promiscuity of
cytochrome P450 monooxygenase IlaL. The diverse analogues
of ilamycin B2 produced also demonstrated the relaxed
substrate specificity of cytochrome P450 monooxygenase
IlaR. These results suggested that ilamycins C1/C2 and D
analogues could be generated by an in vitro enzymatic reaction
with IlaR or whole cell transformation of ilaR-containing
engineered strains fed with ilamycins E1/E2 and F analogues as
substrates. Our findings highlighted the relatively broad
substrate specificity of IlaL and IlaR, which contradicts
previous reports indicating their strict substrate specificity.26
We are currently conducting further investigations into the
substrate promiscuity of IlaL and IlaR.
Biological Evaluation of “Non-Natural” Ilamycin
Analogues. Ilamycins are recognized for their excellent
antitubercular and tumor cytotoxic activities. To investigate
the effect of different C-3-substituted tyrosine building blocks
on the bioactivity of ilamycins, the compounds generated
through mutasynthesis in this study were evaluated for
antitubercular and tumor cytotoxic activities using previously
reported methods,27,28 except for fluorinated ilamycin B1 (20),
which was excluded due to its extremely low yield. The
antitubercular activities of those compounds are listed in Table
1. As shown in Table 1, the bioactivity against Mycobacterium
tuberculosis H37Rv varied among the tested ilamycin analogues
from 0.30 to above 20 μM. After evaluating their antitubercular
activity, we proceeded to investigate cytotoxic activity of the
ilamycin derivatives against seven tumor cell lines and one
somatic cell line by the MTT method.28 Results revealed that
the halogenated ilamycins (12−22) displayed weak or no
cytotoxic activity against cell lines with IC50 values above 30
μM. In contrast, the 3,5-dinitro group containing ilamycin
analogues (7−11) exhibited varying cytotoxic activity toward
different cell lines with IC50 values ranging from 8.87 μM to
above 30 μM (Table S10).
From the results of the tested ilamycin analogues’ bioactivity
against M. tuberculosis H37Rv, we can see that most of the
compounds possessing the 6-hydroxy-5-methyl-2-piperidinone
ring (13, 17, 21, and 23, with an exception of compound 8/9)
retained potent antitubercular activity, with an MIC of 0.30
μM, albeit lower than their parent ilamycins E1/E2.6
Furthermore, we observed that brominated ilamycin B1
(ilamycin B1−3, 16, MIC = 0.30 μM) and fluorinated ilamycin
B1 (ilamycin B1−4, 20, MIC = 2.54 μM) showed stronger
antitubercular activity compared to chlorinated ilamycin B1
(ilamycin B1−2, 12, MIC = 9.99 μM). Regarding the

■CONCLUSIONS
In summary, we successfully applied a mutasynthesis approach
to expand the structural diversity of the cyclopeptide ilamycins.
By creating engineered strains (ΔilaLM and ΔilaMNR) with a
simplified metabolic background and feeding them with
various 3-halogenated
L-tyrosine or 3,5-dinitro-L-tyrosine
precursors, we obtained 16 new halogen-containing or
dinitro-containing ilamycin analogues, as well as two previously
unreported ilamycin congeners (ilamycins S and T). SAR
analysis of these ilamycin analogues confirmed the significance
of the 6-hydroxy-5-methyl-2-piperidinone ring, nitro group,
and halogen atoms in the antitubercular activity of ilamycins,
while the dinitro-containing ilamycin analogues displayed
favorable cytotoxic activities. Significantly, the brominated
ilamycins (16, 17, and 18) represent promising antitubercular
drug leads and warrant further development due to their
potent antitubercular activity and minimal cytotoxicity. Our
results also further established the value of the mutasynthesis
approach in expanding of structural diversities of structurally
complex natural products with numerous oxygen-containing
substituents and an abundance of stereochemistry centers.
■METHODS
Reagents and General Experimental Procedures.
Reagents for polymerase chain reactions (PCRs) were
purchased from Takara Co. (Dalian, China) and Trans Gene
Co. (Beijing, China). The plasmid kit and gel extraction kit
were purchased from Omega (Beijing, China). The media used
for fermentation were purchased from Guangdong Huankai
Microbial Technology Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China). Luria−
Bertani (LB) broth used for E. coli cultivation was purchased
from Oxoid Co., Ltd. (Hants, United Kingdom). Organic
solvents for compound isolation and purification were bought
from Guangzhou Chemical Regent Factory (Guangzhou,
China) or J&K Scientific Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). All
chemicals and solvents were of analytical or chromatographic
grade. Unless otherwise indicated, other chemicals were
purchased from standard commercial sources and used without
further purification. All primers and reagents used in this work
were purchased from Sangon Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd.
(Shanghai, China).
Analytical HPLC was performed using an ODS (2) column
(150 mm × 4.6 mm, 5 μm) on an Agilent 1260 HPLC system.
Semipreparative HPLC was performed on an Agilent 1260
HPLC system equipped with a diode array detector (Agilent
Corporation, Santa Clara, CA), using an ODS-A column (10
mm × 250 mm, 5 μm, YMC, Co., Ltd., Kyoto, Japan). High-

Large-Scale Fermentations of Two Mutants Supple-
mented with Different 3-NO2-L-Tyr Analogues: Isolation
and Identification of Compounds 7−25. To obtain
adequate amounts of the ilamycin derivatives for structural
elucidation and bioactivity evaluation, large-scale fermentation
of each mutant with different precursor supplements was
carried out independently. First, a portion of mycelium and
spores of the ΔilaLM or ΔilaMNR mutant was seeded into 50
mL Am2ab medium in a 250 mL flask. After incubation in a
shaker at 28 °C, 200 rpm for 48 h, 5 mL of the culture was
transferred into a 250 mL flask containing 50 mL Am2ab
medium and then cultured at 28 °C and 200 rpm. The
sterilized 3-NO2-L-Tyr analogues were individually pulse-fed
into the culture of the mutants at 60 h (0.3 mM) and 72 h (0.2
mM) separately to achieve a final concentration of 0.5 mM and
neutralized to pH 7.2 with 1 M NaOH each time, followed by
culturing for another 7−8 days. The growth culture was
centrifuged at 3900g for 10 min to yield the supernatant and
mycelium cake. The supernatant and mycelium were
independently extracted by twice volume of butanone and
acetone three times, respectively. The organic extracts were
combined and concentrated to dryness, yielding a syrupy
residue.

Antitubercular Activity Evaluation of 17 New De-
rivatives. Seventeen new compounds (7−24 except 9) were
measured for their minimal inhibitory concentrations (MICs)
against selectable marker-free autoluminescent M. tuberculosis
H37Rv using the previously described method.27 Compounds
7−24 were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and then
serially diluted to final concentrations (μg/mL) of 128−
0.03125 for testing with 7H9 broth (added with 0.2% glycerol
and 10% v/v oleic acid albumin dextrose catalase). MIC values
were measured on days 7 and 14.
Cytotoxic Activities of 16 New Derivatives. Seventeen
new compounds (7−24 except 9 and 20) were tested for their
cytotoxic activities against different normal and tumor human
cell lines, and the MTT assay was used to assess cytotoxicity as
described previously.6,28 Briefly, different tumor cells and
normal cells were seeded in 96-well plates at the appropriate
density and allowed to attach overnight. Drugs at various
concentrations were added to the culture medium and cells
were cultured for another 68 h at 37 °C. MTT (5 mg/mL, 20
μL/well) was then added to the wells for additional 4 h, and
the medium was discarded, followed by adding 150 μL of
DMSO to dissolve the MTT formazan crystals. Absorbance
was measured at 540 nm, with background subtraction at 670
nm using a Model 550 Microplate Reader (Bio-Rad, Hercules,
California). The Bliss method was used to calculate the half
maximal (50%) inhibitory concentration (IC50) values.32 Data
represent the mean ± SD of at least three independent
experiments.
