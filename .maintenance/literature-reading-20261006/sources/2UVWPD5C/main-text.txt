ABSTRACT: Paclitaxel (Taxol) is widely recognized as one of the
most effective natural anticancer drugs, yet its production is limited
by its scarce natural supply and the low yields obtained from its
chemical synthesis. One of the most puzzling steps in its
biosynthesis is the formation of the oxetane-containing D-ring, a
key structural element necessary for its microtubule binding and
anticancer activity. In our study, we use density functional theory
(DFT) calculations to explore how cytochrome P450 drives this
critical transformation. We focus on two proposed reaction
pathwaysthe ene-acetoxy (EA) pathway and the ene-hydroxy
(EH) pathwayand investigate how they operate on both the
high-spin (quartet) and low-spin (doublet) states of Compound I.
Our results indicate that on the high-spin surface, epoxidation
occurs in two steps, with the first step being the slow, rate-limiting part of the reaction, while on the low-spin surface, the process
proceeds in a single step. The computed activation energies and the observed stability of the epoxide intermediates support the idea
that enzyme-catalyzed epoxidation is a pivotal intermediate in the formation of the oxetane ring during Taxol biosynthesis. These
mechanistic insights not only enhance our understanding of this complex biosynthetic pathway but also have the potential to guide
future efforts aimed at improving Taxol production.
■INTRODUCTION
Paclitaxel (Taxol),1 a diterpenoid derived from the Pacific yew
(Taxus brevifolia),2 is one of the most potent natural anticancer
therapeutic agents. Its natural supply is very low compared to
its growing demand, and its chemical synthesis provides
economically unviable yields.3−5 Chemical synthesis remains
challenging, and recent research has therefore focused on
microbial and plant-based biosynthesis, microbial fermenta-
tion, and metabolic engineering to boost Taxol yields.6,7
Innovative drug delivery approaches, such as biomaterial-based
nanoparticles, have also been developed to improve the
targeted delivery of Taxol, enhancing anticancer efficacy
while reducing side effects.8 These advancements highlight
the ongoing interest in understanding and optimizing
biosynthetic pathways, including the critical cytochrome
P450-mediated steps involved in forming the characteristic
oxetane ring of Taxol.9−11
To aid engineering efforts in this direction, it is essential to
understand the various underlying enzymatic steps and
mechanisms involved in paclitaxel biosynthesis. These begin
with the cyclization of geranylgeranyl diphosphate to taxa-
4(5),11(12)-diene catalyzed by taxadiene synthase, to deliver
the three-ring scaffold (Scheme 1: rings A, B, and C). A series
of predominantly oxidation and acetylation−functionalization
follows,12 eventually forming the final ring (the D-ring). The
formation of the oxetane ring is one of the least understood
steps in paclitaxel’s biosynthesis; yet its presence, or that of an
equivalent synthetic moiety,13 has been shown to aid in
microtubule binding and, thus, plays a significant role in the
pharmaceutical activity of paclitaxel.14,15 Moreover, growing
interest in the use of oxetane rings in pharmaceutical chemistry
to create steric bulk and metabolic protection without
changing hydrophobicityreplacing moieties such as gem-

undertaken to investigate the mechanisms of these enzymes.17
Establishing a mechanism for oxetane ring formation in

Several mechanisms have been proposed for D-ring
formation based on experimental data,18,19 generally involving
oxidation of the exocyclic vinyl bond (Scheme 1), with the
dominant hypothesis being that the oxetane ring is formed
from an ene-acetoxy intermediate.18 It is worth noting that
cytochrome P450 monooxygenase (CYP450) enzymes are
known to be involved in multiple reaction mechanisms,
including those aligned with the epoxide ring formation that
would initiate the ene-acetoxy pathway.20,21 The present
understanding is that a CYP450 enzyme catalyzes only the
formation of an epoxide (oxirane) intermediate. The possibility
of CYP450 involvement in the subsequent step, epoxide ring
rearrangement to an oxetane ring, has been relatively
unexplored and is the central focus of this study.
Although the CYP450 enzymes are known to catalyze ring
expansion rearrangements, studied both experimentally and
theoretically,22−25 the epoxide-to-oxetane rearrangements (or
ring expansion) have not been studied. This rearrangement
would be extremely difficult under enzyme-free conditions,26,27
and although it is achievable through organic synthesis, the
yield is very low. Enzymes are known to aid in the stabilization
and biasing of otherwise unfavorable transition states. There-
fore, we explore the plausibility of the involvement of a
CYP450 enzyme in the rearrangement step.
Lastly, Ondari and Walker28 used a hydroxy (OH) group
instead of an acetoxy group on the neighboring carbon (C5) to
propose an alternative pathway for the formation of the
oxetane intermediate. They extensively studied this reaction
route and found that it was approximately 2-fold slower than
the natural ene-acetoxy pathway. No mechanism was proposed
for the parallel ene-hydroxy pathway. In this work, we have
analyzed the mechanism of the ene-hydroxy pathway alongside
the ene-acetoxy pathway and compared their mechanisms.
Our computational study of the mechanism of epoxide
formation supports the enzyme-catalyzed pathway. More
importantly, we have studied the rearrangement from epoxide
to oxetane along three possible alternative pathways: (i)
uncatalyzed, (ii) acid-catalyzed, and (iii) enzymatic. The major
disadvantage of the uncatalyzed and acid-catalyzed mecha-
nisms is that the epoxide intermediate needs to come out of
the active site of the enzyme. In contrast, in the enzyme-
catalyzed mechanism, the reaction can continue at the same
site. The possibility of an enzymatic mechanism is quite
significant, as the conformation of the enzyme is known to be
relatively rigid near the heme group, according to the available
crystal structure of the cytochrome P450 enzyme.29
■COMPUTATIONAL METHODS
Preparation of the Model Systems. Compound 1
(Active Catalyst Species). Extensive computational studies are
available on the cytochrome P450 enzymes.29−46 In general, in
computer modeling of these systems, all the side chains of the
heme group of cytochrome P450 are replaced by H atoms to
model the enzyme’s active site.47 The cysteinate ligand is
modeled either as thiolate (SH−), methyl mercaptide
(SCH3
−), or cysteinate anion (CysS−), among which the
thiolate ligand has been shown to produce results closer to
those obtained using quantum mechanics/molecular mechan-
ics (QM/MM) methods.48 Therefore, in our study, we
modeled the active site by removing all side chain groups
from the porphyrin ring and replacing the axial cysteinate
ligand with a thiolate group (Cpd I, Scheme 2).
Several studies have demonstrated that the CYP450 enzyme
follows multistate reactivity (MSR).49,50 In the literature, two
spin states, namely, doublet (2A2u) and quartet (4A2u), are
generally considered to explain the reaction mechanism
involving CYP450.51−53 de Visser et al. explained that spin
states higher than quartet are comparatively of very high
energy (ref. 29 and the references therein). In this study, we
have also considered two spin states, namely, the quartet as the
high-spin (HS) state and the doublet as the low-spin (LS)
state. The Fe−O and Fe−S bond distances in the iron-oxo
complex (Cpd I) are found to be 1.626 (1.624) and 2.533
(2.539) Å, respectively, for the HS (LS) state, which are very
close to the results obtained from previous theoretical
modeling and experimental findings.54 Note that minor
changes in these bond distances compared to literature values
may occur due to variations in the basis sets used in our
calculations.
Substrate. The substrate (1, Scheme 2) is flexible, with
many possible conformations. To choose the best conforma-
tion for our study, we carried out a molecular dynamics
simulation only for the isolated substrate using the semi-
empirical AM1 method.55 Here, the semiempirical AM1
method was used to generate the MD parameters on the fly.
The simulation of the substrate was carried out as follows: (1)
A simulation with the semiempirical AM1 method was
performed at T = 600 K with an NVT ensemble, and the
trajectory was generated for 20 ps; (2) several snapshots of the
substrate were collected at regular intervals from the trajectory;
(3) these snapshots were minimized with the RI-BP86/def2-
SVP method.56−59 The simulation temperature was set high
(600 K) to facilitate conformation changes by overcoming
larger conformational energy barriers. In the most stable
conformation from our calculations, the reactive ring of the
substrate (ring C, Scheme 2) has the boat form. To reduce the
size of the substrate for computational affordability, we

Theoretical Methods. A modeled version of the substrate
in the presence of the enzyme active site was used for
optimization. All the stationary states for the various reaction
pathways were obtained at the B3LYP/def2-SVP level of
theory.60 For the Fe atom, the LANL2DZ potential was used
for the inner electrons, and the def2-SVP basis sets were used
for the valence electrons.61 The empirical dispersion correction
(DFT-D) was added to properly account for London
dispersion.62−64 The unrestricted formalism was used through-
out this work. Harmonic vibrational frequency calculations
further confirmed the stationary points. The absence of any
imaginary frequency confirmed the ground-state structures
(minima), while the transition states (TS) had only one
imaginary frequency each. The TSs were further analyzed by
optimization along the imaginary mode to ensure that they
correlated with the correct pathway. Often, after the prediction
of an intermediate from a relaxed potential energy surface scan,
a nudged elastic band (NEB) calculation was performed
between the intermediates of the elementary step to get a good
guess of the transition state. The guess structures were then
further optimized to locate the transition state. Detailed
electronic structure analyses, such as natural population
analysis (NPA), natural spin density analysis, and atoms-in-
molecules (AIM) analysis65−67 were carried out for relevant
geometries. The NPA analysis was done to investigate the
presence of substantial charges in the intermediates and TS
structures. A natural spin density analysis was performed to
study the radical character of the intermediate geometries. In
the AIM formalism, bonding interactions are confirmed by
identifying a bond path connecting two atoms and the
corresponding presence of a bond-critical point (BCP)
between them. The Laplacian of electron density at the BCP
∇2ρ(rc) is an important parameter to determine the covalency
between a pair of atoms. A negative value indicates the
accumulation of electron density and thus covalent bonding,
whereas a positive value indicates the depletion of electron
density and thus noncovalent bonding. This simplification
sometimes fails to explain the bonding nature in systems
consisting of heavy atoms; thus, a few other parameters should
be considered for systems with heavy atoms.68−70 Therefore,
other parameters, e.g., kinetic energy density (G(rc)), potential
energy density (V(rc)), electron energy density (H(rc)), and
ratios −G(rc)/V(rc) and G(rc)/ρ(rc), are also calculated at the
bond-critical points. If, in some cases, ∇2ρ(rc) > 0 and H(rc) <
0, the bond can be considered a partial covalent bond, whereas
if H(rc) is positive, then the bond is noncovalent.71 If the
−G(rc)/V(rc) value falls between 0.5 and 1.0 or the G(rc)/ρ(rc)
is less than one, then it indicates a partial covalent
character.72−74
Software. The optimizations were carried out using the
Gaussian 09 program package.75 The NEB calculations were
performed with the ChemShell76−78 software package, utilizing
ORCA80,79 for energy and gradient evaluation. The guessed TS
geometries were then optimized with the Gaussian 09 software
package. The NPA and spin density analyses were done using
the NBO 5.9 software package81 after generating the necessary
files with Gaussian 09. The AIM analysis was performed by the
Multiwfn 3.3.1 software package67 with wave function (.wfn)
files generated by Gaussian 09. The AM1 dynamics were
performed using ChemShell 3.5.

The Reaction Profile. The proposed mechanism for
oxetane formation, shown in Scheme 3, consists of two
steps: CYP450-catalyzed epoxidation of the exocyclic olefin,
followed by rearrangement of the epoxide ring to an oxetane
ring. We have considered two spin states (namely, high-spin
quartet and low-spin doublet) of the active site of CYP450
because it shows multistate reactivity.51 Two pathways
corresponding to the two substratesone with an acetoxy
group (EA) and the other with a hydroxy group (EH)are
discussed side by side to make the comparison easier.
The computational studies83 on the epoxidation catalyzed by
Cpd I have established that the epoxidation on the HS surface
is a two-step process, with the first step being the rate-
determining one. The results of our calculations with the
chosen substrates in this study also reflect the same trend. The
substrate-Cpd I complex of EA and EH in its quartet state4 (1,
I) follows a two-step oxidation process. The activation free
energy for the first step is 8.9 kcal mol−1 for 41 (EA pathway)
and 9.8 kcal mol−1 for 4I (EH pathway), whereas the second
step involves an activation energy of 1.3 kcal mol−1 for 41 and
0.5 kcal mol−1 for 4I.
The epoxidation on the LS surface can, depending on the
substrate, be either a two-step or a single-step process.83 In our
case,2 (1, I) follows a single-step pathway for epoxide
formation. An optimization starting from a point along the
imaginary mode of the TS directly led to the epoxide
intermediate2 (3, III), further supporting the single-step
mechanism. The activation energy value is 10.0 kcal mol−1
for both the EA and the EH pathways (Figures S1 and S2).
Therefore, both the substrates (A(EA) and A(EH)) follow the
single-step mechanism similar to that of trans-2-butene, 1,3-
cyclohexadiene, and 1,4-cyclohexadiene, etc.83
The resulting epoxide intermediates are relatively stable in
energy compared to the substrate−Cpd I complex (2I3:
−43.29;
2IIII:-42.2 kcal mol−1), i.e., (i) the formation of
epoxide is exergonic and hence favorable, and (ii) the
activation energies are relatively low for a reaction in a
biological environment. These observations further support the
existing proposal that the enzyme-catalyzed formation of
epoxide is an intermediate step in the formation of the D-ring
in Taxol biosynthesis. We have also carried out the epoxidation
in the ene-acetoxy pathway with the full substrate along with
the cysteine residue representing the proximal ligand. This
extended model contains 125 atoms, almost double the size of
the minimal model (66 atoms). We computed the reaction
profile for this larger model on both the high-spin and low-spin
surfaces of the reaction profile, and the results are presented in
Figure S3. The overall energetic trend and mechanistic
pathway observed in the larger cluster model remain consistent
with those obtained from the minimal model. This suggests
that the key features of the reaction mechanism are captured
well even in the reduced model. Therefore, we can safely
proceed with the reduced model of the system for our study.
The next step, namely, the rearrangement from epoxide to
oxetane that completes the D-ring formation, is more
interesting. Here, we consider a novel proposal suggesting
that the substrate continues the reaction in the same enzyme
pocket rather than leaving the enzyme to undergo further acid-
catalyzed ring expansion.
The interest of the research community in the oxetane ring
formation in paclitaxel biosynthesis can be gauged by some
recent reports;84,85 the biosynthesis of the oxetanes itself
similarly continues to be relevant.86 Although there is plenty of
literature on enzyme-catalyzed epoxide formation49,52,87−92
and some recent studies84−86 utilize density functional theory
(DFT) calculations to elucidate the enzymatic mechanisms
underlying oxetane formation, this present report may,
however, be the first elaborate computational study. It includes
detailed geometric and spin density analyses of the various
species involved in both the high-spin (quartet) and the low-
spin (doublet) surfaces of the enzyme-catalyzed ring expansion
from epoxide to oxetane, vis-à-vis their acid-catalyzed (Figure
S4) as well as the uncatalyzed (Figure S5) reaction profiles.
In the ring expansion process, the O10 (the epoxide oxygen
coordinated to the Fe) migrates to C7 (Figure 3), and the
acetoxy (on the EA pathway) or the hydroxy (on the EH
pathway) group migrates from C7 to C2, the internal carbon
that is part of the epoxide ring. We can envisage several
possibilities for the entire process, namely., ring expansion
followed by migration of OAc or OH, migration of OAc or OH
followed by ring expansion, or simultaneous ring expansion
and migration. The OAc migration, too, has two possibilities:
(i) the oxygen (O8) that is bonded with C7 gets bonded to C2
(probably through a three-membered transition state) as well,
or (ii) the other oxygen (O9) forms a bond with C2, while the
O8−C7 bond breaks. Among all these possibilities, we discuss
the pathway that we could characterize as the minimum energy
pathway, with the location of the transition state and the
optimization of the minimum energy structures along the path.
Along the pathway that we eventually characterized, the first
step of the rearrangement from epoxide to oxetane starts with
the attack of the O9 atom on C2 (see Scheme 2 for atom
numbering and Scheme 3 for the reaction scheme). The
activation free energy required for epoxide ring opening is
higher than the epoxidation barrier; the barrier is 27.3 kcal
mol−1 for the HS surface and 22.3 kcal mol−1 for the LS surface
along the EA pathway. On the EH pathway, the barriers are
30.6 kcal mol−1 for HS and 22.1 kcal mol−1 for LS (Figure 1).

Another recent experimental and computational study93
reports a completely different reaction pathway for oxetane
formation compared to those previously proposed. Jiang et
al.93 identify a new enzyme, taxane oxetanase, and investigate a
reaction mechanism that directly leads to the oxetane
intermediate, circumventing the involvement of an epoxide
intermediate. Their study reports a reaction energy barrier of
approximately 25 kcal mol−1 for the rate-determining step. In
contrast, our study reports a reaction energy barrier of less than
25 kcal mol−1, suggesting an alternative and potentially
competitive reaction pathway that may exist in plants.
Willenbring and Tantillo reported a proton-catalyzed
reaction for the oxetane formation from the epoxide
intermediate.19 They have shown that the first step in the
proton-catalyzed pathway is effectively barrierless, while the
second involves a high activation energy (25.6 kcal mol−1). We
studied the proton-catalyzed pathway for the ene-acetoxy and
ene-hydroxy substrates. In our case, the optimization of the
structure that includes the proton attached to the epoxy
oxygen leads to the intermediate structure (Figure S4).
Therefore, only the second step was investigated for locating
the TS. The activation energy is found to be 28.9 kcal mol−1
for the EA pathway, which is higher than that for the enzyme-
catalyzed pathway on its LS surface (22.3 kcal mol−1). On the
ene-hydroxy surface, surprisingly, the proton-catalyzed path-
way has an activation free energy of 19.7 kcal mol−1, which is
lower by ∼2 kcal mol−1 than the 22.1 kcal mol−1 observed for
the LS surface of the enzymatic pathway. Thus, the 4-fold
decrease in the rate of EH does not have an explanation based
on the activation energies associated with the pathways (Figure
4).
Characterization of the Transition States. The doublet
TS for the epoxidation step (2TS1−3) the EA pathway contains
small radical character (C1: −0.29; O10: −0.26, Table 1), and
the two C−O bond distances are different; the O bond is
closer to the terminal C, which is less crowded. The TS is
relatively early, with both C−O bond distances more than 2 Å
(Figure 2). The geometrical parameters and the radical nature
of the transition states in both the EH and EA pathways are
similar (Table 1 and Figure 2). As Cpd I gets reduced and the
substrate gets oxidized during the formation of the epoxide, the
interaction between the epoxide and the Fe center is expected
to be nonbonded. QTAIM analysis was performed on the
epoxide intermediates (2I3 and 2IIII, Table S2) to understand
the nature of the interaction; the topological parameters
indeed indicate a nonbonding interaction. As the difference in
partial charge between the epoxide O (O10) and the Fe atom
is reasonably high (|e|O10 = −0.62, |e|Fe = 1.29 in 2I3; |e|O10
= −0.63, |e|Fe = 1.28 in 2IIII), the interaction is electrostatic, as
shown by the results of our calculations (Table S2).
The first epoxidation step on the HS surface involves the
formation of a bond between O10 and C1. The TSs (4TS1−2a
and
4TS1‑IIa, Figure 2) show a relatively shorter C1−O10
distance (in comparison to that of C2−O10), which is similar
for both the EA and EH pathways, in accordance with the
formation of the C1−O10 bond. The following intermediates
(4I2a and 4IIIa) have significant radical character, which can be
correlated with the spin density values on the C2 atom (4I2a, IIa
= 0.939). This radical intermediate further converts to the
epoxide via an early transition state (4TS2a‑3 and 4TSIIa‑III). The
NPA charges and the AIM (Tables S2 and S3) show that the
O10−Fe interaction in the epoxide intermediates is electro-
static, similar to that observed for the LS surface.

The second step of the rearrangement of the EA pathway
involves the bond formation between the O10 and C7 atoms
and the breaking of the bond between the C7 atom and O8. In
this step, the 4TS4−5 is 12.3 kcal mol−1 higher in energy than
the 2TS4−5. Similarly, the TS on the HS surface in the EH
pathway (4TSIV−V) is 14.7 kcal mol−1 higher in energy than the
TS on the LS surface. Visual inspection reveals that the two
geometries differ only by the orientation of the SH bond on
the EA pathway (opposite to one another), and only the Fe−S
and Fe−O10 bonds differ by more than 0.1 Å. In the EH
pathway, the two TSs on the HS and LS surfaces are alike,
except that the orientation of the SH bond is opposite.
Therefore, the reason for the energy difference may be due to
the orbital occupancy in the two different spin states (Figure
6).
Thermodynamically, the epoxidation is favorable on the LS
surface, as the ΔG1−3 value is 5.8 kcal mol−1 lower than that on
the HS surface in the EA pathway. Similarly, the ΔGI−III on the
LS surface is 3.7 kcal mol−1 lower than that on the HS surface
in the EH pathway. Although the activation energies are similar
for both the EA and EH pathways, the large thermodynamic
stability makes the LS surface more preferable compared to the
HS surface for epoxidation. Also, in the EH pathway, the LS
surface is thermodynamically more favorable for the oxetane
formation from the epoxide (ΔGIII−V= = −14.2 kcal mol−1)
compared to the EA pathway (ΔGIII−V = −3.9 kcal mol−1).
Therefore, oxetane formation is favorable on the LS surface.
Considering the entire reaction pathway as the combination
of two separate processes (epoxidation and rearrangement), it
is very difficult to predict which, between the EA and the EH
pathways, would be a better choice for the system to follow. As
seen from the previous discussion, the preferred LS surface is
unable to provide any distinctly preferable choice between the
EA and the EH pathways based on the activation energy as
well as the thermodynamic energy values.
■CONCLUSIONS
We investigated the CYP450-catalyzed enzymatic pathway for
the rearrangement step in the formation of the oxetane
intermediate. The well-studied epoxidation pathway, catalyzed
by the enzyme active site, shows no preference in terms of
energy between the HS and LS surfaces for both the ene-
acetoxy and the ene-hydroxy pathways. However, for the
rearrangement step, the LS surface is clearly more favorable in
both cases when compared to the HS surface in terms of
activation energies. Because a proton in the substrate binding
site of cytochrome P450 is not easily accessible, the enzyme-
catalyzed pathway is the most suitable for oxetane formation.
From the activation energy data of the LS surface, it is clear
that there is no apparent preference between the ene-acetoxy
and ene-hydroxy pathways for the rearrangement step.
The TSs and intermediates for the rearrangement steps are
characterized computationally. It is shown that the model
active site can effectively act as a Lewis acid to lower the
activation energy barrier of the reaction. In all cases, a large
partial negative charge on the O10 atom is shown to be
neutralized by the large partial positive charge of the Fe center
in the model active site.
