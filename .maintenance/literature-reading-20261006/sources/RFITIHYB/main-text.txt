28
29   12          Polyketide C-methylation occurs during a programmed sequence of dozens of
30
31   13   reactions carried out by multidomain polyketide synthases (PKSs). Fungal PKSs
32
33
     14   perform these reactions iteratively, where a domain may be exposed to and act upon34
35
36   15   multiple enzyme-tethered intermediates during biosynthesis. We surveyed a collection
37
38   16    of C-methyltransferase (CMeT) domains from non-reducing fungal PKSs to gain insight
39
40     17    into how different methylation patterns are installed. Our in vitro results show that41
42
43   18   control of methylation resides primarily with the CMeT, and CMeTs can intercept and
44
45   19   methylate intermediates from non-cognate non-reducing PKS domains. Furthermore,
46
47     20   the methylation pattern is likely imposed by a competition between methylation or
48
49
50   21   ketosynthase-catalyzed extension for each intermediate. Understanding site-specific
51
52   22   polyketide C-methylation may facilitate targeted C-C bond formation in engineered
53
54   23   biosynthetic pathways.

     25          Polyketide synthases (PKSs) have been long-standing targets for engineering6
7
8    26   biosynthetic pathways because of the inherent structural and stereochemical complexity
9
10   27    built into their products.1 PKSs tether growing intermediates to the enzyme by
11
12
     28    thioesterification to the phosphopantetheine arm of a carrier protein that delivers the13
14
15   29   intermediates to client domains for successive rounds of elongation and modification.
16
17   30   The core reaction—ketosynthase-catalyzed C-C bond formation by decarboxylative
18
19     31   Claisen condensation—extends the intermediate by two carbons each cycle. However,
20
21
22   32  PKSs occasionally implement a secondary C-C bond forming reaction through
23
24   33    regiospecific -methylation by an S-adenosylmethionine (SAM)-dependent C-
25
26     34   methyltransferase (CMeT) embedded in the PKS.27
28
29   35        Type I highly-reducing PKSs (HR-PKSs) found in both marine cyanobacteria and
30
31   36    fungi, modular and iterative systems respectively, can methylate polyene -ketoacyl
32
33
     37   substrates as in curacin and lovastatin biosynthesis, through use of a PKS-embedded34
35
36   38   CMeT.2, 3 In both examples, multiple -ketoacyl species of different chain length could
37
38   39   be presented to the CMeT, but only a single regiospecific methylation is observed.
39

19    240   give rise to diastereomers—demonstrating non-native methylation following the final
20
21
22  241   extension.
23
24  242      We propose that the kinetic balance of extension and methylation determines the
25
26  243   observed methylation pattern. As with LovB, different domains may have catalytic
27
28
    244    efficiency towards different substrates such that only the programmed outcome is29
30
31  245    kinetically favorable. At low concentrations of CMeT, not all programmed methylations
32
33  246   can occur before the KS performs another extension generating an off-path
34
35
    247   intermediate. At high concentration, the selectivity against positions that are not part of36
37
38  248   the “methylation program” can be overcome by increasing the overall rate of
39
40  249   methylation. These experiments cannot replicate the kinetic advantage found in an
41
42    250    intact NR-PKS, where the effective concentrations of acyl-ACP and client domains are
43
44
45  251   high. It is also possible that other conformational changes transmitted within an intact
46
47  252  NR-PKS could influence control of methylation. The primary determinant of the
48
49  253   methylation pattern, however, appears to be the CMeT domain itself, in addition to
50
51
    254   surveillance by the KS.52
