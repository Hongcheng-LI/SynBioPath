Biosynthesis of AS2077715 and Funiculosin: Pathway Reconstitution
and Identification of Enzymes that Form the All-cis
Cyclopentanetetraol Moiety

ABSTRACT: The complete biosynthetic pathways of the potent antifungals AS2077715 (1) and funiculosin (2) are reconstituted
and characterized. A five-enzyme cascade, including a multifunctional flavin-dependent monooxygenease and a repurposed O-
methyltransferase, is involved to perform the dearomatization, stereoselective ring contraction, and redox transformations to morph a
hydroxyphenyl-containing precursor into the unusual all-cis cyclopentanetetraol moiety.
R
ing contraction reactions to modify the carbon skeletons
of molecules is an important strategy in total synthesis of
natural products and medicinal chemistry.1 One of the most
desirable, yet challenging ring contractions is oxidative phenol
contraction to break the robust aromatic ring.2 Oxidative
dearomatization and contraction of aromatic rings under high
temperature or with a metal catalyst have been reported,3,4 but
regio- and stereocontrol of the reaction are difficult to achieve.
Enzymatic dearomatization has received increased attention in
recent years as a biocatalytic tool.5,6 These enzymes, often
discovered in natural product biosynthetic pathways, can
display a promiscuous substrate scope and exquisite
regioselectivity and stereoselectivity.7−10 Proposed biosyn-
thetic pathways of oxidative ring contractions include those
for xenovulene A,11 terrein,12 cryptosporiopsin,12 aflatoxin
B1,13 and kinamycin,14 etc. (Figure 1A). Uncovering the
enzymatic basis for such transformation can improve our
understanding of Nature’s chemical logic and expand the
biocatalytic toolbox.
A remarkable example of aromatic dearomatization and ring
contraction is seen in the fungal natural products AS2077715
(1) and funiculosin (2), isolated from Capnodium sp. 33985515
and Penicillium funicolosum,16 respectively. Both compounds
show significant antifungal activity by inhibiting mitochondrial
cytochrome bc1 complex.17,18 The defining structural feature of
1 and 2 is the all-cis cyclopentanetetraol moiety that is
connected to the 5-position of a N-methyl-3-pyranyl-4-
hydroxy-2-pyridone. To date, total syntheses of 1 and 2 have
not been reported.19,20 The biosynthetic gene cluster (BGC)
for either compound has not been characterized either.
Examining the structure of 2, the pyridone and pyran portions
of the molecule are nearly identical to that found in the known
mycotoxin (−)-sambutoxin (3) (Figure 1B).21 The 5-position
of the pyridone of 3 is substituted with a para-hydroxyphenol
that is formed after hydroxylation of a phenylalanine-derived
phenyl ring.22 Therefore, we hypothesize that oxidative
dearomatization and ring contraction take place to convert 3

The strains Capnodium sp. (strain number: TTI-000886)
and Talaromyces coalescens (strain number: TTI-000630) from
the Texas Therapeutics Institute (TTI) collection produce 123
and 2 (Supplemental Methods, Table S5, Figures S20−S25),

TTI-000886 was confirmed by X-ray crystallography.23 To
search for responsible BGCs, we used the previously
characterized smb BGC,22 which encodes the genes smbA-G
required for biosynthesis of 3, to query the genomes of both
TTI strains (Figure 2A). We identified the cap and fun BGCs
as candidates for biosynthesis of 1 and 2, respectively (Figure
2A). Each BGC encodes well-conserved homologues of the
smb genes (capA-G and funA-G), as well as tailoring enzymes
that may be involved to afford 1 or 2 (Table S3). In the cap

based on the mechanism of 1.17
We first cultured TTI-000886 with L-Phe supplement to

titers compared to 1 (Figure 2C, (i). 4 is the analog to 3 and is
the likely intermediate prior to oxidative ring contraction
(Table S10 and Figures S47−S51). Heterologous expression of
capA-G in Aspergillus nidulans A1145 ΔSTΔEM24 indeed
afforded 4 (Figure 2C, (ii). The intermediates leading to 4
were characterized (Tables S6−S9, Figure S1 and S26−S46). 9
and 12 contain the identical pyridone-tetrahydropyran
connectivity as seen in 1 and 4, but the 5-hydroxyphenol is

S58−S63) and cyclopentanetriol (Table S16 and Figures S81−
S86) in 12. Both molecules are likely biosynthetic
intermediates of 1, with 12 being the penultimate product.

To confirm the additional cap enzymes can transform 4 to 1,
we expressed CapH-L in Saccharomyces cerevisiae RC0125 and
fed 4. Gratifyingly, 4 was transformed into 1, along with 9 and
12 as minor products (Figure 2C, (iv). In addition, we
detected the presence of two additional compounds 5 and 6
(Figure 2C, (iii), with MW increases of +16 and +32 compared
to that of 4, respectively. Isolation of 5 was not successful as
the compound readily oxidized and degraded. 6 was purified
and characterized to be the 3′,4′,5′-trihydroxyphenyl derivative
of 4 (Table S11 and Figures S52−S57). We inferred that 5 is
most likely the o-hydroquinone intermediate (Figure 2B). In
parallel, we expressed funH-L and funM in RC01. Upon
feeding of 3, 2 was produced and confirmed to be funiculosin.
These studies established the associations between the cap and
fun BGCs with 1 and 2, respectively; and identified enzymes
that are involved in the oxidative ring contraction of 4 and 3.
We then tested individual protein functions using RC01 as a
biotransformation host, as well as using either purified proteins
expressed from E. coli (CapJ-L) (Figure S3) or yeast
microsomal fractions containing membrane proteins (CapH
and CapI). First, 5 and 6 were observed when 4 was fed to
RC01 expressing FMO CapL (Figure S4). This is consistent
with known functions of flavoproteins in oxidizing hydrox-
ylphenyl moieties.26 Using selected ion-monitoring at m/z ([M
+ H]+) = 448, we observed two additional compounds in this
transformant (Figure S5). One compound coeluted with 9,
while the other compound 10 was formed at a higher titer. We
performed large scale biotransformation to purify and
characterize 10 (Supplemental Methods). A combination of
NMR, chemical derivatization, circular dichroism (CD)
exciton chirality analysis, and electronic CD calculation
(Tables S13−S14, Figures S6 and S64−S74) led to the
assignment of 10 as the trans-4′,5′-diol cyclopentenone as
shown in Figure 2B. In vitro assays of CapL with 4 afforded 5
and 6 as major products, with 9 and 10 detected as minor
products (Figure 2D, (i). Direct assay of 6 with CapL also led
to the formation of 9 and 10 (Figure 2D, (ii), indicating 6 is
the precursor.
Interestingly, when 4 was supplemented to RC01 expressing
both CapL and the putative O-MT CapK, we observed
emergence of 9 as the dominant diastereoisomer of the
cyclopentenonediol, with the level of 10 significantly
attenuated (Figure S5). This suggests CapK may function
with CapL in controlling the stereochemical outcome of the
ring contraction step. CapK is predicted to be an O-MT,
although no O-methylation reaction is required based on the
structure of 9. The general base histidine conserved in all O-
MT is also present in CapK.27 Recombinant CapK purified
from E. coli retained ∼90% of S-adenosylmethionine (SAM)
(Figure S7). When 6 was incubated with both CapK and
CapL, we observed near exclusive formation of 9 with only
traces amounts of 10 (Figure 2D, (iii). Adding only CapK with
6 led to no reaction (Figure S5), suggesting CapK must
function on a further oxidized product of 6.
As a result, we reexamined the products of the in vitro
reaction between CapL and 6 (Figure 2D, (ii). One unstable
compound 8 with MW of 491, which is 32 mu higher than that
of 6 was detected. We were able to isolate 8 from the reaction
at earlier time points, but full characterization was hampered
by the spontaneous conversion to 9 and 10 (major) (Figures
2D, (iv) and S8). These results point to 8 being an CapL-
catalyzed oxidation product of 6, and can nonenzymatically
ring contract to form 9 and 10. To test whether 8 is the
substrate of CapK, freshly separated 8 was immediately added
to reaction mixtures containing 3 mL sodium phosphate buffer
(pH 7.6) with or without 1 μM CapK. The mixtures were
extracted by EtOAc after 10 min and analyzed. In the presence
of CapK, 8 was nearly all converted to 9 with only traces of 10
(Figure 2D, (v). Time course experiments with 0.2 μM CapK
and 8 were performed with EtOAc quenching at 15 s, 30 s, 1
min, and 5 min (Figure S9). Data from this experiment
confirmed CapK significantly accelerates the consumption of 8.
Collectively, these data show that CapL is a multifunctional
flavoenzyme that can solely oxidize the p-hydroxyphenyl in 4
to 8, which can nonenzymatically form the cyclopentenonediol
in 9 and 10; while the predicted O-MT CapK is required to
control the stereoselective conversion of 8 to 9, thereby
establishing the cis-diol product that is on pathway to 1.
Although full structure determination of 8 was unsuccessful,
we proposed it is a tautomeric mixture of the lactone and the
ring-opened aldehyde-carboxylate as shown in Figure 2B.28
This proposal is based on (i) its MW and the proposed
mechanism of FMO (Figure S2); (ii) results from the L-Phe
(ring-D5) feeding studies in which two ring deuterium atoms
were retained in 9 (as a result, in 8 as well). We propose that
the deuteriums at positions 2′ and 6′ are retained; and (iii) the
required chemical transformation by CapK to set two
stereocenters in 9. A proposed mechanism for the formation
of 6 to 8 via an intermediate 7 is shown in Figure 2B.29 We
propose the C4′−C5′ bond in 7 is oxidatively cleaved to
generate the aldehyde-carboxylate 8 (Figure S2). CapK, a
decoy O-MT homologue, then catalyzes the decarboxylative,
syn-selective aldol reaction to extrude C5′ as CO2, and forms a
new C4′−C6′ bond in 9 via a nucleophilic vinylogous
enolate.30−33 This reaction can happen nonenzymatically
from 8 without CapK,34 but is not stereoselective and forms
both 9 and 10, as well as other possible diastereomers that can
be detected by MS but cannot be isolated due to very low
amounts.
Feeding of 9, but not 10, to RC01 expressing predicted DHs
CapI and CapJ, and P450 CapH, led to the formation of 1 and
12 (Figure 2E, (i). CapI and CapJ likely catalyze sequential
stereoselective two-electron reductions of the cyclopentenone
in 9 to the all-cis cyclopentanetriol in 12. Although both are
annotated as DHs, the protein sequences differ significantly.
CapI is predicted to be a transmembrane protein (Figure S10)
with similarity to steroid 5α-reductase DET2,35 while CapJ
belongs to the Gfo/Idh/MocA family (Figure S10), which
catalyzes the redox reaction of hydroxyl or ketone groups.36
Feeding of 9 to RC01 expressing either CapI or CapJ alone led
to reduced products 11 or 13, respectively. 13 was structurally
characterized to be the cyclopentenetriol product (Table S17
and Figures S87−S92), which is in line with predicted function
of CapJ. However, 13 is a shunt product as further reduction
by CapI to 12 cannot be observed using either biotransforma-
tion or purified yeast microsomes containing CapI (Figure
S12). In contrast, CapI-containing microsomes were able to
reduce 9 to 11 (Figure 2E, (ii). Isolation of 11 was challenging
as the conversion was low and 11 appeared to be unstable
during biotransformation conditions. To verify the identity of
11, 9 was chemically hydrogenated with H2 over Pd/C, which
led to formation of a reduced product with the same retention
time and MW as 11, and was characterized to be the ketone
product (Table S15 and Figures S75−S80). The intermediacy

In conclusion, we identified the biosynthetic pathways to 1
and 2. Formation of the all-cis cyclopentanetetraol is
remarkable considering it is derived from the phenyl ring of
phenylalanine.22 The cap and fun pathways deploy a barrage of
one- and two-electron redox enzymes, as well as a repurposed
methyltransferase enzyme, to perform the stereoselective
oxidative ring contraction steps.
