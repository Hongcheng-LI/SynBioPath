24 Short Summary

25 In this study, we presented the chromosome-level genome assembly for the
26 Himalayan yew and revealed that tandem duplication was a driving force of the
27 evolution of Taxol biosynthetic pathway in the yew genome, which not only clustered
28 90% the known genes in the pathway on the same chromosome, but also provided a
29 plentiful of genetic resources for nature to shape this complex pathway. The reference
30 genome of Taxus would be a great platform for decoding the complete biosynthetic
31 pathway of Taxol and other taxoids.

33 Abstract

34 Taxus, commonly known as yew, is a well-known gymnosperm with great

35 ornamental and medicinal value. Here by assembling a chromosome-level genome of

36 the Himalayan yew (Taxus wallichiana) with 10.9 Gb in 12 chromosomes, we

37 revealed that the driving force of gene family evolution in the genome was tandem

38 duplication, that not only clustered the main genes of taxadiene synthase, P450s and

39 transferases for paclitaxel biosynthesis on the same chromosome, but also provided

40 genetic resources for the nature to sculpture the core structure of taxoids at different

41 positions and subsequently established the complex pathway of paclitaxel by

42 neofunctionalization. We also confirmed two isoenzymes of the known genes in

43 paclitaxel biosynthetic pathway from the cluster. The reference genome will serve as a

44 platform for decoding the complete biosynthetic pathway of paclitaxel and

45 understanding the chemodiversity of taxoids in gymnosperm.

50 Introduction

51 The seed plants mainly include one angiosperms lineage (flowering plants) and

52 four gymnosperm lineages. Gymnosperms diverged from the sister clade of flowering

53 plants about 300 million years ago (MYA) (Stewart et al., 1993). Different from the

54 flowering plants, which expanded around the world with more than 352,000 species,

55 gymnosperms only retained about 1000 species in the four extant lineages (Biswas

56 and Johri, 2013). Polyploidy or whole genome duplication (WGD) plays a critical role

57 in the evolutionary history of flowering plants, however, there is no detectable WGD

58 event in the sequenced gymnosperms after the divergence from flowering plants (Li et

59 al., 2015; Nystedt et al., 2013; Wan et al., 2018). It seems that gymnosperms drove

60 into a completely different way from the flowering plants. However, the distinct

61 driving forces of gymnosperms during evolutionary processes is still an enigma.

62 Taxus is a well-known gymnosperm due to the production of Taxol (paclitaxel)

63 (Baloglu and Kingston, 1999; Danishefsky et al., 1996; Nicolaou et al., 1993; Wani et

64 al., 1971), one of the most successful anticancer drugs in the past 30 years (Malik et65 al., 2011). More than 500 taxoid compoundsPre-proofhave been isolated from different species

66 in Taxus (Schneider et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2011), which have been developed to

67 novel applications in clinical therapy for many diseases (Ganem and Franke, 2007).

68 Until now, the total market of taxoids has reached to above $1 billion per year

69 (Nazhand et al., 2020). However, the extremely low content of taxoids in yew tree

70 (like paclitaxel only 0.001-0.05% w/w) has been the limited factor for the further Journal
71 development of taxoids (Nicolaou et al., 1994; Wani and Horwitz, 2014). Recent

72 years, more and more efforts have been devoted to metabolic engineering and

73 synthetic biology for developing alternative production systems in heterologous hosts

74 (Ajikumar et al., 2010; Howat et al., 2014; Li et al., 2019; Nazhand et al., 2020; Zhou

75 et al., 2015), but it was obstructed by the complex biosynthesis pathway, requiring

76 about 19 enzymatic steps from the precursor geranylgeranyl diphosphate (GGPP)

77 (Chau and Croteau, 2004b). Untill now, 13 enzymes in the pathway have been

78 characterized, including a taxadiene synthase (TS) which catalyzes the cyclization of

79 GGPP into taxadiene (Wildung and Croteau, 1996), five P450s (T5αOH, T10βOH,

80 T2αOH, T7βOH, T13αOH) which catalyze a series of hydroxylation on taxane

81 skeleton (Chau and Croteau, 2004a; Chau et al., 2004; Jennewein et al., 2004;

82 Jennewein et al., 2001; Schoendorf et al., 2001; Zhang et al., 2014), five

83 acyltransferases (TAT, DBAT, TBT, BAPT, DBTNBT) which further modify the

84 taxane skeleton (Walker and Croteau, 2000b; a; Walker et al., 2002b; Walker et al.,

 94 Results and discussion

 95 Genome sequencing and analysis

 96 To explore the genome sequence of T. wallichiana (Figure S1), we first generated

 97 ~1 Tb Illumina Hiseq sequencing data and estimated the genome size of 10.6 Gb

 98 (Table S1 and Figure S2). Then, 869.8 Gb Nanopore sequencing reads (~85× coverage)

 99 and 1077.1 Gb Hi-C reads (~100 × coverage) were generated for chromosome-scale

100 assembling (Tables S2-S3). We obtained a total size of 10.9 Gb high quality genome

101 containing 12 chromosomes (contig N50 of 8.6 Mb and scaffold N50 of 987 Mb)

102 (Figure 1, Figure S3 and Tables S4-S5). By integrating ab initio predicted genes,

103 homology predicted genes and transcriptomic data from the leaves and barks of T.

104 wallichiana together (Materials and Methods), we predicted 44,008 protein-coding

105 genes and 2,796 non-coding RNA genes (i.e., 505 microRNAs (miRNA), 689

106 ribosomal RNAs (rRNA), 1,090 transfer RNAs (tRNA), and 512 small nuclear RNA

107 (snRNA)) in the genome (Tables S7-S8). 85% core genes of 1,614 embryophyta could

108 be mapped into the genome by BUSCO (Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy109 Orthologs) evaluation (Table S9),Pre-proofindicating that the genome assembly of T.

110 wallichiana is reasonably complete (Figure S4). 95% protein-coding genes have

111 significant hits in the functional annotation databases (i.e., InterPro, GO: Gene

112 Ontology, KEGG: Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes, and so on) (Table

113 S10).

114 In the T. wallichiana genome, 85% sequences are repetitive DNA elements, Journal
115 mainly including 77% long terminal repeat retrotransposons (LTR-RTs) (Figure S5,

116 Table S6). However, except for 53% Gypsy and 10% Copia LTR-RTs, we also found

117 about 14% ambiguous LTR-RTs. According to sequence similarity analysis, these

118 ambiguous LTR-RTs could be classified into 2,527 different groups, which might be

119 the evolutionary relics of the known LTR-RTs without activity (Ou and Jiang, 2018).

120 Indeed, most of LTR-RTs are incomplete based on structure analysis, and the

121 solo-LTR (the absence of any adjacent internal region of the corresponding

122 retroelement) to intact-LTR ratio (5.5:1) is obviously higher in T. wallichiana than

123 other gymnosperm species (Wan et al., 2018) (Figure S5). Based on the estimated

124 insertion time of LTRs (Figure S6), 90% of intact-LTRs were generated very recently





 5

[PDF page 7]
125 (<50 Million years ago), indicating that the ancient LTR-RTs have been lost or

126 mutated to solo-LTRs. These results indicated that T. wallichiana continued to

127 eliminate repetitive sequences during the evolutionary process (Havecker et al., 2004),

128 which might be the main reason of relatively small genome size of T. wallichiana

129 among the Taxaceae species (Figure S7).

131 Gene duplication in Taxus

132 Whole genome duplication (WGD) is one of the most important evolutionary

133 driving force for all extant flowering plants (Ren et al., 2018). Especially for

134 flowering plants, it has been invoked as a cause of macroevolutionary change,

135 explaining most of evolutionary novelties from extinction resistance to fundamental

136 evolutionary innovation (Clark and Donoghue, 2018). But the controversy about

137 WGD event in gymnosperm species has been existed for many years (Li et al., 2015;

138 Van de Peer et al., 2017). Here we intended to seek evolutionary relics of WGD in

139 Taxus by detecting paralogous synteny gene blocks among different chromosomes in140 T. wallichiana genome. However, Pre-proofwe only detected 22 synteny blocks including 304

141 genes (~0.69% of total genes) (Figure 1) and these synteny blocks showed a peak

142 around 52 Mya (Ks = 0.28) based on their evolutionary divergences (Figure S8),

143 indicating that the retained synteny blocks would be recent segmental duplications

144 rather than the genetic relics of ancient WGD event. We further made a synteny

145 analysis between T. wallichiana and other two gymnosperm species with complete Journal
146 chromosome-level genomes (i.e., Ginkgo biloba and Sequoiadendron giganteum). We

147 found that 56% and 72% genes in T. wallichiana had one-to-one synteny orthologs

148 with G. biloba and S. giganteum respectively (Figure 2A and Figure S9). These results

149 indicated that the WGD event might have not happened during the evolutionary

150 processes of the known gymnosperm species.

151 Although WGD event did not occur in T. wallichiana, we still found that more

152 than 80% of total genes were duplicated genes in the genome. By analyzing gene

153 duplication mechanisms in T. wallichiana, we found that half of duplicated genes

154 (15,673 genes) resulted from gene tandem duplication events (Figure 2B). The

155 percentage of tandem duplicated genes is higher than all of 120 flowering plants





 6

[PDF page 8]
156 (Table S11). Interestingly, we also found that the average number of introns in

157 duplicated genes was significantly less than that in single-copy genes (2.6 vs 4.3, P =

158 1e-73) (Figure S10). Moreover, one third of duplicated genes don’t have introns in T.

159 wallichiana, the proportion is higher than most of flowering plants (Figure 2C, Table

160 S12). Gene duplication by RNA-mediated retrotransposon would remove the original

161 introns in the duplicated copies. However, we only detected 9% gene duplications

162 belonging to RNA-based retrotransposition. Thus, the most possible processes of gene

163 duplication in T. wallichiana should be initiated by RNA-based retrotransposition and

164 subsequently expanded by tandem duplication. Furthermore, about 8% tandem

165 duplicative genes in T. wallichiana displayed an excess rate of nonsynonymous

166 substitution to synonymous substitution (dN/dS > 1), indicating adaptive evolution in

167 these genes (Table S13). The percentage of tandem duplicated genes with rapidly

168 evolutionary rate is higher than all of 120 flowering plants. Thus we speculated that

169 tandem duplication might be the main driving force of gene family evolution in T.

170 wallichiana genome (Wendel et al., 2016).171 Due to a plentiful of duplicationPre-proofevents, 616 gene families have significantly

172 expanded in the T. wallichiana genome (Figure 2D). Most of the highly expansive

173 gene families, such as NB-ARC (a domain containing disease resistance protein) and

174 protein kinase, are involved in transcriptional regulation, signal transduction, and

183 T5αOH, T10βOH, T2αOH, T7βOH, T13αOH and DBAT) out of the 11 known genes

184 were located on the chromosome 10 (Figure 3B and Figure S11). 90% (58 out of 64)

185 of P450 genes in the CYP725A subfamily were expanded on this chromosome by

186 tandem duplication (Figure 3B) and 50% genes (27 out of 52) in TRF004A subfamily

192 Origin and evolution of paclitaxel biosynthetic pathway

193 Until now, Taxaceae is the only family in gymnosperm producing paclitaxel (Isah,

194 2015). In order to decipher the origin and evolution of paclitaxel biosynthetic pathway,

195 we analyzed the evolutionary processes of three gene families involved in paclitaxel

196 biosynthesis. TS gene is the first key enzyme for paclitaxel biosynthesis from GGPP.

197 Besides the known TS, we also identified a new duplicate of TS (TS-2) on the same

198 chromosome, which has about 83% amino acid identity with the reported one in T.

199 wallichiana (Figure 3B and Figure S13). We also detected the orthologs of TS in the

200 studied Cupressales plants, but not in non-Cupressales plants (Figure 3C). Functional

201 assays of these TS orthologs in a yeast platform (Wang et al., 2014; Wang BB, 2014)202 showed that four TS genes from SciadopitysPre-proofverticillata, Pseudotaxus chienii, and T.

203 wallichiana could convert GGPP into taxadiene (Figure 3C and Figure S13).

204 Moreover, these four genes with TS function are more related to each other than other

205 TS orthologs without TS function based on phylogenetic and protein structure

206 analysis (Figure S14). However, when we co-expressed the TS gene and the extant

207 CYP725 genes from S. verticillata in the yeast platform (Figure S15), we could not Journal
208 detect new products, indicating that taxadiene could not be further catalyzed by

209 CYP725 genes in S. verticillata. Thus, although the ability of cyclizing GGPP to

210 taxadiene by TS genes had originated in the common ancestor of Cupressales around

211 200 Mya (Figure S16), the pathway of paclitaxel could have only originated in the

212 common ancestor of Taxaceae.

213 Taxoids had been detected in many Taxaceae species (Hayashi, 2019), but we

214 only observed gene expansion for the CYP725A P450 subfamily and TRF004A

215 transferases subfamily in P. chienii and T. wallichiana (Table S14). Phylogenetic

216 analysis of all CYP725A subfamily genes showed that two possible expansions

217 occurred after the common ancestor of Taxaceae diverged from Cupressales (Figure





 8

[PDF page 10]
218 3D and Figure S17). The first expansion happened in the common ancestor of

219 Taxaceae around 180 Mya (Figure 3D), resulting in the functional differentiation of

220 T5αOH, T14βOH, T2αOH and T7βOH (branch 2). By screening P450 genes in this

221 branch, we also identified a new gene with the same function of T5αOH (T5αOH-2)

222 (Figure 3B and Figure S15). The second expansion occurred after the common

223 ancestor of P. chienii and T. wallichiana diverged from Torreya grandis around 150

224 Mya (Figure 3D), resulting in the emergence of T10βOHs (branch 3). However, most

225 of these genes might have been lost later in the species without the complete

226 paclitaxel biosynthetic pathway. Interestingly, we also found that 28 unknown genes

227 in CYP725A subfamily highly co-expressed with the known genes in paclitaxel

228 biosynthetic pathway (Figure S18), indicating that these P450s would be potential

229 genes for biosynthesis of paclitaxel or its analogues. Thus, gene expansion by tandem

230 duplication in CYP725A subfamily provided a lot of evolutionary resources for the

231 nature to modify the core of paclitaxel at different positions.

232 The transferase TRF004A subfamily could further modify the precursor of233 paclitaxel with different side groupsPre-proofand played important roles in the clinical

234 function (Meng et al., 2016). The expansion of the TRF004A subfamily evolutionary

235 branch happened before T10βOH evolutionary branch but after the T5αOH

236 evolutionary branch (Figure 3D). However, we did not find any homologous genes in

237 other species besides P. chienii and T. wallichiana. Therefore, it was interesting to

238 decipher the evolutionary mechanisms of how TRF004A subfamily was retained in T. Journal
239 wallichiana and its closed relatives. By carefully analyzing the protein structure

240 models in TRF004A subfamily, we observed an obvious hydrophobic region nearby

241 the substrate transport channel (Figure 3E). Moreover, this particular hydrophobic

242 region of TRF004A subfamily only evolved in T. wallichiana by comparing with

243 other transferases in T. wallichiana and the TRF004 family genes in other

244 gymnosperm species, respectively (Figure 3E and Figure S19). We speculated that the

245 evolutionary hydrophobic regions in the tunnel-adjacent regions of TRF004A proteins

246 might help to draw the hydrophobic substrates to active centre. Therefore, the

247 expansion of genes in TRF004A subfamily by tandem duplication provided a lot of

248 opportunities for the nature to develop novel side groups on the precursor of





 9

[PDF page 11]
249 paclitaxel and finally formed the complete biosynthetic pathway of paclitaxel in the

250 ancestor of Taxus.

251

252 In summary, our study revealed that tandem duplication might be the main reason

253 for the formation of the complex paclitaxel biosynthesis pathway in T. wallichiana

254 (Fan et al., 2008). Through tandem duplication, more and more enzymes were created

255 by nature to modify taxoids and finally the paclitaxel was produced. Unfortunately,

256 we cannot confirm more functional genes in the paclitaxel biosynthetic pathway due

257 to lack of corresponding catalytic substrates. It is very possible that the unknown

258 genes in the paclitaxel biosynthetic pathway should be hid in these tandem duplicated

259 genes. Our study presents a reference genome for the yew and also provides genetic

260 sources for complete pathway identification of paclitaxel in future.

262 Materials and Methods

263 Plant materials
264 To explore the genome of T. wallichiana, we picked fresh leaves from an at least 50 years
265 old yew cultivated in Kunming Institute of Botany, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Figure S1).
266 For RNA-seq, we collected fresh leaves and tree barks from the same individual plant.
267
268 Genome size evaluation
269 Total 570G filtered Illumina data was retained for 17-mer analysis, which was widely
270 used in genome size evaluation (Chen et al., 2020; Li et al., 2010). The 17-mer frequency
271 distribution derived from the sequencing reads was plotted in Figure S2, the peak of the
272 17-mer distribution is 48, and the total K-mer count is 509,993,117,838 (= 5.0999E+11), then
273 the genome size was estimated (by formula: Genome Size = K-mer_num/Peak_depth) as
274 10.62Gb. Using the similar strategy, we estimated the genome sizes of other four Taxaceae
275 species (i.e., Cephalotaxus sinensis, Torreya grandis, Amentotaxus argotaenia, Pseudotaxus
276 chienii) by generating about 50X BGI-seq data.

278 Genome sequencing
279 We integrated Illumina Hiseq sequencing, Nanopore sequencing and Hi-C sequencing to
280 complete genome sequencing of T. wallichiana (Supplementary Methods). For Illumina
281 sequencing, 12 libraries were constructed with 250bp, 2k, 5k and 10k insert fragments, and
282 then sequenced on a HiSeq 2500 system with a PE150 strategy; For Nanopore sequencing, Pre-proof
283 genomic DNA was extracted by QIAGEN® Genomic DNA extraction kit (Cat#13323,
284 QIAGEN), and detected by NanoDrop™ One UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher
285 Scientific, USA), and sequenced by Nextomics (Wuhan, China). For Hi-C sequencing, two
286 Hi-C libraries were prepared by a standard procedure (Belton et al., 2012), and then were
287 quantified and sequenced using the Illumina Hiseq platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA).
288 Three sequencing methods generated about 1 Tb Illumina Hiseq sequencing reads, 869.8 Gb
289 Nanopore sequencing reads and about 1 Tb Hi-C sequencing reads.290 Journal
291 Genome assembly
292 The ONT Albacore software was used to perform the base calling, adaptor removal and
293 low-quality base filtration on fast5 files (Heikema et al., 2020). After removing Nanopore low
294 quality reads (mean_Qscore＜7), all the remained reads were assembled into contigs by
295 Nextdenovo v2.3 (https://github.com/Nextomics/NextDenovo) with parameters “nextgraph -a
296 1 -u 1”. After correcting by Nextpolish (Hu et al., 2020), all the filtered Illumina reads (~96×)
297 were mapped to the corrected contigs with BWA-mem (Li and Durbin, 2009) and further
298 corrected by 3-round Pilon (v1.21) (Walker et al., 2014); Chromosomes were then assembled
299 by 3D de novo assembly software (Dudchenko et al., 2017) using filtered Hi-C data, the
300 parameters were “-m haploid -i 25000 -r 4” and 12 chromosomes were corrected by software
301 Juicebox (Robinson et al., 2018).

320 Detecting tandem and retrotransposition duplications
321 The intra-species paralogs for three gymnosperm species (i.e., T. wallichiana, Ginkgo
322 biloba and Sequoiadendron giganteum) and about 120 flowering plants were first identified
323 by OrthoMCL (Li, 2003) with amino acid identity more than 40. For each paralog group, if
324 two paralogous genes were closely adjacent to each other in the same chromosome, or the
325 number of interval genes between two paralogous genes were less than 10 and both them have
326 the similar exon and intron structures, the two paralogous genes were considered as the Pre-proof
327 potential “tandem duplications”. For retrotransposition duplications, we first predicted all
328 potential transposed genes by DupGen_finder (Qiao et al., 2019). Then, retrotransposition
329 duplicative genes were screened from the transposed genes if one of gene in transposed gene
330 pair contains intron and the other gene doesn’t contain intron.
331
332 Calculating dN/dS of tandem duplicate genes
333 All pairwise tandem duplicative genes were obtained from the detected tandem duplicate334 gene groupsJournalfor all studied plants. For the group with gene number > 2, all nonredundant gene
335 pairs were considered. Then, the nonsynonymous: synonymous substitution (dN/dS) ratio for
336 each gene pair was calculated by PAML v4.9 (Yang, 2007). For a plant, the proportion of
337 rapidly evolving genes was estimated by counting the number of genes in all gene pairs with
338 dN/dS > 1. Given that a genes pair with dN/dS > 1 may only contain a rapidly evolving gene,
339 the number divided by 2, and then divided by the total number of tandem duplicate genes in a
340 plant, was considered as the proportion of rapidly evolving genes in a plant.

367 Homology modelling of TS homologs
368 The three-dimensional models of TS homologs were built using RosettaCM (version 3.5)
369 (Song et al., 2013), and the overall structures were constructed based on the crystal structure of TS
370 from Taxus brevifolia (PDB ID: 3P5R, resolution: 2.25Å), the closed conformers of enzyme Pre-proof
371 substrate cavity were constructed based on the C-terminal of the crystal structure of Bornyl
372 Diphosphate Synthase (PDB ID:1N20). The fragment files of target sequences for RosettaCM
373 were generated using Robetta server (http://www.robetta.org/) (Kim et al., 2004). For each TS
374 homolog, the sequence alignment between template and target sequence was performed by
375 Clustal Omega (Sievers and Higgins, 2014). Totally 1000 models for the target sequence were produced
376 and the top ten models with lowest total score were clustered, and the model with lowest
377 overall energy was chosen as the candidate. Furthermore, the all-atom refinement of the378 candidate structureJournalwas implemented using Rosetta relax application (Conway et al., 2014),
379 and 1000 models were produced to fully sample the local conformers. Finally, the model with
380 the lowest overall energy was selected as the protein structure of the target sequence.
