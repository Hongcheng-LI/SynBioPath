ABSTRACT: Under the guidance of genome mining combined with bioassay-coupled
metabolomic analyses, an unprecedented macrodiolide sanyensin (1), with two flexible
macrolides fused by the rigid oxabicyclo[3.3.1]nonane core, was isolated from the
deep-sea-derived Streptomyces sp. OUCT16-30. The stereochemistry of 1 was
established by NOEs (nuclear Overhauser effects), J-based configuration analysis,
Marfey’s analysis, and together with a newly introduced stereochemical study
workflow, which greatly shortens the time to obtain correct conformations of flexible
structures based on the NMR constraints, thus leading to reliable quantum chemical
calculations to establish the absolute configurations. This workflow is expected to have
broad applicability for elucidating the stereochemistry of flexible natural products. The
macrodiolide framework of 1 is proposed to be formed through a biocatalytic
cyclodimerization, followed by a series of nonenzymatic reactions. This work leads to
new insights into the unexplored biosynthetic potential of deep-sea microbes and also
provides a practical streamline for efficient mining of novel natural products, from
discovery to stereochemical finalization.
Marine natural products (MNPs) are considered an important
treasure house for new drugs. To date, over 40,000 MNPs have
been discovered (https://marinlit.rsc.org/), out of which 20
have become drugs available on the market1,2 and 35 are
candidates under clinical trials.3 Many MNPs have attracted
attention because of their structural novelty. In particular, the
extreme environment in the deep ocean, which still remains
largely unexplored, gives the living microbes high levels of
biodiversity and unique metabolic pathways that pave the way
for the biosynthesis of new chemical weapons to fight diseases
threatening human beings.4−6
In our search for new sources of novel natural products from
the in-house library of deep-sea-derived bacteria, an
unreported type of streptogramin biosynthetic gene cluster
(BGC) (named san, GenBank ID: PQ187432) was discovered
via the genomic analysis of Streptomyces sp. OUCT16-30.
Then, a bioassay-coupled metabolomic analysis toward
medium- and time-dependent culture products of the
OUCT16-30 strain was sequentially conducted to track the
streptogramin chemotype, leading to the targeted isolation of
an unprecedented dimeric streptogramin skeleton, sanyensin
(1), along with its monomeric analogues (2−4). Compound 1
features a unique butterfly-shaped framework with two flexible
macrolide “wings” fused by an oxabicyclo[3.3.1]nonane
“body”. Bearing a flexible structural property, the stereo-
chemical determination of 1 is a challenging task.
X-ray crystallography, which would have been the preferred
experimental technique for clarifying the three-dimensional
(3D) structure of 1, is unfortunately not applicable due to
difficulties in getting crystals. Quantum chemical (QC)
computational calculations are an alternative approach that
has proven useful for stereochemical determination of a large
number of rigid structures.7,8 However, the flexibility of the
bilateral macrolides of 1 leads to a very large number of
conformational possibilities, which in turn affect the chiroptical
properties. Hence, with only QC calculations, it would not be
possible to obtain reliable absolute configurations.
Herein, we introduce an effective stereochemical study
workflow for flexible natural products that sequentially includes
NMR-based 3D model preconstruction (NPC), NMR-con-
strained refinement (NCR), GEometry Optimization (GEO),
ECD (electronic circular dichroism) calculation, and DP4
NMR calculation (Figure 1) to establish the absolute
configurations of 1. This workflow saves significant time in
searching for correct conformations in solution; thus, it is
expected to have broad applicability for elucidating the

■EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
General Experimental Procedures. Optical rotations
were measured on a JASCO P-1030 digital polarimeter
(JASCO Corp., Tokyo, Japan). The ECD spectra in MeOH
were obtained using a JASCO J-715 spectropolarimeter. The
IR spectra were determined on a Nicolet NEXUE470 FT-IR.
1D and 2D NMR spectra were provided by Bruker Avance III
600 spectrometers, and chemical shifts were reported with
reference to the residual solvent peaks (δH 3.31 and δC 49.0 for
CD3OD, δH 3.50 and δC 39.5 for DMSO-d6). High-
performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) was performed
on an Agilent 1360 Infinity instrument equipped with a diode
array detector. The HPLC-HRESIMS data were obtained on
an Agilent 1360 HPLC system coupled with a Q-TOF Ultima
Global GAA076 MS spectrometer. The optical density
measurements were recorded on a Biotech Epoch3 microplate
reader.
Multitime Point Cultivations and LC-MS Analysis.
Streptomyces sp. OUCT16-30 was cultivated in 5 mL of seed
medium (TSBY) for 48 h at 30 °C and 220 rpm. Then, each
seed culture broth was added to 50 mL of GYM medium and
cultured at 30 °C (220 rpm) for 1−5 days. The multitime
point culture broths were extracted twice with an equal volume
of EtOAc, followed by vacuum evaporation. Sequentially, each
EtOAc extract was dissolved in 1 mL of MeOH, and it was
subjected to HPLC-HRESIMS analysis. Analytical HPLC was
performed with a linear gradient from 10 to 100% B/A (phase
B: 100% ACN; phase A: H2O; Agilent Eclipse Plus C18, 100 ×
2.1 mm, 3.5 μm; wavelength: 220, 240, 260, 280, 300, 320, and
365 nm; flow rate: 0.2 mL/min).
Metabolomic Data Analysis. The HPLC-HRESIMS data
(.raw) of multitime point culture extracts (day 1−day 5) of
Streptomyces sp. OUCT16-30 were uploaded to XCMS Online
(https://xcmsonline.scripps.edu/landing_page.
php?pgcontent=mainPage) for metabolic differential analysis.
After data preprocessing, such as peak identification, peak
integration, and peak alignment, the data matrix (.csv) was
uploaded to the MetaboAnalyst 6.0 platform (http://www.
metaboanalyst.ca/). Abnormal variables in the data were
filtered. The data set normalized by sum and Pareto scaling
was submitted to principal component analysis (PCA) and
heatmap analysis. In the PCA score plot analysis, each data
point represents a multidimensional linear combination of all
metabolites in the sample, and the distance between them
represents the difference between samples. The farther the
sample points are, the greater the metabolic difference between
the two samples; otherwise, the smaller the difference.
Fermentation, Extraction, and Isolation. Streptomyces
sp. OUCT16-30 was inoculated into 1 L Erlenmeyer flasks
containing 300 mL of GYM liquid medium and deposited on a
rotary shaker at 220 rpm and 30 °C for 24 h. The combined
culture broth (18 L) was extracted with double volumes of
EtOAc at room temperature and concentrated with a vacuum
evaporator. The combined organic extracts were concentrated
(1.6 g) and then partitioned between 90% MeOH and n-
hexane to remove nonpolar components. The aqueous MeOH
layer (1.4 g) was concentrated and subjected to reversed-phase
(C18) open column (ODS-A, 120 Å, S-30/50 mesh)

Marfey’s Analysis. Compound 1 (100 μg) was hydrolyzed
with 6 N HCl (1 mL) at 100 °C for 24 h, and HCl was
removed under the N2 gas stream. The hydrolysates were
dissolved in H2O (100 μL) and Marfey’s reagent (Nα-(2,4-
dinitro-5-fluorophenyl)-L-alaninamide, L-FDAA; 1 mg/mL in
acetone) (200 μL) together with 1 M NaHCO3 (50 μL) in a
reaction vial. Then, the reaction vials were incubated at 50 °C
for 60 min. After cooling to room temperature, the reactions
were quenched with 2 N HCl (25 μL). The D- and L-
authentic standard amino acids (100 μg) were also prepared by
the same protocol. For analysis of Marfey’s reaction products
with hydrolysates and standard amino acids, the following
chromatographic method was used in reversed-phase HPLC
with ZORBAX SB-C18 (5 μm, 4.6 × 150 mm) via gradient
elution of solvent A (H2O + 0.1% TFA) and solvent B (90%
ACN + 0.1% TFA) at a flow rate of 1 mL/min (210, 260, 294,
and 360 nm).
Stereochemical Study Based on NMR-Constrained
Conformational Construction Coupled with Quantum
Chemical Calculations. (a) NMR-Based 3D Model Pre-
construction. The 3D models of (16R,16′R)-1, (16R,16′S)-1,
(16S,16′S)-1, and (16S,16′R)-1 were preconstructed based on
the NOE correlations using the program Chem3D, which were
followed by the energy minimization driven by the MM2/
MMFF94 force field. This step was repeated until the NPC
models of stereoisomers conformed to the key NOEs of 1.
(b) NMR-Constrained Refinement. The NPC models of
16,16′-stereoisomers of 1 were subjected to dynamic annealing
within the program CNS 1.3 (or X-POLR) using the NOE
distance constraints established by CCPNMR 2.4. A final set of
structures (>20) was calculated within CNS, and the structure
with the lowest energy (Elowest) was used to represent the NCR
model of each stereoisomer. The calculated 3D conformers
were analyzed with the program PYMOL.
(c) Geometry Optimization. The NCR models of 16,16′-
stereoisomers of 1 were subjected to GEO with DFT
calculations at the B97D/TZVP level of theory using the
Gaussian 09 program to obtain GEO models.
(d) ECD Calculation. The TD calculations were performed
on each NCR or GEO model using the long-range-corrected
hybrid CAM-B3LYP. The number of excited states per
molecule was 50. Solvent (MeOH) effects were considered
by using the polarizable continuum model. The ECD spectra
were generated by the program GaussView 5.0. The ECD
spectra of the NCR model of each 16,16′-stereoisomer was
calculated and compared with the experimental ECD data of 1.
When the calculated ECD of the NCR model was inconsistent
with the experimental data, it further went through step (c)
and repeated step (d).
(e) NMR Calculation. All of the calculations were managed
by the PyDP4 Python script within DP4-AI, which is available
from http://www-jmg.ch.cam.ac.uk/tools/nmr/ and GitHub
https://github.com/KristapsE/DP4-AI/. The raw 1H and 13C
NMR spectra of 1 and a set of NCR or GEO models of 16,16′-
stereoisomers of 1 were prepared as input files. Each
conformer was subjected to molecular mechanics calculations
in the gas phase utilizing the MMFF force field using
MacroModel, which was set to find all low energy conformers
within 10 kcal/mol at least 5 times. The NMR calculations
were carried out with the GIAO method at the mPW1PW91/
6-311G(d) level of theory (with chloroform as the solvent).
The DP4 probabilities of 16,16′-stereoisomers of 1 were
calculated using an Applet within DP4-AI. The stereoisomer
showing a calculated ECD consistent with the experimental
data of 1 and a predominant DP4 NMR probability
represented the correct absolute configurations.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Paired Omics Enables the Targeted Discovery of
Unprecedented Sanyensin (1) from Streptomyces sp.
OUCT16-30. The OUCT16-30 strain was isolated from a
deep-sea sediment from the Indian Ocean (4495 m depth).
The 16S rRNA gene sequence analysis showed that it belongs
to the Streptomyces genus, with 99.77% identity to that of S.
sanyensis 219820T (GenBank ID: NR_116599.1) (Figure S1).
The genomic sequence analysis of Streptomyces sp. OUCT16-
30 using antiSMASH9 and BLAST10 revealed the presence of a
hybrid PKS/NRPS streptogramin-like BGC (110 kb), named
san (Figure S2). The san-encoding proteins exhibit 91−99%
identity to their homologues in the virginiamycin BGC (vir)
from S. virginiae11 and 47−89% identity to those in the
pristinamycin BGC (prs) from S. pristinaespiralis Pr11 (Table
S1).12 These gene clusters, collectively referred to as
streptogramin BGCs, contain cross-dispersed gene regions
that code two chemically unrelated antibiotics (groups A and
B).11−14 Comparative analysis of san with known (Figure S2
and Table S1) and putative (Figure S3 and Table S2)
streptogramin BGCs discovered throughout genome mining in
the NCBI databases showed that it displays fusion character-
istics of vir-type and prs-type BGCs. Thus, san is classified as a
new type of streptogramin BGC in nature. This analytical
result provided valuable information for exploring new
streptogramin analogues in Streptomyces sp. OUCT16-30 and
for understanding how these mixed-up large streptogramin
BGCs were formed in the evolution.
As the first step to activate the streptogramin production in
Streptomyces sp. OUCT16−30, the strain was cultivated in 7
different media, for which GYM was selected as the preferred
medium that showed notable antibacterial activity (Table S3),
and meanwhile presented compounds displaying streptogramin
A-like UV spectra (λmax 220 nm). Then, the metabolic
chemodiversity during specific culture periods (day 1−day 5)
was detected by untargeted comparison of HPLC-HRESIMS
metabolic profiles using MetaboAnalyst 6.0,15 a streamlined
tool suited for metabolomics data analysis. Three-dimensional
projection maps of PCA score plots highlighted three distinct
clustered groups of samples (Figure 2), in which day 2−day 4
formed one group, while day 1 and day 5 displayed obvious
variance on the first principal components (PC1, 29.9%). This
observation was confirmed through hierarchical clustering and
heatmap statistical analysis (Figure S4), which displayed
similar sample grouping. Comparative HPLC metabolomic
analysis of multitime point culture products showed the
compounds with streptogramin A-like UV spectra on day 1
(Figure S5), providing evidence for the production of
streptogramins, and they were believed to be responsible for
the antibacterial effects on day 1. Thus, a scale-up of 1 day
cultivation (18 L) was carried out and, using a UV-directed
separation, sanyensin (1) (Figure 3A), a novel macrodiolide

Planar Structure of Sanyensin. Sanyensin (1) was
isolated as a yellowish, amorphous solid. A molecular formula
of C56H72N6O13 was obtained by an HRESIMS ion at m/z
1037.5238 [M + H]+ (calcd for 1037.5236), requiring 24
degrees of unsaturation. Interestingly, according to the 13C
NMR spectrum of 1, most peaks appear in pairs, indicating a
dimeric skeleton composed of units A and B. This assumption
was supported by the assignments of two proline (Pro)
residues with the 1H spin systems of H-24/H-25/H-26/H-27
and H-24′/H-25′/H-26′/H-27′ in COSY and the HMBC
correlations from H-27/H-27′ to a carboxyl carbon C-1/C-1′
(Figure 3B). Based on the HMBC correlations (Figure 3B),
unit A was assigned to construct a typical hybrid polyketide−
peptide virginiamycin M (VM; alternatively named pristina-
mycin II, PII) skeleton.11,13 However, unit B does not form a
standard VM. The HMBC correlations from H-14′ to C-17′
and C-18′, from H-17′ to C-13′, C-14′, C-18′, C-15, and C-16,
and from H-17 to C-15′, C-16′, and C-17′ revealed that the
VM scaffold of unit B breaks between C-16′ and C-17′ but
forms C-14′/C-17′, C-16′/C-17, and C-17′/C-16 bonds
instead, which comprise a cyclohexane bridge. Moreover,
acetalization had taken place between 14-OH and 16′-keto to
form a hemiketal ring with a typical dioxygenated quaternary
carbon δC 100.5 (C-16′) (Table S4). The planar structure of 1
was thereby established as a novel scaffold with two
macrolactone rings (units A and B) fused by the
oxabicyclo[3.3.1]nonane core.
The monomeric analogues 2−4 were identified as 14,15-
anhydro-VM2 (2), VM1 (3), and VM2 (4), respectively, by
NMR data assignments (Table S5) and comparing with those
previously reported.16−18 Compound 2 was isolated from a
natural source for the first time.

The absolute configurations of the nonprotonated carbons
C-16 and C-16′ were determined using a newly introduced
stereochemical study workflow based on NMR-Constrained
conformational construction coupled with Quantum Chemical
(NCQC) calculations (Figure 1). As the first step, four 16,16′-
stereoisomeric 3D-models of 1 [(16R,16′R)-1, (16R,16′S)-1,
(16S,16′S)-1, and (16S,16′R)-1] were preconstructed using
Chem3D based on the NOESY correlations of 1. This step was
repeated until the generated models supported as many NOEs
as possible associated with the rigid oxabicyclo[3.3.1]nonane
core and all the NOEs in the flexible macrolide region. Then,
these NPC models were subjected to dynamic annealing using
CNS19 based on the NOE distance constraints (Table S6)
calculated by CCPNMR.20 Among the 20 CNS-generated
conformational ensembles, the lowest energy (Elowest) con-
former was selected to represent the 16,16′-stereoisomeric
NCR model of 1 (Figure 4A and Table S7). As shown in
Figure 4A, only (16R,16′R)-1 supported the key NOE of H-
14/H-14′, while other stereochemical NCR-models did not
conform to this correlation. Sequentially, the NCR model of
(16R,16′R)-1 was further subjected to geometric optimization
at the B97D/TZVP level of theory. To verify the efficiency of
this NMR-constrained conformational organization streamline,
the ECD calculations were carried out on the CAM-B3LYP/6-
31G(d) theory level for the conformers generated by different
combinations of the refinement steps. As shown in Figure 4B,
the NCR process (Conf. C) greatly upgraded the conforma-
tional qualities of (16R,16′R)-1 to generate the ECD spectrum
in good agreement with the experimental ECD data of 1. The
GEO step followed by NCR also generated a conformation
(Conf. D) that exhibited a similar ECD spectrum. However,
the NPC model (Conf. A) and its direct GEO conformer
(Conf. B) did not generate acceptable ECD spectra (Figure
4B). It proved that NCR works as an effective step to obtain
correct solution conformations of flexible structures in a short
time, less than 1 min for the generation of 20 conformers, as
compared to hours and days to search all of the conformational

Proposed Biosynthetic Pathway of Sanyensin. With
an unprecedented architecture of two macrolides fused by a
rigid oxabicyclo[3.3.1]nonane core, the biosynthesis of
sanyensin (1) in Streptomyces sp. OUCT16-30 attracted our
interest. The biosynthetic pathway of the monomer units of 1
is proposed based on previously established VM and PII.11,12
Starting from an isobutyryl-CoA unit, the putative SanB−SanJ
encoded by san BGC contribute to the assembly of a linear
hybrid polyketide−peptide chain (Figures 6 and S42A). Then,
the terminal Pro addition of 4a is proposed to be catalyzed by
SanT, a homologue of NRPS SnaD in S. pristinaespiralis Pr11
(Figures 6, S42A and Table S1).12 Sequence analysis revealed
that SanT contains an epimerase (E) domain, probably
catalyzing the epimerization of
L-Pro to D-form (Figure
S42A), which was consistent with the result of Marfey’s
analysis (Figure 3E). The C-terminal type I TE domain in
SanT (SanTTE) is predicted to be responsible for cyclization
and dimerization of linear intermediate 4a, resulting in the
formation of monomeric (4) and dimeric (1a) macrolide
frameworks (Figure 6). To date, the TE-catalyzed oligomeriza-
tion mechanism of NRPS and PKS has been reported two
pathways: “forward transfer” and “reverse transfer”.23−26 Since
SanTTE showed very low sequence homology (<30%) with any
other reported oligomerization-catalyzing TEs (Figure S44), its
acting mechanism could not be narrowed to one pathway.
Continuously, 1a forms a 14′,15′-dehydrated product 1b, while
no dehydratase-encoding genes were found in san BGC (Table
S1), which is speculated to be located outside the gene cluster.
Sequentially, 1b undergoes nonenzymatic aldol reaction (1c),
Michael addition (1d), and acetalization to yield 1 (Figure 6).
In order to explore whether 1 is generated artificially in the
separation process, 2 and 4 were mixed and retained at room
temperature under acidic (pH 1) or basic (pH 11) conditions
up to 7 days, but they did not form dimeric fusion analogues
(Figure S45). This result supported our deduction that the
macrodiolide scaffold of 1 is cyclized by SanTTE in the

Bioactivity Evaluation. In our investigation of the
antibacterial activity against a panel of multidrug-resistant
bacteria, including three Gram-positive strains and one Gram-
negative strain, 2−4 showed varying degrees of growth
inhibition against E. faecium (0.19−3.13 μg/mL) and S. aureus
(3.13 μg/mL), while 1 showed no activity up to 25 μg/mL
(Table S11). All of the compounds were inactive against E.
faecalis and K. pneumoniae. These results were in accordance
with the reported functions of the streptogramin family
antibiotics, which mainly inhibit Gram-positive pathogens.13,14
Streptogramins, being used as a human therapeutical agent and
an animal growth promoter for over 50 years,27−29 consist of
two structurally unrelated subgroups (A and B) that lead to
100-fold stronger synergistic effects.13 Although the homolo-
gous genes (san+number) coding group B streptogramins exist
in san BGC (Figure S2 and Table S1), the counterpart cyclic
depsipeptides were not detected throughout the whole
metabolome of Streptomyces sp. OUCT16-30. Hence,
commercial virginiamycin S1 and quinupristin were purchased
to investigate a possible synergistic antibacterial effect with 1,
but unexpectedly, none of them showed synergism (Table
S12). In the cytotoxicity evaluation, 1 exhibited lower toxicity
to human hepatocytes (L-02) and kidney cells (293T) than the
monomeric structure (Table S13), which may have the
potential as a new nontoxic scaffold in drug development.
■CONCLUSIONS
In summary, using a paired-omics-driven strategy, we achieved
the targeted discovery of sanyensin (1), an unprecedented
macrodiolide with a rigid oxabicyclo[3.3.1]nonane core, from
the deep-sea-derived Streptomyces sp. OUCT16-30. The
stereochemistry of 1 was determined by a combination of
NOEs, JBCA, and Marfey’s analysis and using a newly
introduced NCQC stereochemical study workflow, in which
the NMR-constrained conformational refinement greatly
shortens the time to search for the correct conformations of
flexible structures in solution, thus enabling reliable QC (ECD
and DP4) calculations to establish the absolute configurations.
This workflow is expected to have broad applicability for
elucidating the stereochemistry of flexible natural products.
The biosynthesis of 1 is achieved by san BGC, and its
macrodiolide framework is believed to be formed through
cyclodimerization catalyzed by the TE domain of SanT,
followed by a series of nonenzymatic reactions. This work not
only provides new insights into the unexplored biosynthetic
machinery of deep-sea microbial metabolites but also provides
an effective streamline for mining of novel natural products,
from discovery to stereochemical finalization.
