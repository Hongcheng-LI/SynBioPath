Non-Canonical Type II Terpene Cyclases Deliver Rare Terpenoid
Architectures

ABSTRACT: Terpene cyclases (TCs) catalyze the generation of complex polycyclic terpenoids through elaborate mechanistic
cascades. Very little is known about the unusual type II TCs responsible for generation of a large diverse family of meroterpenoids
known as indole diterpenoids (IDTs). The radarins are insecticidal and cytotoxic IDTs that possess an unusual core architecture for
which the catalytic machinery is unknown. Here, we interrogate the genome of Aspergillus fresenii to reveal the biosynthetic
machinery responsible for delivering the specific radarin scaffold. We demonstrate that a bespoke regioselective IDT epoxidase
(RadM) acts at the terminal olefin of precursor 3′-geranylgeranylindole, providing access to this distinct class of IDT architectures.
Coexpression of this epoxidase with a noncanonical type II TC RadB led to the biosynthesis of radarin C, in a complex skeletal
rearrangement that proceeds via rare alcoholic deprotonation and ketone formation. Our modeling of RadB reveals that catalysis by
RadB is enabled by a unique His residue that acts as both the proton donor and acceptor. Intriguingly, coexpression of RadM with an
alternative A. fresenii IDT cyclase gene, which does not share this catalytic His, revealed an alternate partial cyclization of 14,15-
epoxy-3′-GGI, producing a new decalin IDT. This work solves the final piece of the biosynthetic puzzle to access the full suite of
known IDT architectures, informs type II cyclase engineering efforts, and expands the repertoire of impressive chemistry catalyzed
by noncanonical TCs.
I
ncredible structural diversity is a hallmark of secondary
metabolites (SMs). Terpenoids, which elegantly exemplify
this diversity, are renowned for their skeletal complexity and
attendant biological activities.1 Central to delivering these
structures are terpene cyclases (TCs) that catalyze complex
cyclization reactions that diversify linear isoprenoid precur-
sors.2
Meroterpenoids are an abundant class of SMs that are
generally categorized by their starter moiety, most commonly
indole or a polyketide, which is fused with a terpenoid
(polyprenyl) carbon chain of varying lengths (Figure S1).3
Cyclization of these fused carbon chains is catalyzed by
noncanonical type II TCs4 to provide a diverse array of
complex and fascinating hybrid molecules (Figure S1).5 The
indole diterpenoids (IDTs) are the largest meroterpenoid class
and are known to possess a range of bioactivities including
insecticidal,6−8 anticancer,9−14 antiviral,15,16 and ion-channel
blocking properties.17−19 Elucidation of numerous IDT
biosynthetic pathways has established the early steps in core
IDT biosynthesis (Figure S2).20−25 The first dedicated step of
the biosynthetic route is the condensation of indole and
terpene components to yield 3′-geranylgeranyl indole (3′-GGI,
1). 3′-GGI (1) is epoxidized by an IdtM-type FAD-dependent
monooxygenase to prime the scaffold for protonation-initiated
cyclization by a noncanonical transmembrane TC (generally
known as IdtBs). Variation in the cyclization cascade catalyzed
by different IdtBs generates an array of IDT skeletons.23,24 The
scaffolds generated by this chemistry usually undergo further
and often elaborate derivatization via the action of oxidases,26
prenyltransferases,27−29 and other decorative enzymes.30
To date, all described IDT biosynthetic pathways include an
IdtM epoxidase that regioselectively oxidizes the C10−C11 or
“internal” olefin of 3′-GGI (1) (Figure 1A, Figure S4). The
resulting 10,11-epoxy-3′-GGI (2) product is then cyclized by
an IdtB-type TC, most frequently to form emindole SB
(3),21,25,28 but also to give an array of other IDT architectures
(Figure 1A).23,24 While biosynthesis of IDTs via epoxidation of
the internal olefin has been well described, there are currently
no reported examples of IdtMs that catalyze the initial
epoxidation of the terminal olefin or IdtB-type TCs that
cyclize 14,15-epoxy-3′-GGI (4). However, the isolated radarin
(5)9 and petromindole (6)31 IDTs have unusual skeletons that
imply such machinery must exist in nature (Figure 1B).
Along with their unique mechanistic origin and fascinating
structures, the radarins (isolated from Aspergillus fresenii) are
attractive targets due to their insecticidal properties and
significant anticancer activity.9 As the biosynthetic machinery
that delivers the radarins is unknown, we sought to discover
the specific enzymes required. Here, we identify a specific IdtM
monoepoxidase that primes the cyclization cascade through
regiospecific epoxidation of the terminal olefin of 3′-GGI (1),
along with the IdtB cyclase that converts the resulting 14,15-
epoxy-3′-GGI (4) to radarin C (5). Unexpectedly, we also

The fungus Aspergillus fresenii is endowed with a remarkable
diversity of IDTs, being reported to produce radarins,
sulpinines, penitrem derivatives, and an aflavinine isomer
(Figure S3).7,9,32 To identify candidates for genes that specify
radarin biosynthesis, we searched the genome of A. fresenii
strain CBS 550.6533,34 using the protein sequences of the
PaxM epoxidase and PaxB cyclase from the PAX cluster which
specifies paxilline (7) production (Figure S5) and was the first
identified IDT BGC.21,25 Our attention was drawn to a
clustered pair of genes (radM and radB), respectively,
predicted to encode the IdtM-type and IdtB-type proteins
(denoted as RadM and RadB). RadM and RadB protein
sequences were aligned against IdtM and IdtB proteins that
catalyze established meroterpenoid reaction chemistry (Figure
S6, Supplementary Files 1 and 2). Phylogenetic analysis of the
IdtM alignment identified that RadM grouped with epoxidases
from nonindole meroterpenoid pathways that catalyze a
terminal epoxidation reaction (Figure S7). Similarly, analysis
of the IdtB alignment grouped RadB with nonindole
meroterpenoid cyclases that accept 14,15-epoxy-geranylger-
anylated polyketides (Figure S8). The radM and radB genes
were also colocated alongside radX, a gene predicted to encode
a cytochrome P450 monooxygenase (denoted RadX; Figure
2A). RadX shares 43.6% sequence identity with a characterized
indole hydroxylase (FtmC from Aspergillus fumigatus35),

concluded that this “RAD” cluster was likely to specify radarin

Penicillium paxilli strain CY2, which lacks its native paxilline
(7) IDT gene cluster,20 was used as a heterologous host to
determine the roles of the RAD-encoded protein machinery.
The radM gene was expressed in CY2 alongside the genes
paxG and paxC, which encode the geranylgeranyl pyrophos-
phate synthase and indole prenyltransferase enzymes required
for producing the 3′-GGI (1) substrate (Figure S2). Two
metabolites were uniquely detected in strains expressing these
genes, the major at tR 10.9 min (m/z 424.3 [M + H]+) and
minor at tR 16.6 min (m/z 406.3 [M + H]+) (Figure 2B-IV).
The mass of the major compound corresponded to a diol (9)
anticipated from spontaneous hydrolysis of any singly
epoxidized form of 1,23 and the mass of the minor compound
matched either 10,11-epoxy-3′-GGI (2) or 14,15-epoxy-3′-
GGI (4). Upscaled growth of this strain enabled isolation of 9,
which was confirmed to be the C14−C15 isomer using NMR
spectroscopy (Tables S12 and S13, Figures S17−S21). This
finding shows that RadM catalyzes singular epoxidation of the
terminal olefin, providing the substrate required for radarin
biosynthesis. RadM appears to be the first enzyme reported to
catalyze regiospecific epoxidation at the terminal olefin of 1,
allowing access to the branch of IDTs arising from this specific
reaction chemistry (Figure S4). Unlike many other IdtMs that
catalyze a second R-epoxidation at the terminal olefin, the
epoxidation by RadM must deliver a product of S-
configuration to provide the correct substrate for radarin
cyclization (vide infra). To confirm the C14 configuration of
the unstable epoxide 4, we employed a modified Mosher
method36 on the corresponding diol 9 (Figures S12 and S22−
S29). This analysis confirms that the absolute configuration of
9 is 14R and therefore RadM delivers the anticipated 14S-
configuration of epoxide 4.
We next sought to identify the cyclase responsible for
radarin biosynthesis. Concerted cyclization of 14,15-epoxy-3′-
GGI (4) to form radarin C (5) should begin with decalin ring
formation (carbocation 4a), then a Markovnikov addition to
form carbocation 4b, followed by a highly intricate backbone
rearrangement involving alcohol deprotonation that triggers an
elegant cascade of 1,2-hydride shifts and 1,2-methyl migrations
(Scheme 1).37 Coexpression of radB with paxG, paxC, and
radM in CY2 led to the production of a metabolite with a mass
consistent with 5 (m/z 406.3 [M + H]+, tR 14.1 min) (Figure
2B-V). Isolation and structural characterization by NMR
confirmed the structure as radarin C (5) (Tables S14 and S15,
Figures S30−S35). Rather than a proposed squalene-hopene
cyclase-type enzyme,37 this observation demonstrates that this
chemistry is indeed delivered by a noncanonical type II cyclase,
with similarities to other meroterpenoid cyclases. The
cyclization cascade catalyzed by RadB utilizes a rare alcoholic
deprotonation to form a ketone moiety and bears mechanistic
similarities to the formation of the “most rearranged”
triterpene, friedelin.38
The final gene within the RAD cluster (radX) was added
alongside paxG, paxC, radM, and radB and expressed in CY2.
Transformant analysis revealed a unique compound consistent
with the expected mass for radarin A (7) (m/z 422.3 [M +
H]+, tR 10.9 min) (Figure 2B-VI), which exhibited key
fragment ions of 146.1 m/z and 148.1 m/z diagnostic for
hydroxylation of the indole ring (Figure S15).39 Positioning of
this indole ring hydroxylation was verified by NMR, confirming
the structure to be 8 (Tables S16 and S17, Figures S36−S41).
Hydroxylation is a common decoration of the terpene
component of IDTs, but this is the only known example of
hydroxylation of the indole moiety. While radarin B (10) and
radarin D (11) are reported to be produced by A. fresenii, no
candidate reductase-encoding gene was found in the RAD
cluster (Figure 2A).
We then turned our attention to a second nonclustered gene
within A. fresenii (rosB) that encoded an IdtB (RosB) sharing
79% sequence identity with RadB. Our phylogenetic analysis
considered RosB as another candidate 14,15-epoxy-3′-GGI (4)
cyclase (Figure S8). Expressing rosB in P. paxilli CY2 alongside
paxG, paxC, and radM resulted in production of metabolite 12,
([M + H]+, 406.3, tR 12.8 min), (Figure 2B-VII, Figure S16).
Isolation and characterization enabled the assignment of a new
compound reported here for the first time, roseyrin (12, Table
S18, Figures S42−S47), which contains a trans-decalin scaffold
not previously observed in the IDT class. Mechanistically,

The IdtB-type cyclases are unusual type II TCs with seven
transmembrane helices, and as such no crystal structures have
been obtained, and limited studies have illuminated the
catalytic mechanisms of these cyclases.24,40,41 To interrogate
the catalytic rationale behind these mechanisms we examined
structural models42 of RadB and RosB and their putative
substrate binding pockets (Figure 3). Reaction initiation
requires protonation of the epoxide, and previous work on
the merosesquiterpenoid cyclases Pyr4, AdrI, and InsA7
suggests a specific acidic Glu or Asp residue carries out this
function.40 The corresponding conserved Glu62 residue is also
found in both RadB and RosB (Figure S6).24,40 Interestingly,
the Glu62 of RadB does not have appropriate positioning or
predicted pKa elevation expected for this catalytic role24,40,41
(Figure 3). Our modeling indicates that a unique His (His58)
of RadB is likely to act as the proton donor for this enzyme
(Figure 3, Figure S6). The formation of radarin C (5) requires
deprotonation of the resulting alcohol intermediate 4b, and
notably the imidazole moiety of His58 is also positioned
appropriately to act additionally as the required catalytic base
(Figure 3). Importantly, quenching of the carbocation through
alcohol deprotonation to result in a ketone moiety is very rare
in terpene cyclization chemistry.1 Our sequence alignment
shows that His58 is not seen in any other known
meroterpenoid cyclases (Figure S6), highlighting the novelty
of the RadB catalytic machinery. It should be noted that the
ensuing complex rearrangement cascade on deprotonation
requires precise positioning of the substrate within the active
site of RadB. It seems likely that subtle differences in the active
site architecture between RadB and RosB, and the absence of
this crucial His, account for the substantially different reaction
outcomes. Pinpointing this residue sets the scene for more
diverse type II TC engineering studies, with the potential to
expand the diversity of these cyclic architectures and
understand how product selectivity is controlled.
Despite early IDT biosynthesis via the internal epoxidation
of 3′-GGI (1) being well-established, the route through
terminal C14−C15 epoxidation had not been defined.24 Here,
we identify the key regiospecific epoxidase, RadM, which
uniquely functionalizes the terminal epoxide of 1, and the type
II TCs that catalyze proton-initiated cyclization of this primed
linear precursor (4). Of the two related cyclases capable of this
chemistry, RadB catalyzes a remarkably complex cyclization
cascade. This work exposes the biosynthetic componentry
dedicated to this unusual branch of the IDTs generated from 4
and reveals linkages in complex terpene chemistry that expand
the meroterpenoid class of natural products.
