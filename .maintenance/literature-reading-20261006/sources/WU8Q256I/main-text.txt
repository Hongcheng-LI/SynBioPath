ABSTRACT: The biosynthetic pathways of natural products involve
unusual biochemical reactions catalyzed by unique enzymes. Amino-
propylation, although apparently simple, is an extremely rare
modification outside polyamine biosynthesis. The canonical pathway
used in the biosynthesis of peptide-adenylate antibiotic microcin C of
E. coli (Eco-McC) entails alkylation by the S-adenosyl-methionine-
derived 3-amino-3-carboxypropyl group of the adenylate moiety and
subsequent decarboxylation to yield the bioactive aminopropylated
compound. Here, we report the structure and biosynthesis of a new
member of the microcin C family of antibiotics, Bsu-McC, produced by
Bacillus subtilis MG27, which employs an alternative aminopropylation
pathway. Like Eco-McC, Bsu-McC consists of a peptide moiety that facilitates prodrug import into susceptible bacteria and a
warhead, a nonhydrolyzable modified isoasparaginyl-adenylate, which, when released into the cytoplasm, binds aspartyl-tRNA
synthetase (AspRS) inhibiting translation. In contrast to the Eco-McC, whose warhead carries a single aminopropyl group attached
to the phosphate moiety of isoasparaginyl-adenylate, the warhead of Bsu-McC is decorated with a tandem of two aminopropyl
groups. Our in silico docking of the Bsu-McC warhead to the AspRS-tRNA complex suggests that two aminopropyl groups form
extended interactions with the enzyme and tRNA, stabilizing the enzyme−inhibitor complex. We show that tandem
aminopropylation results in a 32-fold increase in the biological activity of peptidyl-adenylate. We also show that B. subtilis adopted
an alternative pathway for aminopropylation in which two homologous 3-aminopropyltransferases utilize decarboxylated S-
adenosylmethionine as a substrate. Additionally, Bsu-McC biosynthesis alters the social behavior of the B. subtilis producer strain,
resulting in a sharp decrease in their ability to form biofilms.
■INTRODUCTION
Natural products comprise a treasure trove of unusual chemical
structures and biochemical reactions that produce them.
Ribosomally synthesized posttranslationally modified peptides
(RiPPs) constitute a vast superfamily of natural products that
combine structurally and functionally diverse groups of
compounds.1 RiPPs play important roles in bacterial
physiology, including the mediation of quorum-sensing
signals,2 induction of complex morphogenesis,3 serving as
metallophores4 and enzyme cofactors,5 etc. Many RIPPs have
antimicrobial or antifungal activity,6 making them prospective
leads for future antibiotic development.
RiPPs are classified into several families depending on the
type of post-translational modifications and the enzymes that
introduce them.1,7 Microcin C-like compounds (McCs)
constitute a unique family of RiPP antibiotics modified with
nucleotides.7 A typical McC is a peptide nucleotide consisting
of a short formylated peptide and an adenosine or, less
commonly, modified cytosine monophosphate moiety linked
to a C-terminal isoasparagine residue (iN) via an N−P
phosphamide bond. The best-studied member of the family,
microcin C from Escherichia coli (Eco-McC), is a peptide-
adenylate that is active at submicromolar concentrations
against enterobacteria, including clinically relevant E. coli,
Salmonella, Shigella, and some strains of Klebsiella and
Yersinia.8,9 McC acts via a rare Trojan horse mechanism
(Figure 1A). The peptide part of McC is responsible for
binding to the conserved YejABEF oligopeptide transporter
and efficient import into the target bacteria.10 Once in the
cytoplasm, the peptide part is deformylated by the peptide
deformylase (PDF) and then digested by cellular amino-
peptidases,11 releasing the active warhead, an isoasparaginyl-

synthetase (AspRS) and prevents aminoacylation of tRNAAsp,

A minimal biosynthetic gene cluster (BGC) sufficient for
producing a bioactive peptidyl-adenylate comprises only three
genes: mccA, encoding a peptide precursor; and mccB and
mccC, coding for the family defining nucleotidyltransferase and
a major facilitator superfamily (MFS) export pump,
respectively.13 MccB modifies the MccA precursor peptide in
a two-step reaction (Figure 1B), resulting in the formation of a
bioactive peptidyl-acyl-N-P-adenylate14,15 or peptidyl-acyl-N-P-
cytidylate.9,16 Many mcc BGCs, including that of E. coli,
acquired additional genes responsible for secondary post-
translational modifications or self-immunity.17 The peptidyl-
adenylate of E. coli is additionally decorated with a rare post-
translational modification, an aminopropyl group attached to
the phosphate moiety.18 This modification is introduced by the
sequential action of MccDEco, the S-adenosylmethionine-
dependent 3-amino-3-carboxypropyltransferase (ACP trans-
ferase), and MccE1Eco, the N-terminal decarboxylase domain of
the bifunctional MccEEco protein (Figure 1B).19 The addition
of the aminopropyl group increases the inhibitory activity of
the peptidyl nucleotide 8-fold,19 thus constituting a function-
ally important secondary modification. Identification of new
activity-enhancing modifications of RiPPs and characterization
of their biosynthetic pathways provide invaluable tools for
engineering new-to-nature molecules with desired properties.
Here, we report a new member of the McC-like RiPP family
produced by B. subtilis MG27. The B. subtilis McC-like
compound carries a unique secondary modification consisting
of a tandem of two aminopropyl groups attached to the
phosphate moiety of the adenylate. Despite the structural
similarity between E. coli and B. subtilis McCs, both microbes
evolved alternative pathways for aminopropylation. In contrast
to E. coli McC, B. subtilis McC aminopropylation modification
requires decarboxylated S-adenosylmethionine as a substrate
and is catalyzed by two aminopropyltransferases, MccM1Bsu
and MccM2Bsu. We demonstrate that tandem aminopropyla-
tion results in a 32-fold increase in the bioactivity of peptidyl-
adenylate. We also show that Bsu-McC biosynthesis causes
hyperactive growth of B. subtilis MG27 and reduces its ability
to form a biofilm.

The Biosynthesis of Bsu-McC Hijacks the Polyamine
Biosynthetic Pathway. Sequential installation of amino-
propyl groups on the phosphate moiety of the adenylated
MccABsu precursor peptide resembles the aminopropylation
pathway in the biosynthesis of E. coli McC.19 Although, in
contrast to E. coli (Figure 1B), mccBsu BGC lacks gene(s)
coding for a decarboxylase, hypothetically, an unknown cellular
decarboxylase may perform the function of MccE1Eco.
However, in the presence of SAM, neither MccM1Bsu nor
MccM2Bsu modified the purified adenylated precursor MccABsu
(Figure S9) in an in vitro assay (Figure S10). Moreover, we
never observed a mass ion corresponding to 3-carboxy-3-
aminopropylated peptidyl-adenylate, the putative intermediate
product of this reaction (Figures 1B and 3E, right panels), in
samples prepared from whole cells. We therefore surmised that
MccM1Bsu/MccM2Bsu may utilize decarboxylated SAM
(dcSAM), an intermediate product of the spermidine
biosynthetic pathway, as a source of the aminopropyl group.
To test this hypothesis in vitro, we converted SAM into
dcSAM using the purified housekeeping E. coli SAM
decarboxylase SpeD (Figure S11) and added it as a substrate

peptidyl adenylate by sequentially adding two aminopropyl

The Presence of Two Aminopropyl Groups Increases
the Bioactivity of McC. Purified Bsu-McC did not affect the
growth of B. subtilis 168 and had only a minor inhibitory effect
on the growth of E. coli BL21, even at 2 mM (Figure S13). The
resistance of B. subtilis could be due to the absence of the
YejABEF oligopeptide transporter10 or the presence of the
genome-encoded S51 family peptidase MccG, which cleaves
the carboxamide bond of isoasparaginyl-adenylate, thus
providing immunity to McC-like compounds.26 However, the
lack of the Bsu-McC inhibitory effect on E. coli cells is puzzling.
Theoretically, it could be caused by the poor uptake of Bsu-
McC by E. coli YejABEF, inefficient processing of the Bsu-McC
peptide by E. coli cellular aminopeptidases, or weak binding to
the cellular target.
To address this conundrum, we first performed in silico
docking of tandem-aminopropylated isoasparagine-adenylate
into the active site of AspRS using the crystal structure of the
E. coli AspRS in complex with tRNAAsp and aspartyl-adenylate
(PDB: 1C0A)27 as a template for modeling. The docking
model, generated using CB-Dock228 and further refined with
Molsoft ICM-Pro 3.9, shows that without tRNA, the Bsu-McC
warhead fits nicely into the enzyme’s catalytic pocket without
clashes with the polypeptide backbone or side chains (Figure
5A). The isoasparagine-adenylate moiety of Bsu-McC super-
imposes well with the natural substrate aspartyl-adenylate with
a root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) of 0.48 Å, making
similar hydrogen bonds and polar and van der Waals contacts
with AspRS active site residues (Figure 5A−C). The tandem
aminopropyl groups of the Bsu-McC warhead contribute to
binding by interacting with several polar and hydrophobic side
chains of the AspRS residues. Of these, the most prominent
contact is made by the secondary amine of the proximal
aminopropyl group, which is predicted to form a hydrogen
bond with the carboxylic group of E482 (Figure 5B). The
more flexible distal aminopropyl group makes polar contacts
via its primary amine with the carboxylic group of E437 and
carbonyl of H448, while the propyl group makes hydrophobic
interactions with the side chain of M447 (Figure 5B).
The model also suggests that the warhead placement in the
catalytic pocket does not interfere with tRNA binding. Instead,
the phosphates of the tRNA 3′-terminal dinucleotide electro-
statically attract the positively charged amines of Bsu-McC
aminopropyl groups, further stabilizing its binding to AspRS
(Figure S14A). Moreover, the presence of tRNA in the
complex with AspRS sterically blocks access to the active site
pocket, hindering Bsu-McC warhead dissociation (Figure
S14B). These observations suggest that the extended
interactions of the two aminopropyl groups with AspRS and
tRNA should enhance the stability of the enzyme−inhibitor
complex. Therefore, similar to the warhead of the Eco-McC,
the warhead of Bsu-McC is expected to inhibit the activity of E.
coli aspartyl-tRNA synthetase.
Inspired by our in-silico model, we generated a doubly
aminopropylated E. coli McC by using purified E. coli McC
(Eco-McCap) as a substrate for aminopropylation by MccM2Bsu
in the presence of dcSAM (Figure S15). The unmodified (Eco-
McC), monoaminopropylated (Eco-McCap), and tandem-
aminopropylated (Eco-McCapap) compounds were evaluated
for their inhibitory activity toward McC-sensitive E. coli BL21
using a drop-diffusion assay. Results showed that the 0.25 μM
Eco-McCapap produced a growth inhibition zone comparable to
1 μM monoaminopropylated peptidyl-adenylate or 8 μM
unmodified E. coli peptidyl-adenylate (Figure 5D). As
expected, moderate overexpression of AspRS, but not ProRS,
protected E. coli BL21 against Eco-McCap and Eco-McCapap
toxicity (Figure S16). These findings are in line with our in-
silico prediction, revealing that the addition of the second
aminopropyl group significantly enhances Eco-McC’s toxicity
against E. coli BL21, reducing the apparent MIC by 4-fold and
32-fold compared to the monoaminopropylated and unmodi-
fied compounds, respectively.

of B. subtilis Strains. While studying the Bsu-McC

■DISCUSSION
Secondary modifications such as macrocyclization, oxidation,
hydroxylation, dehydration, epimerization, acylation, glycosy-
lation, etc., are common in RiPPs.29 These modifications are
necessary for enhanced bioactivity,30,31 improved the stability
of the molecules,22,32 or allow to overcome resistance.9 Among
the enzymes involved in the secondary modification of natural
compounds, SAM-dependent methyltransferases are among
the most common. These enzymes typically use SAM as a
methyl group donor, transferring it to a heteroatom or, less
often, to a carbon atom in RiPPs.33 In contrast, the use of SAM
as an ACP donor is very rare with only a few known
biochemical reactions. These include posttranscriptional
modification of tRNAs34 and biosynthesis of siderophores
such as staphylopine35 and pseudopaline,36 the antibiotic
nocardicin A,37 and several other small molecules.38 Unlike
most other natural compounds, the biosynthesis of McC relies
on SAM as a source of the aminopropyl group. Consequently,
decarboxylation of the ACP group should occur either before
or after its transfer to the phosphate moiety of McC. In E. coli
or Y. pseudotuberculosis McC, the aminopropylation reaction
involves two steps: first, aminocarboxypropyltransferase MccD
transfers the ACP group to the phosphate of the peptidyl-
nucleotide compound, followed by ACP decarboxylation by
pyridoxal-5′-phosphate (PLP)-dependent decarboxylase
MccE1 (Figure 1B).9,19 Interestingly, similar coupled reactions
were described in the biosynthesis of several natural translo-
case I inhibitors, including caprazamycin, sphaerimicin,
muraymycin, and others. However, the first step in these
reactions is catalyzed by an unusual PLP-dependent amino-
butyryltransferase, while the decarboxylation step is performed
by Mur23,39 which is homologous to MccE1 (22% sequence
identity).
The AlphaFold-predicted structures of B. subtilis 3-amino-
propyltransferases, MccM1Bsu and MccM2Bsu,40 exhibit sig-
nificant structural similarity to the 3-amino-3-carboxyamino-
propyltransferase MccDEco (Supplementry Figure S18). How-
ever, unlike MccDEco, they show high specificity for dcSAM as
the aminopropyl group donor (Figures 5C, S10, and S12). The
structural determinants that restrict the substrate specificity of
these enzymes to dcSAM have yet to be elucidated. Notably,
dcSAM is a key intermediate in the biosynthesis of spermidine
and spermine, which are found in both prokaryotic41 and
eukaryotic42 cells. To the best of our knowledge, MccM1Bsu
and MccM2Bsu are the first known enzymes outside the
polyamine biosynthetic pathway to utilize dcSAM as a
substrate.
Using dcSAM as an aminopropyl donor may seem like a
more efficient strategy and therefore might be expected to be
more widespread in nature. However, mcc BGCs containing
the mccD-mccE1 gene pair are found across diverse bacterial
phyla,17 while BGCs encoding mccM1/mccM2 homologues are
restricted to a small subset of Bacilli. This distribution may
reflect the metabolic cost of producing aminopropylated McC
within the cell. For instance, disruption of the spermidine
biosynthetic pathway inhibits the planktonic growth of Gram-
negative bacteria,43 whereas Gram-positive B. subtilis44 and
Streptococcus pneumoniae45 can grow normally without
producing spermidine. This suggests that repurposing a
portion of the cellular dcSAM pool toward McC-ap biosyn-
thesis may impose a smaller fitness burden on Gram-positive
bacteria than on Gram-negative bacteria. Additionally, unlike
SAM, dcSAM is not a universal metabolite. Many bacteria lack
SAM decarboxylase and instead rely on alternative spermidine
biosynthesis pathways that bypass dcSAM.46 Thus, using SAM
as a source of the 3-amino-3-carboxypropyl group, which
requires further decarboxylation of the intermediate product by
a dedicated enzyme like MccE1, represents a more adaptive
aminopropylation strategy. This versatility may have con-
tributed to the broader distribution of BGCs containing mccD/
mccE1 homologues across bacterial species.
Adding a single aminopropyl group to McC enhances its
bioactivity 8-fold, while the second aminopropyl group
increases it 4-fold further (Figure 5D). This significant increase
likely results from improved molecular fit and stability within
the AspRS active site, which leads to more effective enzyme
inhibition. Our structural modeling supports this view,
providing insights into the molecular basis for the improved

complex and sterically hinder the Bsu-McC warhead

B subtilis are among the most prevalent microbes in the
rhizosphere, competing with diverse bacterial species.47
Production of antibiotics and quorum-sensing inhibitors and
the formation of root-associated biofilms are the bases for
effective biocontrol and prevention of bacterial diseases of
plants.48−50 Although the effects of Bsu-McC on the soil
microbiome remain unknown, we observed an unexpected
impact on the social behavior of the producer strain. Both the
natural Bsu-McC producer B. subtilis MG27 and the laboratory
strain of B. subtilis 3A38 heterologously expressing mccBsu
exhibited reduced biofilm formation (Figure 6). Unlike some
RiPPs (e.g., lanthipeptides SapB,51 AmfS,3 and SapT52) that
induce morphogenic effects like sporulation in certain
Streptomyces strains, Bsu-McC inhibits biofilm formation rather
than promoting it and does not act as a paracrine signal on
bacilli if supplemented in trans (Figure S17). Its production
likely disrupts metabolic pathways, slowing down the complex
regulatory cascade that leads to biofilm development.53,54 For
instance, Bsu-McC biosynthesis could cause a reduction in
cellular dcSAM concentration, thus hindering spermidine
synthesis and postponing bacilli entering the biofilm state.44
However, deletion of mccM1Bsu/M2Bsu did not rescue this
phenotype (Figure 6), suggesting the effect is not due to
reduced dcSAM or spermidine synthesis.44
A second possible explanation is ATP depletion in cells
actively producing the peptidyl-adenylate. In B. subtilis, the
expression of many genes strongly depends on the cytoplasmic
levels of ATP or GTP.55 Biosynthesis of Bsu-McC might
elevate (p)ppGpp levels, as seen in E. coli, where AspRS
activity is inhibited by the accidentally released McC warhead,
eliciting stringent response.56 Accumulation of high levels of
(p)ppGpp with concomitant depletion of the intracellular
concentration of GTP may lock B. subtilis cells in a motile
state, preventing their differentiation and biofilm formation.57
This could explain the actively growing flat colony morphology
observed in Bsu-McC-producing B. subtilis strains. Whether the
Bsu-McC biosynthesis depletes the cellular energy pools or
triggers complex regulatory cascades warrants further inves-
tigation, which could reveal novel insights into microbial
physiology.

into a Petri dish and allowed to solidify. For the mccE2Bsu

additionally supplemented with 1- and 10-mM arabinose,
respectively. 3-μL drops of the serially diluted McC solutions
were deposited on the surface of the soft agar and allowed to
absorb. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 12 h to form a
bacterial lawn, and the growth inhibition zones became visible.
The compound’s minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC) was
defined as the minimal concentration of an antibiotic at which
the growth inhibition zone was observable.
