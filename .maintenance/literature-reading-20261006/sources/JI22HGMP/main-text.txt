Emerindanols A and B: Two Bipolyhydroindenol Sesterterpenes with
5/6−6/5 Coupled Ring System Discovered by Genome Mining

ABSTRACT: Genome mining of Emericella sp. XL-029 achieved a
new type E sesterterpene synthase, EmES, which affored a novel
bipolyhydroindenol sesterterpene, emerindanol A. Heterologous
coexpression with the upstream P450 oxidase revealed C-4
hydroxylated product, emerindanol B. Notably, emerindanols A
and B represented the first sesterterpenes featuring a unique 5/6−6/
5 coupled ring system. EmES was postulated to initiate through
C1−IV−V pathway and convert the fused ring intermediate into the
final coupled ring product through a spiro skeleton.
S
esterterpenoids, a relatively small subclass of terpenes,
exhibit the ability to form intricate polycyclic structures
with diverse pharmacological properties owing to their 25-
carbon precursor geranylfarnesyl diphosphate (GFPP).1,2 For
instance, novel ophiobolin-derived sesterterpenoids with
potential HMG-CoA reductase inhibitory activity were isolated
from Bipolaris sp. TJ403-B1;3 six cytotoxic asperane-type
sesterterpenoids known as asperunguisins A−F were charac-
terized by a unique hydroxylated 7/6/6/5 tetracyclic system
and isolated from the endolichenic fungus Aspergillus unguis;4
two unusual sesterterpenoids, aspterpenacids A and B, with a
carbon skeleton consisting of a 5/3/7/6/5 ring system were
obtained from the mangrove endophytic fungus Aspergillus
terreus H010;5 peniroquesines A−C possessing a fused 5/6/5/
6/5 pentacyclic ring system were isolated from Penicillium
roqueforti YJ-14;6 seven terpestacin-type sesterterpenoids with
anti-inflammatory activity were obtained from the phytopatho-
genic fungus Bipolaris maydis.7 Consequently, these intriguing
properties of sesterterpenoid stimulate continuous exploration
within their skeletal structures.
Fungal sesterterpene synthases (STS) are distinguished by
their bifunctional architecture, comprising C-terminal prenyl-
transferase (PT) domains that are essential for generating
GFPP and N-terminal terpene cyclase (TC) domains that play
a crucial role in catalyzing the cyclization of GFPP.8 They
exhibit precise catalytic mechanisms that adeptly transform
linear substrates into diverse and complex skeletons, thus
contributing to the remarkable variety of sesterterpenes found
in nature.2,9 In recent years, genome mining technology has
been increasingly employed to explore STS, leading to the
discovery of fascinating sesterterpenes such as sesterfisherol
with a 5/6/8/5 tetracyclic ring system synthesized by NfSS,10
astellifadiene with a 6/8/6/5 tetracyclic ring system synthe-
sized by EvAS,11 fusoxypene A with a 5/6/7/3/5 pentacyclic
ring system produced by FoFS,12 preasperterpenoid A with a
5/7/3/6/5 pentacyclic ring system synthesized by PvPS
(Figure 1).13 To date, 52 fungal STSs have been identified
and characterized for their ability to produce approximately
100 sesterterpenes with diverse scaffolds.8 Based on their initial
carbon cation formation strategy, fungal STSs can be divided
into Clade I STSs initiating cyclization by forming a 15−5-
membered ring system and Clade II producing an 11−5-
membered ring system. This gives Clade I STSs an advantage
in forming multiring skeletons. Phylogenetic analysis reveals
that within Clade I there were only two characterized type E
STSs (PaSS and PbSS), representing the fewest subtypes
within this clade and leaving ample room for further
exploration (Figure S1).13,14
In the course of our ongoing endeavors to explore new STSs
responsible for structurally unique metabolites, the fungi
Emericella sp. XL-029 gained our attention due to the presence
of multiple terpene gene clusters in its genomic sequence.15
Subsequent genome mining efforts led to the identification and
characterization of a sesterterpene biosynthetic gene cluster
named eme, comprising a type E STS gene (EmES, accession
number WWS34622) and a P450 monooxygenase gene
(EmEP450, accession number WWS34621) (Figure S2).

The probe PbSS was employed to search for homologous
protein sequences from Emericella sp. XL-029, aiming to mine
new type E STSs. Two homologous proteins, EmQS and
EmES, were identified, and their proximity to P450 genes
emphasized their crucial role in sesterterpene biosynthesis
(Tables S2 and S3). The significant similarity observed
between EmQS and EvQS, as well as between EmQP450
and Qnn-P450, suggested their involvement in the synthesis of
quiannulatic acid isolated from Emericella sp. XL-029 (Table
S3).15,16 The bioinformatics analysis revealed that EmES
comprised a total of 718 amino acids, and the sequence
alignment demonstrated the presence of conserved TC domain
motifs (DDXXD and NSE), as well as conserved PT domain
motifs (DDXXD and DDXXN) (Figure S3). Furthermore, it
was found that EmES belonged to a new sub-branch of type E
STS with homology to PbSS (47% identity and 62%
similarity), suggesting that GFPP might serve as a precursor
for synthesizing unique sesterterpenes by EmES (Table S3).13
Additionally, it is anticipated that these resultant compounds
could undergo hydroxylation or oxidation through oxidative
catalysts provided by P450 enzymes, leading to derivatives with
modified functional groups.
To characterize the products synthesized by EmES, we
expressed EmES heterologously in Aspergillus oryzae (AO)
NSAR1.17 The A. oryzae transformant AO-EmES was created
via recombinant plasmid introduction. Subsequently, gas
chromatography−mass spectrometry (GC−MS) analysis of
the mycelial extract from AO-EmES revealed a new peak at m/
z 358 (Figure 2A and Figure S4). Ultimately, colorless
crystalline compound 1 (25 mg, 2.5 mg/L) was obtained from
the mycelial extract of AO-EmES (Supporting Information
section 1.8).
The molecular formula of C25H42O with five degrees of
unsaturation was determined by high-resolution electrospray
ionization mass spectrometry (HRESIMS) at m/z 357.3177
[M −H]−peak (calcd for C25H41O 357.3163) (Figure S5).

To do the further verificaion of EmES, we obtained its full-
length complementary DNA (cDNA) from the AO-EmES
strain and subsequently transferred into Escherichia coli BL21-
CodonPlus(DE3)-RIPL (Figure S17). Following isolation and
purification of the recombinant protein (Figure S18), we
investigated EmES’s catalytic activity in the presence of
magnesium ions (Mg2+), utilizing geranylgeranyl diphosphate
(GGPP) and isopentenyl diphosphate (IPP) as substrates. The
cyclized product 1 was detected through GC−MS (Figure
S19), providing further confirmation that EmES was
responsible for emerindanol A formation.
Cytochrome P450 monooxygenases play a pivotal role in
oxygenation functionality of terpenes. To gain deeper insights
into the function of cytochrome P450 gene EmEP450, which
encoded a modification enzyme closely located to EmES in
eme, we introduced it into the AO-EmES strain and
constructed transformant AO-EmES + EmP450. HPLC
analysis revealed an additional peak in the mycelial extract,
absent in wild-type or EmES-expressing strains (Figure 2B). In
order to identify the structure of this new compound, large-
scale fermentations were conducted, resulting in isolation of 2
(13 mg, 1.6 mg/L, Supporting Information section 1.8).
The HRESIMS data (Figure S20) revealed that compound 2
possessed a molecular formula of C25H42O2. A comparison of
the
1H NMR and
13C NMR data between 1 and 2
demonstrated that 2 had an additional hydroxyl group in
contrast to 1 (Figures 24 and 25, Table S5). Furthermore,
analysis of the 2D NMR spectra (Figures S23 and S26−S28)
confirmed that the hydroxyl group was located at C-4. This
finding indicated that EmEP450 specifically catalyzed the C4-
hydroxylation modification of 1. Based on the identical
NOESY correlations (Figure S29), 2 and 1 were speculated
to share the same relative configuration of the skeletal
structure. Moreover, significant NOESY correlations of H-4/
H3-23 suggested an α-orientation of 4-OH (Figure S23).

Compounds 1 and 2 equipped a novel 5/6−6/5 coupled
ring system, suggesting a distinctive cyclization pathway and
highlighting the distinct function of their synthase. Based on
the genetic classification of EmES, we proposed a cyclization
mechanism involving the C1−IV−V cyclization pathway
(Figure 4).3,10 This process initiated with GFPP ionization,
followed by 1,15−14,18-cyclizations through carbocation-
mediated double annulation processes leading to intermediate
a. Subsequent 1,5-hydrogen shift and 6,10-cyclization resulted
in intermediate b,18 which could undergo sequential 1,2- and
1,5-hydrogen shifts to generate intermediate d. The
intermediate e was generated via a 2,11-cyclization reaction,
followed by the subsequent formation of g through a 1,5-
hydrogen shift and methyl group migration. Following this
sequence of events, two consecutive ring shrinkage reactions
predominantly mediated by carbocations induced the for-
mation of coupled ring skeleton i from the pre-existing fused
ring system with seven-membered rings on both sides via spiral
ring intermediates. Finally, deprotonation and addition
reaction with H2O converted i into 1. Based on this premise,
compound 2 was generated through 1 under EmEP450.
Emerindanols A and B represented the first fungal
sesterterpenes with a unique coupled ring skeleton. To date,
only three coupled ring skeletal terpenoids have been identified
from fungi: sesquiterpenoids α-cuprenene19 and trichodiene,20
and diterpenoid fujenal21 (Figure S30). However, plants and
marine organisms have been found to produce coupled ring
sesterterpenes such as linderasesterterpenoid A22 and anvilone
A,23 respectively, suggesting that the diversity of fungal
sesterterpene skeletons remains largely unexplored. The
discovery of emerindanols A and B in this study supports
this notion. Moreover, EmES is the first known STS capable of
catalyzing the synthesis of coupled ring sesterterpenes,
indicating its potential involvement in a novel catalytic
mechanism that warrants further investigation.
In summary, we have identified and characterized EmES, a
representative member of type E STS, capable of biosynthesiz-
ing novel tetracyclic system sesterterpenes from Emericella sp.
XL-029 through genome mining. Furthermore, we charac-
terized EmEP450 as a functional enzyme that hydroxylates at
the C-4 position. This extraordinary finding expands our
understanding of sesterterpenes and serves as evidence for the
awe-inspiring complexity and endless possibilities within
natural products. Additionally, it provides further support for
utilizing genome mining technology to discover unique and
intriguing chemical structures.24
