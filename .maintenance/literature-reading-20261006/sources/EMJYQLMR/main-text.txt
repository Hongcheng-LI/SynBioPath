Cytochrome P450-Mediated Skeleton Rearrangement of Taxadiene
in an Engineered Escherichia coli System

ABSTRACT: In this study, we constructed a taxadiene overproduction
platform and identified a cytochrome P450, CYP701A8, that activates
the inert C−H bonds in taxadiene to produce three oxidized products
(1−3). Compound 1 possesses a newly identified 1 (15→11)
abeotaxane skeleton, while 3 features a distinctive 6/10-fused
carbocyclic core with an α,β-unsaturated ketone moiety. Our quantum
computations suggested a carbocation-driven rearrangement in the
formation of 1. These results support CYP701A8 as a promising
biocatalyst for the generation of novel taxane diterpenoids.
T
axol is a prominent and effective antitumor agent widely
used in treating breast, lung, and ovarian cancers.1
Structurally, it possesses a 6−8−6−4 core bearing a highly
strained bicyclo[5.3.1]undecane ring system with a bridgehead
double bond, as well as highly oxidized and postmodification
groups.2 In the past several decades, tremendous efforts were
channeled toward the isolation and characterization of more
than 550 structurally diverse taxane diterpenoids, which were
categorized into four subfamilies consisting of classical taxanes,
secotaxanes, abeotaxanes, and cyclotaxanes (Figure 1 and
Figure S1).3,4 Despite these efforts, the commercialization of
Taxol faces challenges due to inadequate supplies, primarily
resulting from low levels of accumulation in yew plants.5,6 To
address this, more than 60 research groups have completed 10
total syntheses.2 Noteworthy among these is the two-phase
strategy reported by Baran and co-workers, which involves a
cyclase phase and an oxidase phase, mimicking the biosynthesis
pathway.7 However, an obstacle in this synthetic pathway is the
inability to achieve the C−H functionalization of the two
C(sp3)−H bonds at C-1 and C-7 through directed oxidation.
Instead, specific oxidation steps and particular chemical
reagents are required, leading to multiple revisions of the
synthesis route.8
How to efficiently achieve the stereoselective and
regioselective functionalization of inert carbons is a pivotal
challenge in the organic synthesis of Taxol using the two-phase
strategy. Enzymes, particularly the fascinating biocatalysts
cytochrome P450s (P450s), have become paramount in
addressing this challenge. P450s, belonging to a superfamily
of heme monooxygenases, exhibit versatility in their catalytic
roles, primarily excelling in the hydroxylation of inert C−H
bonds. Their substrate scope further enables them to interact
with a remarkable range of complex substrates, highlighting
their potential as potent biocatalysts.9−12 Inspired by Baran’s
two-phase strategy and natural biosynthesis pathways of

Taxol,7,8 we envisioned a chemoenzymatic synthesis strategy.
This strategy involves utilizing an engineered Escherichia coli
platform for the initial reconstruction of the taxane carbon
skeleton, followed by skeleton postmodifications using specific
P450s and organic chemical tools to achieve stereoselective
and regioselective functionalization of C−H bonds. In this
study, we constructed a truncated artificial pathway to
efficiently produce taxadiene. We further explored the
capabilities of a P450, CYP701A8, which selectively activated
the C−H bonds of taxadiene, resulting in the production of
three oxidation products. Notably, among these products are
two taxane skeletons that have not been reported previously.
We have successfully established a truncated artificial
pathway for the production of diterpenes, including terpente-
triene and ent-kaurene, at an approximate yield of 100 mg/
L.13,14 This platform was employed for the synthesis of
taxadiene (Figure 2A and Figures S2 and S3). The first system,
pLD10001, containing phoN and ipk, can produce substantial
quantities of DMAPP and IPP in E. coli.13,15 Next, we
constructed plasmid pLD10117, harboring the genes for
isopentenyl diphosphate isomerase (idi), geranylgeranyl
diphosphate synthase (ggdps), and taxadiene synthase (ts).
Co-expressing pLD10001 and pLD10117 in E. coli BL21(DE3)
resulted in strain DL10107. After a 5 day fermentation in
lysogeny broth (LB) medium, taxadiene was successfully
produced. Optimization of the concentrations of glycerol,
isopentenol (ISO), dimethylallyl alcohol (DMAA), and IPTG
and fermentation days (1% glycerol, 5 mM DMAA, 0.25 mM
IPTG, and 5 days) achieved a taxadiene yield of 20.3 ± 0.3
mg/L (Figure 2B and Figure S4).
In a previous study, the P450 enzyme CYP701A8 from
Oryza sativa demonstrated the ability to hydroxylate various
diterpene skeletons, such as ent-kaurene, pimaradiene, and
cassadiene, suggesting its broad substrate scope (Figure 2C).16
Consequently, we explored the potential interaction between
taxadiene and CYP701A8. Employing AlphaFold2, we
modeled the structure of CYP701A8.17,18 Molecular docking
results revealed the effective accommodation of taxadiene by
CYP701A8, with key hydrogen atoms positioned at C-18 near
the heme iron (within 5 Å), indicating a potential reaction site
(Figure S5). Utilizing our aforementioned truncated artificial
pathway, we observed that CYP701A8 catalyzed taxadiene into
three distinct products compared to the control DL10107
(Figure 3A). The MS data showed the molecular ions
corresponding to three new peaks at m/z 288, 288, and 286,
indicating potential oxidative products of taxadiene (Figures
S6, S15, and S24). To elucidate the chemical structure of these
products, we conducted large-scale fermentation of strain
DL10108 (45 L) to accumulate the target products.
Compounds 1 (12 mg), 2 (7 mg), and 3 (5 mg) were isolated
and subjected to one- and two-dimensional NMR, HRMS, and
other physical parameter experiments (Figure 3B, Figures S6−
S68, and Tables S4−S34).
Compound 1 was isolated as a colorless oil. Its molecular
formula, C20H32O, was established on the basis of HRMS(ESI)
m/z [M + Na]+ calcd for C20H32ONa 311.2345 (found
311.2344) and revealed five degrees of unsaturation. Its 13C
and HSQC spectra showed the presence of 20 carbon signals,
including five methyls (δC 16.5, 16.6, 23.3, 26.2, and 26.3), six
methenes (δC 22.4, 23.2, 28.2, 36.8, 39.3, and 40.2), four
methines (δC 41.5, 43.1, 122.2, and 128.6), and five quaternary
carbons (δC 34.6, 64.1, 77.2, 136.2, and 143.3). Careful
analysis of the 1H−1H COSY spectrum led to the identification
of three spin−spin coupling systems corresponding to H-5/H2-
6/H2-7, H2-9/H2-10, and H-1/H2-2/H-3/H2-13/H2-14. These
fragments were linked on the basis of the elucidation of
HMBC correlations. The correlations from H3-16 (H3-17) to
C-11 (δC 64.1) and C-15 (δC 77.2); from H3-18 to C-11, C-12,
and C-13; from H3-19 to C-3, C-7, C-8, and C-9; from H3-20
to C-3, C-4, and C-5; from H2-6 to C-5; from H-10 to C-11;
and from H-13 to C-14 demonstrated that it possesses an

planar structure of 1 was thus determined as shown in Figure
3B. In the ROESY spectrum (Figure S13), the key correlations
of H3-19/H-2b, H3-17/H-2b, and H3-16/H-1 suggested that

named 15-hydroxyabeotaxadiene.
Compound 2 was obtained as a colorless oil. Its molecular
formula, C20H32O, was established on the basis of HRMS(ESI)
m/z [M + Na]+ calcd for C20H32ONa 311.2345 (found
311.2356). Compared to that of the precursor of taxadiene, its
1H NMR spectrum displayed two characteristic signals of δH
5.21 (1H, br s, H-18a) and 4.96 (1H, br s, H-18b),
corresponding to a gem-disubstituted double bond. Addition-
ally, its
13C NMR spectrum showed a distinct oxidized
quaternary carbon resonance (δC 79.5, C-11). The HMBC
correlations of H-18a and H-18b with C-13 and C-11, along
with those of H3-16 and H3-17 with C-11 and C-15 (δC 41.6),
confirmed the presence of an exocyclic double bond and a
hydroxyl group attached to C-12 and C-11 in ring A,
respectively. Due to the lack of essential ROESY evidence
regarding the tertiary C-11 hydroxyl group, its relative
configuration was determined through a thorough comparison
of experimental and calculated 1H and 13C NMR parameters
(Figures S48−S57 and Tables S20−S27). The collective
results strongly indicated the likelihood of the 11R
configuration. Subsequently, TDDFT ECD calculations were
conducted on two possible enantiomers of 2 (Figure S58). The
results confirmed the absolute configuration of 2 as 1S, 3S, 8S,
and 11R. Thus, compound 2 was determined and named taxa-
4(5),12(18)-dien-11β-ol.
Compound 3 was obtained as a colorless oil. It has a
C20H30O molecular formula as deduced by HRMS(ESI) m/z
[M + H]+ calcd for C20H31O 287.2369 (found 287.2361),
indicating six degrees of unsaturation. Its 1H NMR spectrum
shows five alkene protons (δH 5.65, 5.60, 5.32, 4.90, and 4.74)
and three methyls at δH 0.79 (3H, s), 1.66 (3H, s), and 1.79
(3H, s). Its 13C NMR spectrum exhibits 20 carbon signals,
including one trisubstituted and two gem-disubstituted double
bonds, along with a carbonyl group (δC 207.3). In the 1H−1H
COSY spectrum, we identified three isolated spin coupling
systems corresponding to H-1/H2-2/H-3/H2-13/H2-14, H-5/
H2-6/H2-7, and H2-9/H2-10 as shown in Table S6. Through
analysis of HMBC correlations, including those from H2-16
(H3-17) to C-1 (δC 43.4) and C-15 (δC 149.0); from H2-18 to
C-11 (δC 207.3), C-12, and C-13; from H2-9 to C-8; from H2-
10 to C-10; from H3-19 to C-3, C-7, and C-9; and from H3-20
to C-4 and C-5, provided conclusive evidence connecting these
three spin systems to form the 6/10-fused structure with an
isopropenyl group. Thus, it is inferred that compound 3
possesses an unprecedented 11,15-secotaxane skeleton. The
key ROESY correlations of H3-17/H-13a determined that the
isopropenyl group was in the β-orientation. Finally, the
calculated ECD curve of (1R,3S,8S)-3 was in good agreement
with the experimental data (Figure S68). Thus, the structure of
compound 3 was determined and named 11-ketosecotax-
adiene.
To further confirm the catalytic function of CYP701A8, we
conducted in vitro and feeding assays (Figures S69−S77). First,
the codon-optimized CYP701A8 with its signal peptide
truncated and N-terminus modified was expressed and purified
from an E. coli expression system (Figures S69 and S70).19 We
used the original substrate, ent-kaurene, as a positive control.

The experimental results showed that CYP701A8 enabled
catalysis of the ent-kaurene into 3α-hydroxy-ent-kaurene with
the redox partner Rhfred and NADPH (Figures S71−S73).
However, no products were observed when we individually
incubated taxadiene and 2 with the in vitro enzymatic system of
CYP701A8, regardless of the use of any of the three redox
partners (CamA/B, Fdx/FdR, and RhfRed) or H2O2 (Figures
S74 and S75). This might be attributed to the limited solubility
of taxadiene and 2 in aqueous systems and the restricted
activity of CYP701A8 with unsuitable redox partners. In the
feeding assay with the CYP701A8-expressing E. coli strain,
taxadiene and 2 were fed individually. Taxadiene feeding
resulted in the clear detection of 1 and 2 in the GC-MS
spectra, while 3 was not detected with either substrate (Figures
S76 and S77). These results confirm CYP701A8’s role in the
oxidative rearrangement of taxadiene and its preferential
activity toward taxadiene over 2.
To the best of our knowledge, compounds 1 and 3 exhibit
two unprecedented carbon skeletons within the diverse taxane
diterpenoids reported.3 We then proposed possible pathways
for the formation of these two unique taxane diterpenoids
(Figure 4A). On the basis of the docking results and chemical
structures of compounds 1−3, we hypothesized that the
transformation of taxadiene by CYP701A8 might follow a
carbocation mechanism similar to the terpene cyclization or
methyl rearrangement mediated by AriF, PenM/PntM,
TwCYP71BE86, and VrtK during the biosynthesis of several
(mero)terpenoids.20−24 First, hydrogen abstraction and cation
formation at C-18 lead to intermediate B through a 1,3-
hydrogen shift, forming intermediate C. Alternatively, the C-13
hydrogen abstraction to directly form intermediate C has also
been proposed (Figure S78). Then, intermediate C undergoes
a carbocation shift to C-11, followed by Wagner−Meerwein
rearrangement to produce intermediate D, which, upon
quenching with water, yields 15-hydroxytaxadiene (1). A
different pathway involves the carbocation shift of intermediate
B to form intermediate E, which was confirmed by quenching
with water to create taxa-4(5),12(18)-dien-11β-ol (2). Finally,
compound 2 undergoes hydrogen abstraction at the C-17 site,
followed by C−C bond cleavage between C-11 and C-15 to
yield 11-ketosecotaxatriene (3).
To investigate the C →D cyclization mechanism, we
conducted quantum chemical calculations to evaluate the
feasibility of this transformation involving either carbocation or
radical catalysis (Table S35). As illustrated in panels B and C
of Figure 4, due to the intrinsic reactivity of carbocations, this
reaction overcomes a relatively low energy barrier of 7.2 kcal/
mol for the formation and cleavage of the C−C bond,
simultaneously releasing a total of 6.3 kcal/mol of free energy.
In contrast, the radical pathway presented a formidable energy
barrier of 54.1 kcal/mol for similar bond cleavage and
formation processes, resulting in a 2.3 kcal/mol reduction in
total energy. Thus, our calculations supported the occurrence
of C →D carbocation rearrangement.
In summary, our established truncated artificial pathway for
efficient taxadiene production, coupled with structural
modeling, led to the discovery of CYP701A8, a P450 enzyme
uniquely capable of activating C−H bonds in taxadiene. This
enzyme facilitates the carbocation rearrangement of taxadiene,
resulting in the synthesis of two novel taxane skeletons (1 and
3). These findings not only broaden the spectrum of taxane
diterpenoids but also highlight CYP701A8 as a promising
biocatalyst for taxane diterpenoids. Building on this platform,
we aim to explore additional P450s capable of activating the
inherent C−H bonds in taxadiene, further expanding the tool
box of biocatalysts for the chemoenzymatic synthesis or
synthetic biology of Taxol.
