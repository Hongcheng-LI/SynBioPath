ABSTRACT: Fungal secondary metabolite (SM) biosynthetic
gene clusters (BGCs) containing dimethylallyltryptophan syn-
thases (DMATSs) produce structurally diverse prenylated indole
alkaloids with wide-ranging activities that have vast potential as
human therapeutics. To discover new natural products produced
by DMATSs, we mined the Department of Energy Joint Genome
Institute’s MycoCosm database for DMATS-containing BGCs. We
found a DMATS BGC in Aspergillus homomorphus CBS 101889,
which also contains a nonribosomal peptide synthetase (NRPS).
This BGC appeared to have a previously unreported combination
of genes, which suggested the cluster might make novel SMs. We
refactored this BGC with highly inducible promoters into the
model fungus Aspergillus nidulans. The expression of this refactored
BGC in A. nidulans resulted in the production of eight tryptophan-containing diketopiperazines, six of which are new to science. We
have named them homomorphins A−F (2, 4−8). Perhaps even more intriguingly, to our knowledge, this is the first discovery of C4-
prenylated tryptophan-containing diketopiperazines and their derivatives. In addition, the NRPS from this BGC is the first described
that has the ability to promiscuously combine tryptophan with either of two different amino acids, in this case, L-valine or L-allo-
isoleucine.
P
renylated indole alkaloids are a diverse group of
compounds found in various organisms, including plants,
fungi, and bacteria. These compounds are characterized by the
presence of a hybrid chemical structure containing indole with
isoprenoid units attached to their periphery. The indole moiety
usually originates from L-tryptophan. Alternatively, the indole
may originate from diketopiperazines consisting of tryptophan
and a second amino acid, such as proline, tryptophan, or
alanine (Figure 1). Prenylated indole alkaloids are known to
possess a broad range of biological activities that have been,
and continue to be, explored for their potential human
therapeutic uses. Some well-known examples include ergot-
amine, used to treat acute migraines,1 fumitremorgin C and its
analogue, investigated for their chemotherapeutic activities
against breast cancer resistance protein,2,3 and paraherquamide
A, investigated for its potent anthelmintic activity.4 In addition
to their pharmaceutical potential, prenylated indole alkaloids
have been explored as sources of bioactive molecules for use in
agriculture as natural pesticides.5 Due to their wide spectrum
of biological activities, prenylated indole alkaloids have become
the subject of significant interest in the fields of drug discovery
and natural products chemistry.6−8

The genome sequencing revolution has facilitated the
discovery of new natural products by investigating biosynthetic
gene clusters (BGCs) and the genes within them. Studies have
revealed that fungal secondary metabolite (SM) BGCs
containing dimethylallyltryptophan synthases (DMATSs) are
involved in the biosynthesis of prenylated indole alkaloids.
Numerous studies, moreover, have demonstrated the versatility
of these enzymes in catalyzing both normal and reverse
prenylations at various positions on the indole ring.8−11
In this study, we examined fungal genomes in the Joint
Genome Institute’s MycoCosm database and found a
particularly interesting BGC in Aspergillus homomorphus CBS
101889, a fungus that was isolated from soil located 2 km away
from the Dead Sea in Israel.12,13 BLASTP searches revealed
that while the genes from this cluster were homologous to
individual genes from several BGCs described previously, this
cluster seemed to have a previously unreported combination of
these genes. We reasoned that this unique combination of
genes might create novel SMs. We then heterologously
expressed the BGC in the model species Aspergillus nidulans,
a filamentous fungus with a well-developed heterologous
expression system developed in our previous studies.14,15 We
were able to isolate eight tryptophan-containing diketopiper-
azines produced by this BGC and determine their structures.
Six of them proved to be new to science. To the best of our
knowledge, this is the first report of C4-prenylated tryptophan-

■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Analysis of DMATS-Containing BGCs in MycoCosm.
We found an interesting DMATS- and NRPS-containing BGC,
which we designate the ahm BGC, in A. homomorphus CBS
101889 v1.0 during a search of the Joint Genome Institute’s
MycoCosm database (https://mycocosm.jgi.doe.gov/), which
contains 1517 ascomycete genomes that contain 3637 DMATS
and 17,879 NRPS genes. The BGC contains one putative
NRPS gene (that we have designated ahmA, JGI protein ID
446073), one putative reverse DMATS gene (ahmB, JGI
protein ID 428235), one putative normal DMATS gene
(ahmC, JGI protein ID 397919), and one putative flavin-
containing monooxygenase (FMO) gene (ahmD, JGI protein
ID 472955) (Figure 2A). Furthermore, the BGC is surrounded
by genes that are clearly not related to secondary metabolite
biosynthesis, suggesting that the cluster is small and is
composed of only these four genes. BLASTP search results
(Table S1) revealed that the closest NRPS homologues
described in the scientific literature were the NRPSs from the
echinulin family of BGCs, EchPS in Aspergillus ruber CBS
135680 (46% amino acid identity)11 and CriC in Eurotium
cristatum NWAFU-1 (46% identity)16 as well as the NRPS
from the acu-dioxomorpholine cluster in Aspergillus aculeatus
ATCC 16872 (42% identity).17 The acu-dioxomorpholine
cluster in A. aculeatus ATCC 16872 contains a gene encoding a
phenylpyruvate reductase, AdxB, that converts phenylpyruvate
into phenyllactate, which the NRPS then combines with
tryptophan.17 The ahm BGC did not contain a phenylpyruvate

Heterologous Expression of the ahm BGC in A.
nidulans Results in the Production of Novel Trypto-
phan-Containing Diketopiperazines. We amplified the
target genes from Aspergillus homomorphus CBS 101889
genomic DNA. Using fusion PCR, we then refactored them
into linear molecules with highly inducible alcA(p) and
aldA(p) promoters and selectable markers. We included 1 kb
yA and wA flanks used to target the fragments to their
respective loci in the A. nidulans genome (Figure 2B,C). Using
a strategy adopted from our previous study and detailed in
Figure 2B,C,14 we transformed this 28,013 bp construct into
the A. nidulans host strain LO11945, which was derived from
the A. nidulans strain LO11098.14 Complete genotypes of
strains constructed for use in this study are given in the
Experimental Section. LO11098 has eight BGCs from A.
nidulans deleted to reduce background SM complexity, seven
selectable markers that allow multiple sequential trans-
formations, and a deletion of the nkuA gene that greatly
reduces nonhomologous integrations of transforming DNA,
thereby resulting in high frequencies of correct integrations at
target sites. To increase SM yields, LO11945 also contains a
construct with the strong constitutive gpdA promoter driving
expression of the alcR transcription factor that, in turn, drives
expression from the alcA and aldA promoters.
The refactored strains were cultivated in lactose minimal
medium (LMM) and induced with methyl ethyl ketone
(MEK). After harvesting, the culture media and mycelia were
extracted with ethyl acetate (EA) and then analyzed by LCMS,

We then conducted a large-scale fermentation followed by

and

Compound 7 was isolated as a yellow oil with a molecular

On the basis of the compound structures and the putative
functions of the biosynthetic genes we expressed, we are able
to propose a biosynthetic pathway for compounds 1−8 (Figure

substrates to produce compound 1 as the cyclic dipeptide
backbone. Next, the reverse DMATS AhmB catalyzes the
prenylation at the C2 position of the indole ring to form 3,

normal DMATS AhmC to generate 5 or 6. Finally, compound

Molecular Genetic Techniques and Strain Construction. The
transformation procedures largely followed the previously outlined
methods,36−38 with a modification in which 100 mg/mL of VinoTaste
Pro (Novozymes) was utilized for protoplasting. Transforming
fragments were generated through fusion PCR, as described earlier,
with a few adjustments.36−38 The DNA polymerase employed was Q5
Hot Start High-Fidelity 2X Master Mix from New England Biolabs.
The annealing temperature was set at Tm (calculated for the primer
with the lowest melting temperature) plus 3−5 °C, where Tm was
computed as previously described37 based on the primer with the
lowest melting temperature. The extension time was 30 s per kilobase.
The flanking regions were approximately 1 kilobase with minor
variations tailored to primer design. Miniprep DNA was prepared for
diagnostic PCR, following the protocol described by Edgerton and
Oakley.39 The DNA polymerase employed was OneTaq Hot Start
Quick Load 2X Master Mix from New England Biolabs. The
annealing temperatures ranged from 55 to 60 °C, and the extension
time was 1 min per kilobase. Multiple diagnostic PCR reactions were
conducted for each gene.
Fermentation and LC-MS Analysis. Liquid LMM medium (15
g/L lactose, 6g/L NaNO3, 0.52 g/L KCl, 0.52 g/L MgSO4·7H2O,
1.52 g/L KH2PO4, 0.72 g/L fructose, and 1 mL/L trace elements
solution) was inoculated with 3 × 107 spores in a 30 mL volume and
incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 150 rpm in 125 mL flasks.
Supplements such as riboflavin (2.5 mg/L), pyridoxine (0.5 mg/L),
uracil (1 g/L), uridine (10 mM), p-aminobenzoic acid (1 mg/L),
biotin (0.02 mg/L), L-lysine (200 mg/L), or choline HCl (20 mg/L)
were added as needed. To induce alcA(p) and aldA(p), methyl ethyl
ketone was added into the medium at a final concentration of 50 mM,
42 h after inoculation. After 72 h of MEK induction, the culture
medium was collected by filtration and extracted with an equal
volume of EtOAc. The mycelium was extracted with 30 mL of 1:1
DCM/MeOH with 1 h of sonication. The resulting extract was dried
in vacuo to obtain an aqueous residue, which was then suspended in
30 mL of H2O and partitioned with 30 mL of EtOAc. To extract
acidic phenolic compounds, the water layer was extracted with the
same volume of EtOAc after acidification to pH 2. The EtOAc extract
from culture medium, acidified culture medium, or mycelium was
evaporated in vacuo and redissolved in 1 mL of 20% DMSO in
MeOH, and a portion (10 μL) was subjected to high-performance
liquid chromatography−photodiode array detection−mass spectros-
copy (HPLC-DAD-MS) analysis.
The HPLC-MS analysis was performed using an RP C18 column
(Phenomenex Luna Omega 3 μm Polar C18 100 × 2.1 mm) on a
ThermoFinnigan LCQ Advantage ion trap mass spectrometer. The
flow rate used was 125 μL/min. The solvent gradient consisted of
95% MeCN−H2O (solvent B) and 5% MeCN−H2O (solvent A),
both containing 0.05% formic acid. The gradient was run under the
following conditions: 0% solvent B from 0 to 5 min, 0−100% solvent
B from 5 to 35 min, 100−0% solvent B from 40 to 45 min, and re-
equilibration with 0% solvent B from 45 to 50 min.
Isolation and Characterization of Metabolites. To purify
metabolites, EtOAc was used to extract the broth from a 5 L culture.
Mycelia were also extracted with 800 mL of 1:1 DCM−MeOH at
ambient temperature overnight, and the resulting extract was
concentrated and re-extracted with EtOAc. Both of the extracts
were subjected to silica gel column chromatography followed by
further separation through semipreparative HPLC. Detailed proce-

Hydrolysis and Chiral TLC Analysis of Compounds 1 and 2.
The TLC analysis methods largely followed a previous study.23 A 2
mg amount of compound 1 was placed in suspension in 0.5 mL of 6
M HCl and heated to 110 °C in a microwave system for 1 h.
Following this, the mixture was cooled to room temperature and then
evaporated until dry. The resulting residue was reconstituted in a 50%
aqueous methanol solution (0.5 mL). For the analysis of the reaction
mixture, 2 μL of the reaction mixture was applied to the Chiralplate,
along with authentic samples (2 μL each) of D- and L-tryptophan and
D- and L-valine in the form of 0.5% w/v solutions. The plate was then
developed using a mixture of methanol, water, and acetonitrile (in a
ratio of 50:50:200, v/v/v). After development, the plate was allowed
to dry and subsequently immersed in a solution of 0.3% ninhydrin in
acetone. Gentle heating with a hair dryer facilitated visualization. The
D- and L-tryptophan, with Rf values of 0.48 and 0.60, and the D- and L-
valine, with Rf values of 0.44 and 0.53, were successfully separated and
identified using this method. The tryptophan and valine present in the
hydrolysis mixture of compound 1 were found to match L-tryptophan
and L-valine, and hence, they were confidently assigned accordingly.
Compound 2 underwent hydrolysis using the same procedure as
detailed for compound 1. In this process, D- and L-tryptophan, with Rf
values of 0.48 and 0.60, respectively, D- and L-isoleucine, with Rf
values of 0.44 and 0.55, respectively, and D-allo- and L-allo-isoleucine,
with Rf values of 0.46 and 0.55, respectively, were identified. The
hydrolysis mixture of compound 2 was found to match L-tryptophan
and L-isoleucine or L-allo-isoleucine. In order to separate L-isoleucine
and L-allo-isoleucine, a mixture of methanol and water (in a ratio of
30:240, v/v) was used to develop the plate with L-isoleucine, L-allo-
isoleucine, and a hydrolysis mixture of 2. The L-isoleucine and L-allo-
isoleucine, with Rf values of 0.64 and 0.60, were successfully separated
and identified using this method. The hydrolysis mixture of
compound 2 co-migrated with L-allo-isoleucine and was therefore
assigned accordingly.
Marfey’s Method. The hydrolysates of compounds 1, 2, 3, 5, and
7 were prepared by the procedures shown above. Next, the
hydrolysates were resuspended in water (50 μL) and then treated
with 1 M NaHCO3 (20 μL) and FDAA (1% solution in acetone, 40
μL) at 45 °C for 1 h. The reaction was subsequently neutralized with
1 M HCl (20 μL) before UPLC-ESIMS analysis. Aliquots of 5 μL of
each analyte were then injected into a Waters Acquity BEH C18
UPLC column, 1.7 μm, 100 × 2.1 mm, maintained at 40 °C with a
solvent system composed of solvent A (water with 0.1% formic acid)
and solvent B (30% water, 70% acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid). The
flow rate was 0.2 mL/min, and the gradient was as follows: 10−40% B
from 0 to 1 min, 40−80% B from 1 to 21 min, 80−100% B from 21 to
22 min, and 100−10% B from 22 to 23 min.
Electric Circular Dichroism Calculation of Compound 7. The
relative configuration of compound 7 was determined through
analysis of the NOESY spectra and ChemBio3D modeling.
Subsequently, configuration optimization was carried out using the
DFT method at the CAM-B3LYP/6-31G(d) level in Gaussian 16
software (Gaussian, Inc., Wallingford, CT, USA). Theoretical
calculations of ECD were performed using time-dependent DFT at
the CAM-B3LYP/6-31G(d) level in MeOH with the polarizable
continuum model. Eight excited states per configuration were chosen
for the calculations. The resulting ECD curves were generated using
GaussView 6.0.
