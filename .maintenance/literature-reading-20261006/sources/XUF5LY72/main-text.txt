Enantioselective Alkene Azidooxygenation by Direct Visible-Light

ABSTRACT: While radical azidooxygenation provides a direct
route to valuable β-oxy azides, an asymmetric catalytic version
remains elusive. Distinct from the previous biocatalytic strategies
introducing azide moiety, here, we report a net-oxidative
photoenzymatic strategy that leverages direct visible-light excitation
of ene-reductases (EREDs) to achieve the highly enantioselective
azidooxygenation of alkenes. This system utilizes the photoexcited
flavin cofactor as a potent oxidant that, in concert with a TEMPO+-
azide complex, initiates the generation of azide radicals. The
ensuing N3 radical and O-centered radicals are then orchestrated
within the active site of the engineered enzyme to furnish
enantioenriched β-oxy azides in excellent yields (up to 89%) and enantioselectivity (up to 99.5:0.5 e.r.). Mechanistic studies
suggest the multifunctional role of 4-AcNH-TEMPO, which facilitates azide radical generation, stereoselectively traps the prochiral
carbon-centered radical, and completes the catalysis as the terminal oxidant. Computational simulations further elucidate the origin
of the stereocontrol.
■INTRODUCTION
Organic azides are highly versatile synthons with broad
applications across synthetic chemistry, materials science,
medicinal chemistry, as well as bioorthogonal reactions.1
Among them, β-oxy azidescharacterized by an azide (-N3)
and an oxygen atom on the adjacent β-carbonare especially
valuable as precursors to functionalized molecules bearing
vicinal nitrogen and oxygen atoms.2 The radical azidooxyge-
nation of alkenes presents an ideal synthetic strategy to β-oxy
azides, leveraging the ready generation of azide radicals and/or
the well-established role of 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidinooxy
(TEMPO) as a radical trap (Figure 1a).3 In 2013, Studer and
co-workers developed a versatile system employing an N3-
iodine(III) reagent as the azide radical source with TEMPONa
as a mild reductant and oxygen agent.4a Following this
pioneering work, several elegant chemocatalytic radical
azidooxygenation methods have been developed, including
TEMPO+BF4
−-mediated azidooxygenation,4b Mn-catalyzed
hydroxyazidation,5 and Cu-catalyzed alkoxyazidation/oxo-
azidation.6 Furthermore, visible-light-promoted7 and electro-
chemically generated8 radical pathways have been disclosed for
hydroxyazidation/azidooxygenation of olefins. Notably, in the
electrochemical azidooxygenation system, Lin and co-workers
developed an efficient azide radical generation way based on
the TEMPO-N3 charge-transfer complex.8 Despite these
advances, the development of an asymmetric version of this
reaction remains an unmet challenge. The primary obstacle lies
in controlling the enantioselectivity of the difunctionalization
involving the highly reactive azide and TEMPO-radical
intermediates.3,9
Recent advances in radical biocatalysis have revealed that
enzymesinherently sustainable and efficient catalystscan
be reshaped to orchestrate challenging non-natural radical
transformations with exceptional precision and stereoselectiv-
ity, as seen in metalloenzymes,10 photobiocatalysis,11 and
electroenzymatic synthesis.12 The development of biocatalytic
C−H azidation exemplifies this progress (Figure 1b). In 2014,
the seminal work of Bollinger and Krebs showcased that
nonheme iron enzymes could generate azide radicals (N3•) to
functionalize the C−H bond.13 Chang’s group characterized an
iron(II)/α-ketogluturate-dependent halogenase capable of
catalyzing the challenging radical azidation of free lysine.14
Lewis and co-workers achieved site-selective azidation of
unactivated C(sp3)-H bonds through direct evolution of an
iron(II)/α-ketoglutarate-dependent dioxygenase.15 To further
harness azide radicals, Huang and co-workers reported the
evolution of (4-hydroxyphenyl) pyruvate dioxygenase from

efficient and enantioselective photodecarboxylative C(sp3)−N3

On the other hand, nonradical enzymatic pathways to
organic azides have also been explored (Figure 1c). Zhang and
co-workers identified an adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and
nitrite-dependent enzyme (Tri 17) that catalyzed organic azide
synthesis through a sequential N-nitrosation and dehydration
of aryl hydrazines.20 Chen’s and Sun’s group, independently,
utilized engineered halohydrin dehalogenase (HHDHs) to
catalyze the ring opening of epoxides to form chiral β-hydroxyl
azides.21 Additionally, Chen’s and Paul’s groups, respectively,
have employed enzymatic cascades to achieve regiodivergent
and stereoselective hydroxyazidation of alkenes.22

Despite these elegant biocatalytic strategies for installing the
azido group, direct radical biocatalytic azidooxygenation of
alkenes remains elusive. With visible-light-induced radicals,
flavoproteins have been demonstrated to be highly versatile
radical enzymes. Pioneering research by Hyster, Zhao, and
others demonstrated that EREDs could be repurposed to drive
various net-reductive radical reactions via light excitation on
enzymatic EDA complexes.23 Recently, inspired by the natural
photodecarboxylases,24 our group and the groups of Ye, Xu,
and Wang independently reported the reshaping of EREDs for
redox-neutral hydroarylation,25 hydrolactonization,26 hydro-
sulfonylation,27 and hydroalkylation of styrenes.28 In contrast
to previously reported ERED-based photobiocatalytic systems
that operate via an electron donor−acceptor (EDA) complex
mechanism for net-reductive transformations,23 this redox-
neutral strategy29 relies on a single-electron transfer (SET)
oxidation triggered by the photoexcited ERED, thereby
eliminating the need for the native reducing equivalent
turnover system. Herein, we report the engineering and
repurposing of direct photoexcited EREDs to achieve net-
oxidative enantioselective azidooxygenation of alkenes (Figure
1d). By utilizing readily available sodium azide (NaN3) and 4-
(acetylamino)-2,2,6,6-tetramethyl-1-piperidinyloxy (4-AcNH-
TEMPO, 2) as both the oxidant and oxygen source, this
newly developed strategy enables the enantioselective radical
difunctionalization of alkenes, synthesizing enantioenriched β-
oxy azides (up to 89% yield and 99.5:0.5 e.r.).

■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Discovery of Initial Activity
To establish a biocatalytic platform for the previously elusive
enantioselective azidooxygenation of alkenes, we evaluated the
reactivity of a panel of wild-type EREDs using alkene (1a) as
the substrate, NaN3 as the azide source, and 2 as both the
oxygen source and the oxidant (Figure 2). Among the screened
ene-reductases, we identified the ene-reductase from Enter-
obacter cloacae (PETNr),30 which showed the highest activity
and best stereoselectivity, forming product 3a in 28% yield and
53:47 e.r. (see Table S1). Using wild-type PETNr, we further
optimized reaction conditions and obtained an improved yield

trifluoromethyl groups were tolerated (3a−3k), forming the
corresponding products with yields up to 89% and
enantioselectivities up to 99.5:0.5 e.r. The electronic properties
of substituents on the phenyl ring have little influence on
yields, whereas steric properties have a much greater impact.

substrate (3i) decreased to 13%, whereas para-fluoro-

Mechanistic Studies
To explore the catalytic mechanism, we conducted a series of
experiments. First, UV−vis absorption spectra suggested that
the flavoprotein was the visible-light-excited species under
standard conditions (Figure S5). Second, analysis of the redox
potentials of reaction components indicated that both TEMPO
(∼0.25 V versus Fc/Fc+) and NaN3 (∼0.45 V versus Fc/Fc+)8
could be facilely SET oxidized by the excited flavoenzyme
estimated to be ∼1.76 V versus SCE (Figure S11).25 Next,
luminescence quenching experiments were performed to
explore which component was initially oxidized. As shown in
Figure 4a, both 2 and NaN3 exhibit a quenching effect on the
excited flavoprotein, while 2 showed a quenching effect more
significant than that of NaN3.
Additionally, the control experiment in the absence of 2 gave
the formation of hydroazidation product 5, indicating that the
excited ene-reductase could oxidize NaN3, generating the N3•
to initiate the reaction without 4-AcNH-TEMPO 2 (Figure
4b). However, given the relatively low yield of 5 (12% yield)
and previous studies that have shown semiquinone form of
FMN (FMNsq)-mediated HAT is highly efficient,23,25,26 we
hypothesized that the initiation of azide radical might be
enhanced by the presence of 4-AcNH-TEMPO 2 under
standard conditions.
To understand the role of 4-AcNH-TEMPO 2 in the radical
initiation step, and inspired by Lin’s work,8 we prepared the
SET oxidized product of 2, 4-AcNH-TEMPO+ClO4
−. Indeed,
we observed a color change upon its mixture with NaN3
(Figure 4c and Figure S8). Being consistent with Lin’s
conclusion, it suggested the formation of the 4-AcNH-
TEMPO−N3 adduct. However, this complex is insufficiently
stable, which decomposes at room temperature (Figures 4c
and S8, < 1 s in HEPES buffer, < 40 s in MeCN/H2O (9:1)),
preventing our monitoring by UV−vis spectra.
Furthermore, we observed that along with the formation of
3a in 75% yield, the byproduct 6 (the reduction product of 2)
was detected with a yield of 64% and 2 remained (Figure 4d,
also see SI section 6.4). This result suggests that 2 can
reoxidize the FMNsq to regenerate the catalytically active ER-
FMNox species with the formation of byproduct 6, thereby
closing the cycle.
Collectively, we proposed that the 4-AcNH-TEMPO 2 was
the major component to be SET oxidized by the excited
PETNr-M3, which then triggers the formation of a N3-radical
through a 4-AcNH-TEMPO−N3 intermediate (CT complex
I). 4-AcNH-TEMPO 2 also functions as a radical trap to
furnish the stereodetermining C−O bond formation step, as
well as the terminal oxidation to turnover the flavoenzyme-
based net-oxidative catalysis.
Based on these results, we proposed a catalytic cycle for
alkene azidooxygenation (Figure 5). Upon blue light
illumination, enzyme-bound FMNox turns into highly oxidizing
species FMNox* (Int. A →Int. B). This excited species then
oxidizes 2 to form 2+ together with the semiquinone state
flavin cofactor FMNsq (Int. C). Concurrently, the complex-
ation of N3
−with 2+ takes place to furnish the charge-transfer
adduct (CT complex I). This intermediate will then undergo
uphill and reversible fragmentation, resulting in the formation
of 2 and N3• (Int. C →Int. D). In the presence of an alkene
(1a), the addition of N3• to the CC delivers the prochiral
benzylic radical intermediate (Int. D →Int. E). Next, the
resulting benzylic radical was captured by 2 within the
enzyme’s active site, producing the enantioenriched product
(Int. F). Finally, extra 2 oxidizes FMNsq to regenerate ground-
state FMNox, together with the formation of 6.
Computational Studies
To gain deeper insight into unnatural photoenzymatic
oxidative catalysis, we first performed time-dependent density
functional theory (TDDFT) and density functional theory
(DFT) calculations (Figure S13). First, the single-electron
transfer (SET) from 2 to the excited state FMNox*, forming
the cationic intermediate 2+ is exothermic by 12.3 kcal/mol
(Figure S13), supporting the hypothesis that visible-light-
triggered single-electron oxidation is thermodynamically
favorable. Next, the association of 2+ with N3
−to form
charge-transfer complex I (CT complex I) is exothermic by
25.4 kcal/mol. Although homolysis of this complex to release
the N3• is slightly endergonic (ΔG = 6.6 kcal/mol), the
subsequent radical addition of N3• across the CC bond of
substrate 1a is both thermodynamically and kinetically
favorable, effectively compensating for the initial energy
penalty and driving the reaction forward.
To investigate the stereoselectivity of the PETNr-M3-
catalyzed envisioned alkene, azidooxygenation (Int. D→Int.
F), we performed classical molecular dynamics (MD)
simulations and quantum mechanics/molecular mechanics
(QM/MM) calculations. The results indicated that the
reaction followed a radical addition →C−O coupling pathway
(Figure 6a,b). MD simulations revealed that substrates 1a, 2,
and N3• were stably bound in the active site of PETNr-M3
(Figure S14). Specifically, N3• forms a hydrogen bond with
W186, while 1a and 2 bind in the pocket via hydrophobic
interactions with L271, F68, F350, and F351, respectively.
Subsequent QM/MM calculations demonstrated that the
radical addition between N3• and 1a had a low barrier of
4.0 kcal/mol, yielding a stable prochiral benzyl radical
intermediate (Int. D→Int. E in Figure 6a).
Starting from Int. E, we performed three independent 100
ns MD simulations and found that the intermediate adopted
two distinct conformationsCH3-up (Int. E-R) or CH3-down
(Int. E-S)which led to the formation of R- or S-configured
products, respectively. Statistical analysis of key dihedral angles
indicated that Int. E preferentially adopted the conformation
yielding the R-configured product, consistent with the
experimentally observed major product (Figure S17). Further
QM/MM calculations revealed that the C2−O coupling barrier
between the prochiral benzyl radical intermediate and 2 was
10.5 kcal/mol in the CH3-up conformation (Int. E-R→TS2-
R) and 14.2 kcal/mol in the CH3-down conformation (Int. E-
S→TS2-S), indicating that the R-pathway was kinetically
favored (Figure 6b). Structural analysis revealed that there was
a significant steric hindrance between the methyl group of 2
and the −CH2−N3 substituent of the prochiral benzyl radical
in Int. E-S (key distance shortened from 3.56 Å in Int. E-S to
2.54 Å in TS2-S, Figure S22), which increased the energy
barrier and led to the formation of the R-configured product.
Finally, QM/MM calculations indicated that hydrogen atom
transfer (HAT) from neutral FMNH• to extra 2 involves a
barrier of 8.9 kcal/mol, regenerating the ground-state FMNox
and forming product 6 (Figure S24).
Notably, reactions of 1a with TEMPO (lacking the
−NHCOCH3 substituent) exhibit significantly reduced stereo-
selectivity (Figure S4), highlighting the role of acetamide in
enantiocontrol. As shown in Figure S17, three independent
100 ns MD simulations and statistical analysis of representative

■CONCLUSIONS
In conclusion, we have presented a net-oxidative photo-
enzymatic transformation based on the direct visible-light-
excited ene-reductases. This system initiates reactivity by
harnessing the strong oxidizing power of the excited-state
flavin, whichpotentiated by TEMPO+-azide complexation
generates azide radicals. The resulting N3- and O-centered
radicals are then tamed within the enzyme’s active site to
achieve a previously elusive enantioselective alkene radical
azidooxygenation. An interesting finding is the multifunctional
role of 4-AcNH-TEMPO, which acts concurrently to enhance
azide radical generation, stereoselectively traps the prochiral C-
radical, and serves as the terminal oxidant. Computational
studies provide a rationale for this reactivity, revealing the
flavin cofactor’s role as a visible-light-excited oxidant and
illustrating how the protein scaffold governs the enantiose-
lectivity of the radical trapping. We anticipate that this work
will inspire further exploration of the excitation of ene-
reductase for net-oxidative unnatural radical biotransforma-
tions.■ASSOCIATED CONTENT
