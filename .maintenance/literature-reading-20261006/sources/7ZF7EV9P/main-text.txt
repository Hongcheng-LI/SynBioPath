ABSTRACT: Parnafungins A−D (1−4) are fungal natural products that inhibit eukaryotic poly(A)-polymerase and were first
discovered by Merck & Co., Inc., through a Candida albicans Fitness Test (CaFT) screening program. The biological activity of
parnafungins is a result of the unique fused hexacyclic structure highlighted by a 5-isoxazolidinone (5ILD) N-heterocycle. In this
work, we characterize the complete biosynthetic pathway of parnafungins through heterologous reconstitution and enzymatic assays.
Nearly half of the 26-gene biosynthetic gene cluster of parnafungin is responsible for the production of a known polyketide natural
product, blennolide C. Starting from the blennolide C fragment, a three-enzyme cascade involving CoA-ligase ParJ, P450 ParO, and
DUF829 ParD catalyzes the formal biaryl cross-coupling between blennolide C and anthranilate. Subsequent oxidative cyclization
generates a phenanthridine product that is then reduced by atypical short-chain reductase ParT. N-Hydroxylation by flavin-
dependent monooxygenase ParB and subsequent lactonization catalyzed by a homologue of dienenolactone hydrolase ParF form the
5ILD ring and complete the biosynthesis of 1 and 2. Methylation of 1 forms parnafungin C (3), and lastly epoxidation forms
parnafungin D (4). Together, our work revealed the chemical logic and enzymology in extending the biosynthetic pathway of a well-
characterized natural product, blennolide C, to introduce considerable additional structural diversity that affords parnafungins with
unique biological activity.
■INTRODUCTION
Nitrogen heterocycles are among the most important building
blocks for pharmaceuticals. As many as 82% of all small-
molecule drugs approved by the FDA in the past 10 years
contain at least one N-heterocycle, and the chemical diversity
of medicinal N-heterocycles is on the rise.1,2 Natural products
have been important sources for bioactive N-heterocyclic
compounds, especially those that are underrepresented in
medicinal chemistry.3 For example, 5-isoxazolidinone (5ILD)
is a nitrogen-containing γ-lactone that has been found in a few
natural products from bacteria,4 fungi,5 plants,6,7 and sponges8
(Figure 1). To date, there are less than 10 such compounds in
the natural product database.9 While being extremely rare,
5ILD natural products have been endowed with various
bioactivities including antifungal4,5 and antifouling.8 Synthetic
5ILD compounds have also been explored as hypolipidemic
(Reglitazar, currently in clinical trials, Figure 1) and anticancer
agents.10,11 Deciphering the biosynthetic pathway and the
chemical logic of how Nature forms the 5ILD heterocycle will
therefore enable efforts to discover new natural products
containing this structural motif and will facilitate the
exploration of these compounds for medicinal and agricultural
applications.
To date, no biosynthesis of 5ILD has been reported. The
pathway for D-cycloserine, which contains a 3-isoxazolidinone
(3ILD) ring (Figure 2A), starts with N-hydroxylation of L-
arginine by heme-dependent enzyme DcsA.12,13 The hydroxy-
lated arginine is then converted to O-ureido-L-serine, which is
subsequently cyclized by an ATP-grasp enzyme DcsG to afford
D-cycloserine.14 The formation of 5ILD could follow a similar
pathway through the cyclization of an activated γ-N-hydroxyl
carboxylic acid (Figure 2B).
To understand the chemical logic and enzyme machinery
used by Nature to construct 5ILD, we investigated the
biosynthesis of parnafungins, the only fungal natural products

that contain this N-heterocycle (Figure 1). Parnafungins A−D
(1−4) are four related natural products isolated by Merck &
Co., Inc., from Fusarium larvarum (now Microcera larvarum)
and Fusarium sp. F-155,597.5,15 Using a Candida albicans
Fitness Test (CaFT) screening program, these compounds
were found to display potent antifungal activity by inhibiting
poly(A)-polymerase (PAP), an essential enzyme required in
mRNA processing.15,16 All parnafungin congeners contain
5ILD as part of an extended 6/6/6/6/6/5 fused ring system.
Parnafungins can undergo different nonenzymatic trans-
formations including C15a epimerization, ring-B isomerization,
and 5ILD (ring-F) opening under physiological conditions
(Figures 3 and S1), which complicates the isolation and
analysis of these natural products. The ring-F-opened
degradation products parnafungins A′ and B′ (5, 6, Figures 3
and S2), as well as parnafungins C′ and D′ (7, 8, Figures S1,
S2), have no antifungal activities, confirming that the 5ILD
moiety is essential for the bioactivity of 1−4.5 Rings A, B, and
C of 1−4 together resembles the xanthone natural product
blennolide C (9) (Figure 2B), which is a building block for the
xanthone dimer neosartorin (Figure S18).17 Based on the
biosynthetic logic of 3ILD, the 2,1-benzisoxazol-3(1H)-one
moiety of 1−4 (rings E and F) can be proposed to derive from
N-hydroxylation and subsequent cyclization with the carbox-
ylate group of anthranilate (Figure 2B). Given the instability of
5ILD and its importance to bioactivity, the formation of the
heterocycle is likely a late-stage transformation in the
biosynthetic pathway. Therefore, we propose coupling of 9
and anthranilate to be the first step in the biosynthetic pathway
that transforms 9 to 1−4 (Figure 2B).
In this work, we report the identification and heterologous
reconstitution of the par biosynthetic gene cluster (BGC) of
1−4. The BGC encodes all of the enzymes required for
formation of 9, as well as nine additional enzymes involved in
formation of all four parnafungin congeners. Our results
revealed an unexpected three-enzyme pathway for biaryl cross-
coupling between 9 and anthranilate and the enzymatic routes
for building the 5ILD N-heterocycle.
■RESULTS
Identification of BGC Involved in Parnafungin
Biosynthesis. To identify the parnafungin BGC, we
sequenced the genomes of two known producers of 1
Cladobotryum varium (NRRL36661) and Microcera larvarum
(F-159,080, one of the original strains reported by the Merck
group).18 Based on the biosynthetic proposal, BGC is expected
to encode enzymes required for the biosynthesis of
intermediate 9. Using this criterion, a large BGC (par)
containing 26 conserved genes (70−95% sequence identities of
homologues between two producers) was identified (Figure 4).
Among them, 13 par genes encode proteins that share at least
40% sequence identity to those from the neosartorin BGC
(nsr, Figure 4, Table 1).17 Eleven of these 13 nsr homologues
encode enzymes that are sufficient to produce 9 based on
published reconstitution studies (Figures 5A and S3).19 In
addition, P450 ParO shares 44% identity with NsrP, which
catalyzes biacyl coupling between 9 and 5-acetylblennolide A
to form neosartorin (Table 1, Figure S18).17 The remaining

Heterologous Reconstitution of Blennolide C (9). The
par BGC in its entirety with over 20 enzymes is beyond the
current limit of vector-based heterologous reconstitution. As a
result, the reconstitution of 9 with par enzymes was targeted
first from the commercially available intermediate chrysopha-
nol (10) (Figures 5A and S3). Based on a previous study, 10 is
first converted to moniliphenone (11) via homologues of NsrF
(ParR, DUF4243 dioxygenase), NsrR (ParS, SDR), and NsrG
(ParH, methyltransferase).17 Three additional enzymes that
are homologues of NsrQ (ParE, NTF2 isomerase), NsrK
(ParG, FMO), and NsrO (ParI, SDR) subsequently transform
11 into xanthone 9.19 Par genes from C. varium are chosen for
the heterologous reconstitution. Expressing ParR, ParS, and
ParH in S. cerevisiae RC01 (ScRC01)21,22 and feeding 10 to the
transformant resulted in the production of 11 as expected
(Figure 5B, Table S9, Figures S54−S59). Further coexpression
of ParE, ParG, and ParI in the host led to accumulation of 9
and blennolide C′ (9′) as a pair of diastereomers at C10
(Figures 5C and S4, Table S7, Figures S42−S47). 9 or 9′ also
undergoes retro-Dieckmann condensation in yeast to produce
gonytolide C (or C′, 9-RD) (Figures 5C and S4, Table S8,
Figure S48−S53). Notably, the set of enzymes ParRSHEGI
exclusively biosynthesizes 9 in the heterologous host. In
contrast, homologous enzymes in the nsr pathway produced 9
as well as closely related isomer blennolide A, two products
that are subsequently coupled to form neosartorin.19 Given the
propensity of 9 to epimerize and ring open, reconstitution of
the remaining par pathway was performed in a heterologous
host expressing ParEGI, with the more stable 11 as the starting
compound.
Biaryl Coupling Is the First Committed Step of
Parnafungin Biosynthesis. The biosynthetic proposal of
extending the pathway from 9 to 1 starts with the biaryl
coupling between 9 and anthranilate. The dimerization of 9
and 5-acetylblennolide A to form the natural product
neosartorin is catalyzed by the P450 enzyme NsrP (Figure
S18).17 In the par BGC, two putative P450 enzymes are
encoded (ParO and ParU). ParO shares a 44% sequence
identity with NsrP and was identified as a likely candidate for
the heterodimeric biaryl coupling. To test the role of ParO,
anthranilate and 11 were fed to ScRC01 coexpressing ParO
and ParEGI (which generates 9 in situ from 11, Figure 5A).
This resulted in the formation of four new compounds all with
molecular weights of 455, which is consistent with the
expected biaryl coupling product 12 (Figure 6A). However,
the biotransformation yield was too low for isolation and
characterization. The consumption of 9 by ParO was less than
5% compared to a control transformant expressing only
ParEGI (Figure S5). The low coupling efficiency suggests that
while the proposed role of ParO is correct, the true substrate

to 9-RD (Figures 6A and S6, Table S12, Figures S70−S75).

ParJ is predicted to be a CoA-ligase that shares 42%
sequence identity with cinnamoyl-CoA synthase Clz12 in the
biosynthesis of zaragozic acid A (Table 1).23 This prediction is
a common annotation for AMP-forming enzymes in the ANL
superfamily.24 Therefore, ParJ may activate the carboxylate
group of anthranilate via ATP-hydrolysis (Figure 2B). ParD
belongs to the family of DUF829 that has no characterized
members (Table 1). Structure prediction using Alphafold3 and
homology search in PDB using the Dali server suggest that
ParD is an α/β hydrolase with a serine protease-like catalytic
triad (S110−H251−D219) (Figure S8).25,26 Based on these
predicted functions, we propose that ParJ may catalyze the
biosynthesis of anthranoyl-CoA (AA-CoA), which may be the
natural substrate of ParO that is coupled to 9 to form 13
(Figure 6A). This CoA-containing adduct could then be
hydrolyzed by ParD to form 12 while regenerating CoA
(Figure 6A).

Collectively, our data show that the first committed step in
parnafungin biosynthesis is the formal biacyl coupling between
9 and anthranilate. This reaction involves a cryptic cascade of
thioesterification−oxidative coupling−thioester hydrolysis that
requires three enzymes. The reason for forming the
anthranilate thioester may be to increase enzymatic recognition
or as a method to sequester anthranilate from primary
metabolism for parnafungin biosynthesis. The involvement of
the uncharacterized protein family DUF829 was not
predictable at the onset of these experiments, which under-
scores the importance of using bottom-up approaches to
investigate biosynthetic pathways.
Reductive Amination Completes the Construction of
Ring-D. To complete ring-D in 1, a C−N bond forming step
between C4 and the aryl amine of compound 12 is required
(Figures 3 and 6A, atom labels follow those in 1). We propose
this C−N bond can be formed via a reaction sequence
consisting of oxidation of benzylic C4 to aldehyde, intra-
molecular cyclization to form imine 5, and reduction of the
imine to give 15 (Figure 7A). Compound 5, also known as
parnafungin A′, and the nonenzymatically formed isomer 6,
known as parnafungin B′, were coisolated with 1 and 2 (Figure
3).5 The initial C4 oxidation step could be catalyzed by either
ParP (nonheme iron and α-ketoglutarate-dependent oxygen-
ase) or ParU (P450 monooxygenase). Both enzymes were
individually expressed in ScRC01 together with ParEGI and
ParOJD, and the resulting strain was supplied with 11 and
anthranilate. Only the transformant with ParP produced four
new compounds, two of which share the same retention time,
mass spectra, and UV−vis spectra (Figure S7) with standards
of 5 and 6 purified from M. larvarum (Figure 7B, Tables S4
and S5, Figures S25−S35). The remaining two compounds (5′
and 6′) share the same mass and UV−vis spectra with 5 and 6
and are likely the corresponding C15a diastereomers (Figure
7A).
Reducing the imine of 5 to the secondary amine in 15 is
challenging since the phenanthridine ring system in 5 is highly
conjugated. Chemical reduction of phenanthridine typically
involves harsh conditions and strong reducing reagents such as
LiAlH4 or catalytic hydrogenation.27,28 Two putative short-
chain reductases (SDRs) are encoded in the par BGC, which
are ParC and ParT. While ParC is predicted to be a classical
SDR with a conserved catalytic triad (S137−Y165−K169),
ParT is an atypical SDR that lacks such a feature.29 Atypical
SDRs, such as triphenylmethane reductase and biliverdin IX
beta reductase, have been shown to catalyze reduction of
highly conjugated systems.30,31 Therefore, ParT is a more likely
candidate for the proposed reduction of 5. To test the
hypothesis, ParT was overexpressed in E. coli and purified to
homogeneity (Figure S9). Incubation of ParT with 5 and
NADPH indeed produced two new compounds, 15 and 15-B,
with a +2 mass increase compared to 5 (Figure 7C).
Compound 15 was isolated from a large-scale enzymatic
assay in an expeditious manner to avoid air oxidation to 5 that
occurs slowly over time. The NMR analysis of 15
unambiguously confirmed the presence of the secondary
amine (Table S14, Figures S81−S85). Compound 15-B shares
the same mass and UV−vis spectra with 15 and is likely the
isomer of 15 from the nonenzymatic rearrangement of ring-B,
which is the same as the previously described interconversion
between 1 and 2 (Figure 3).
These results show that oxygenase ParP and atypical SDR
ParT together complete the biosynthesis of ring-D in
parnafungins. How ParT can efficiently reduce the phenan-
thridine in 5 under physiological conditions remains intriguing.
Such imine reduction activity adds to the impressive list of
reactions catalyzed by atypical SDRs that remain mechanisti-
cally underexplored.

■DISCUSSION
Our study revealed the pathway for constructing the fused
hexacyclic natural product parnafungin A (1) and the
characteristic 5ILD N-heterocyclic moiety required for the
PAP-inhibiting biological activity. The lengthy biosynthetic
pathway extends from the characterized steps involved in the
blennolide C (9) formation. The par biosynthetic pathway
represents an interesting biological example of semisynthesis
that leverages the structural complexity already built into 9.
The extended pathways were investigated as three separate
enzymatic cascades: biaryl coupling via cryptic thioesterifica-

enzymatic assays was performed to aid enzyme functional
assignment. As described for parnafungin A (1), many
compounds investigated here readily undergo a series of
nonenzymatic isomerizations to give shunt products.
While P450-catalyzed biaryl coupling is well-characterized in
the biosynthesis of many phenolic natural products including
anthraquinones,40 xanthones,17 coumarins,41 indoles,42 and
cyclodipeptides,43 the cross-coupling between 9 and anthrani-
late mediated by the three enzymes ParOJD is unique. First,
most biaryl coupling P450s catalyze homodimerization
between two identical substrates or cross-coupling between
two structurally related substrates (Figure S18).17,40−42,44
ParO is one of the few natural P450s performing cross-
coupling of two structurally unrelated substrates (Figure
S18).43 The discovery of such P450s, therefore, broadens the
scope and biocatalytic potential of enzymatic biaryl coupling.
Second, biaryl coupling is typically a late-stage biosynthetic
step that forms the molecular scaffold of the final product. In
parnafungin biosynthesis, however, biaryl coupling is the first
committed step that sets the stage for further installation of
two heterocycles including 5ILD. Such chemical logic
diversifies the molecular complexity of xanthone natural
products.45 The use of the complex natural product blennolide
C (9) as the starting point represents Nature’s strategy of
semisynthetic structural diversification and can be leveraged in
fragment-based genome mining approaches.46 Lastly, the
involvement of the cryptic thioesterification and thioester
hydrolysis in biaryl coupling is unprecedented and reminiscent
of enzymes acting on carrier protein- or peptide-tethered
substrates.47,48 This new logic provides a potential platform to
use CoA or SNAC as a “substrate anchor” to further expand
the substrate scope and synthetic utility of enzymatic biaryl
coupling.49,50
The two enzymes involved in 5ILD formation are also
mechanistically intriguing. First, flavin-dependent N-monoox-
ygenases are grouped into type B FMOs that contain two
Rossmann-fold domains for separate NAD(P)H and FAD
binding.51 In contrast, ParB is a type A FMO that contains a
single Rossmann-fold domain for both NAD(P)H and FAD
binding (Figure S16). Recently, a few other type A FMOs have
been reported to catalyze N-oxygenation to form hydroxyl-
amines and nitrones.32,52,53 Together with ParB, these
examples show that flavin-dependent N-oxygenases do not
fall into a single group.
Second, the observed instability of 16 would in principle
require an efficient lactonization step to form 5ILD. It was
therefore surprising that 16 with a free carboxylate, instead of
an ATP-activated form, is directly lactonized by ParF. Metal-
dependent lactone hydrolases like gluconolactonase and
paraoxonase are known to catalyze direct lactonization of
hydroxy acids.54,55 However, ParF shares no homology with
these enzymes and instead utilizes a DLH-like catalytic triad
for direct lactonization. Based on the mechanisms of DLHs,56
one can propose that the C138 nucleophile, activated by the
H217/D185 dyad, may form an acyl-enzyme intermediate with
16 (Figure S17). Subsequent intramolecular attack of this
intermediate by the hydroxylamine releases product 1 with the
5ILD formed and regenerates the C138 nucleophile. However,
it is not clear how ParF activates the carboxylate group of 16,
which is a prerequisite for nucleophilic attack by C138.
Furthermore, how does ParF favor lactonization, while the
canonical function of members in the DLH family is to catalyze
lactone hydrolysis? Understanding these questions by struc-
tural biology and substrate scope analysis could lead to new
methods of ATP-independent lactonization under a neutral,
aqueous environment.57
■CONCLUSION
Our characterization of parnafungin biosynthesis uncovered a
repertoire of novel enzymology for constructing C−C, C−N,
N−O, and C−O bonds that are required for introducing
additional molecular complexity, including the rare 5ILD
heterocycle, to an already complex natural product. These new
enzymes and the uncovered chemical logic will facilitate the
genome mining of new natural products and associated
biosynthetic enzymes.
