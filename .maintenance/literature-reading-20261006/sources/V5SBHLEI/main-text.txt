Analysis of the Valgamicin Biosynthetic Pathway Reveals a General
Mechanism for Cyclopropanol Formation across Diverse Natural
Product Scaffolds

ABSTRACT: Cyclopropanol rings are highly reactive and may
function as molecular “warheads” that affect natural product
bioactivity. Yet, knowledge on their biosynthesis is limited. Using
gene cluster analyses, isotope labeling, and in vitro enzyme assays,
we shed first light on the biosynthesis of the cyclopropanol-
substituted amino acid cleonine, a residue in the antimicrobial
depsipeptide valgamicin C and the cytotoxic glycopeptide
cleomycin A2. We decipher the biosynthetic origin of valgamicin
C and show that the cleonine cyclopropanol ring is derived from
dimethylsulfoniopropionate (DMSP). Furthermore, we demon-
strate that part of the biosynthesis is analogous to the formation of
malleicyprol polyketides in pathogenic bacteria. By genome mining
and metabolic profiling, we identify the potential to produce
cyclopropanol rings in other bacterial species. Our results reveal a general mechanism for cyclopropyl alcohol biosynthesis across
diverse natural products that may be harnessed for bioengineering and drug discovery.
■INTRODUCTION
Cyclopropanol rings are highly reactive and important
functional groups in organic synthesis.1,2 The inherent strain
of three-membered cyclopropyl rings (27.5 kcal/mol)3 enables
them to undergo synthetically useful ring opening and ring
expansion reactions,1,2 readily forming β-keto radicals upon
single-electron oxidation (Figure 1A).4−6 In addition to serving
as useful intermediates for natural product synthesis, cyclo-
propyl alcohol rings are also directly found, though exceedingly
rarely, in natural products themselves. A total of eight
cyclopropanol-containing natural products (1a, 2, 3a, 4−8)
have been identified (Figure 1B).7−13 A cyclopropanol ring can
profoundly affect natural product bioactivity.13 An important
example of this is the virulence-associated cyclopropanol-
containing polyketide bis-malleicyprol (2a), produced by
human and animal pathogens of the Burkholderia pseudomallei
complex.13 These pathogens are responsible for the pernicious
and deadly human and livestock diseases glanders and
melioidosis.14,15 The cyclopropanol ring of the malleicyprols
is a “warhead” moiety essential for toxicity,13 as a derivative
lacking the ring, called burkholderic acid16 (syn. malleilac-
tone17) is inactive.
In addition to the malleicyprols, valgamicin C (1a) and
cleomycin A2 (3a) are notable for possessing the highly
unusual cyclopropanol-containing amino acids L-cleonine (L-9)
or D-cleonine (D-9).10,18 The valgamicins 1a−1d are cyclic
antimicrobial depsipeptides produced by Amycolatopsis sp.
ML1-hF4, with valgamicin C containing
D-cleonine (D-9)
(Figure S1),10,19 whereas cleomycin A2 (3a) is a congener of
the antineoplastic, cytotoxic polyketide-nonribosomal hybrid
bleomycin A2 (3b) containing L-cleonine (L-9) produced by
Streptomyces mobaraensis (syn. verticillus) ATCC 31307.18
Synthesizing cyclopropanol-containing amino acids has been a
target for organic chemists, but D- and L-cleonine are the only
examples found in nature.20,21 Given the inherent reactivity of
strained cyclopropyl alcohol rings, their ability to act as
molecular warheads, and interest in cyclopropyl alcohol-
containing amino acids, it is surprising that nothing is known
of cleonine biosynthesis. Aside from expanding our knowledge
on the biosynthetic mechanisms22−25 used to create strained
and reactive rings, understanding cleonine biosynthesis would
also accelerate genome mining efforts to identify new and
diverse cyclopropanol-containing natural products.

Here, we report our investigation into the biosynthetic
origin of cleonine and show that its cyclopropanol ring has a
common origin with that from malleicyprol, revealing a general
mechanism for cyclopropanol biosynthesis across diverse
natural product classes and a genetic handle for genome
mining.
■RESULTS
We noted that cleonine has profound structural similarity to
trigonic acid (10), the cyclopropanol-containing intermediate
that forms the cyclopropanol warhead of the malleicyprols.
Trigonic acid is unexpectedly derived from L-methionine via
the sulfonium acid dimethylsulfoniopropionate (DMSP)
(11),26,27 a marine metabolite central to global sulfur cycling.28
Cleonine and trigonic acid differ only in whether there is an
amino or hydroxyl moiety at the α-position (Figure 1C).
Furthermore, we reasoned that trigonic acid could theoretically
be converted to cleonine via oxidation of the α-hydroxyl to a
ketone, followed by a transamination reaction. Based on this
similarity, we hypothesized that cleonine and trigonic acid have
a common biosynthetic origin. Trigonic acid is synthesized by
enzymes encoded in the 36 kbp malleicyprol biosynthetic gene
cluster (named the bur gene cluster).16,26,27 The key steps for
trigonic acid biosynthesis were previously elucidated using the
safer-to-handle Burkholderia thailandensis as a model organ-
ism.26,27 L-Methionine is first converted into DMSP by the
enzymes BurBIDE, followed by the condensation of DMSP
with acetate by the polyketide synthase-nonribosomal peptide
synthetase (PKS-NRPS) hybrid BurA (TE-A-T-KS-AT-TE-
KR-T) to form gonyol (12), then the α-hydroxylation of
gonyol by BurC to form gonydiol (13), and finally the
cyclization of gonydiol into trigonic acid by BurG via loss of
dimethylsulfide (Figure 2).16,26,27
We reasoned that if the same biosynthetic pathway is
responsible for the cyclopropanol ring of cleonine, then
homologues of the genes encoding these enzymes (which we
have named the “trigonic acid operon”) will be present in the
genomes of the valgamicin C and cleomycin A2 producers.
We first investigated cleonine biosynthesis in the context of
valgamicin C produced by the Amycolatopsis sp. ML1-hF4. As
no genome data were available, we sequenced the whole
genome of this strain using next-generation sequencing,
revealing it to be 9 390 682 bp in size. Whole genome
nucleotide and 16S RNA phylogenetic trees placed Amycola-
topsis sp. ML1-hF4 within the Amycolatopsis orientalis (AOS)
subclade, group B (Figures S2 and S3).29,30 Amycolatopsis sp.
ML1-hF4 has a high biosynthetic potential, containing a total
of 40 different biosynthetic gene clusters, seven of which
encode NRPS enzymes (Figure S4). We searched for any gene
clusters containing the trigonic acid operon and/or genes
encoding enzymes for depsipeptide biosynthesis. Only one
gene cluster present in the Amycolatopsis sp. ML1-hF4 genome
meets these criteria: NRPS gene cluster 1 (Figures 3A and S5).
NRPS gene cluster 1 is 150 kbp in size and contains 9 NRPS
genes, collectively encoding 21 NRPS modulesfar more than
necessary to synthesize the six-unit valgamicin C. However, a
sub-portion of this larger gene cluster, which we have named
the val gene cluster, contains the genes valAI, valAII, and
valAIII, which encode NRPS modules whose domain
composition and sequence correspond well to the depsipeptide
backbone of the valgamicins (D-cleonine, L-proline, D-valine, D-
N-Me-valine, and two S-2-hydroxyvaleric acid units). Critically,
a nearly complete trigonic acid operon (valABCDGI)only
missing a gene (burE) for a dehydrogenase homologueis
also present just upstream of valAI−valAIII. We predict that
the enzymes encoded by these genes convert L-methionine into

ValAIII is likely responsible for the synthesis of S-2-
hydroxyvaleric acid because of its homology to bacterial
“depsipeptide modules”.31 Depsipeptide biosynthesis in
bacteria uses dedicated NRPS modules with an adenylation-
ketoreductase-thiolation (A-KR-T) domain organization.32
The A domains of these specialized modules specifically
adenylate α-keto acids, followed by NADPH-dependent
reduction of the α-keto group by the KR domain, forming
an α-hydroxy acid.32 The α-hydroxy acid is then used as an
extension unit in the depsipeptide biosynthesis. The
adenylation domain of ValAIII lacks the conserved Asp in
the A4 motif, used to stabilize the amino group of the selected
amino acid,33 consistent with the selection of α-ketoisovaleric
acid (17) (Figure 3B).32 A complete biosynthetic proposal for
valgamicin C biosynthesis is given in Figure 3A (a full
discussion of the scheme, including pargamicin A19,34 (18)
biosynthesis, is found in Supporting Results Text S1).
The presence of the trigonic acid operon in the val gene
cluster supports our model that the cyclopropanol ring of
cleonine is also derived from DMSP. To experimentally
determine if this is the case, we next turned to valgamicin
isotopic labeling studies in Amycolatopsis sp. ML1-hF4. For
isotopic labeling, we added DMSP−13C3 (19), L-methioni-
ne-13C5-15N (20), or the corresponding unlabeled controls 11
or L-Met to growing cultures of Amycolatopsis sp. ML1-hF4.
The supernatant of each culture was then extracted, and the
organic extract was analyzed using HPLC-HRMS. Valgamicin
C is one of four valgamicins produced by Amycolatopsis sp.
ML1-hF4, each of which contains a different amino acid in the
place of D-cleonine: valgamicin A (1b) contains D-alanine,
valgamicin T (1c) contains D-threonine, and valgamicin V
(1d) contains D-valine (Figure 1).10 We first verified the m/z
signals corresponding to each of the four valgamicins using
authentic references (Figure S6) and then examined these
same signals for changes resulting from the incorporation of
one of the supplemented isotopic substrates. The [M + H]+
ion of valgamicin C shifts +3 u when DMSP−13C3 is
supplemented (Figures 4A,B and S7). Critically, valgamicins
A, V, and T, none of which contain a cyclopropanol ring, are
not labeled by DMSP−13C3 (Figure 4A). The experiment
therefore confirms that DMSP is the precursor to the
cyclopropanol ring of cleonine in valgamicin C. Consistent
with this result, adding unlabeled DMSP to cultures of
Amycolatopsis sp. ML1-hF4 specifically and significantly
(unpaired t test, p = 0.0003) increases the proportion of
valgamicin C from 28.2 ± 2 to 73.2 ± 6% of the total
valgamicins produced (Figure 4B).
Corroborating results are obtained in the case of
L-
methionine-13C5-15N supplementation. In this case, the [M +
H]+ + 1 u signals of all four valgamicins increase from ca. 30 to
89% of the [M + H]+ base peak ion, indicating labeling of the
S-adenosyl methionine-derived N-methyl group of
D-valine
that is present in all valgamicins (Figure S8). However, only
the abundance of the valgamicin C [M + H]+ + 3 u ion
increases upon L-methionine-13C5-15N supplementation (Fig-
ures 4C and S8), consistent with the expected conversion of L-
methionine into DMSP, followed by the conversion of DMSP
into cleonine and its subsequent incorporation into valgamicin
C. Accordingly, we detected DMSP in the pellet of the control
culture (Figures 4D and S9A), and DMSP−13C3 when L-
methionine-13C5-15N was supplemented (Figure S9B), con-
firming that Amycolatopsis. sp. ML1-hF4 produces its own
DMSP.
Having confirmed that cleonine is derived from DMSP, we
next determined if other intermediates toward trigonic acid are
produced by Amycolatopsis sp. ML1-hF4. Indeed, we detected
gonyol in the extracted cell pellet of Amycolatopsis sp. ML1-
hF4, confirming the expected activity of ValA (Figures 4D and
S10). We also detected gonydiol, the downstream metabolite
of gonyol, and predicted product of the hydroxylase ValC
(Figures 4D and S10). The addition of DMSP−13C3,
correspondingly, led to the production of gonyol-13C3 (21)
and gonydiol-13C3 (22) (Figures S10 and S11). Interestingly,
neither trigonic acid nor free cleonine could be detected in any
of the Amycolatopsis sp. ML1-hF4 culture extracts, indicating a
rapid conversion and incorporation into valgamicin C.
Nevertheless, the detection of isotopically labeled DMSP,
gonyol, and gonydiol in Amycolatopsis sp. ML1-hF4 provides
strong evidence that the metabolites are intermediates to D-
and L-cleonine.
To evaluate whether this scheme is a general pathway to
cyclopropyl alcohol-containing amino acids, we next turned
our attention to cleomycin A2 produced by S. mobaraensis
ATCC 31307, the only other known natural product to
contain cleonine. Unfortunately, the UV-mutagenized version
of the S. mobaraensis that produces cleomycin A2 (ATCC
31307)18 was unavailable to us. Nevertheless, wild-type S.
mobaraensis is a known producer of DMSP,35 and we noted
that it contains the trigonic acid operon (though it is not part
of the cleomycin A2 biosynthesis gene cluster),27 both of
which support a conserved pathway from DMSP to cleonine.

By HPLC-HRMS-based metabolic profiling using an authentic
reference, we detected gonyol in the S. mobaraensis culture
extract (Figures 5A and S12), confirming that its BurA
homologue is functional. Notably, we also detected free
trigonic acid, demonstrating the ability of the strain to produce
cyclopropanol rings (Figures 5B and S13).
Furthermore, we confirmed that the BurG homologue
encoded in S. mobaraensis, named MobG (51% identity to
BurG), is responsible for trigonic acid formation in S.
mobaraensis by an in vitro assay. To achieve this, we
heterologously produced MobG, purified it by using nickel-
affinity chromatography (Figure 5C), and incubated it with
gonydiol. HPLC-HRMS analysis confirmed that trigonic acid is
produced in this assay (Figures 5D and S14).
Taken together, the production of trigonic acid in S.
mobaraensis by a BurG homologue supports our model of a
conserved pathway for cleonine biosynthesis in both S.
mobaraensis and Amycolatopsis sp. ML1-hF4.

bacterial species, we used a combination of metabolic profiling
and genome mining. Homology searches identified biosyn-

several other Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria
(Figures 5A and S15).27 For phylogenetic analysis, we used
the ketosynthase domain sequences from the BurA homo-
logues in these gene clusters. Those from malleicyprol-

these trigonic acid operons are functional in these species, we

■DISCUSSION
By a combination of genome mining, metabolic profiling,
isotope labeling, and functional analyses, we have gained first
insight into the biosynthesis of cyclopropanol-containing
amino acids and discovered its peculiar relation to a compound
that plays an eminent role in global sulfur cycling. We have
demonstrated that the cyclopropanol ring of cleonine in
valgamicin C is derived from DMSP. The use of DMSP
coupled with the presence of the trigonic acid operon in the val
gene cluster, the detection of gonyol and gonydiol in
Amycolatopsis sp. ML1-hF4, and trigonic acid itself in S.
mobaraensis, is strong evidence that trigonic acid is the
cyclopropanol-containing precursor to cleonine (Figure 5F).
The most direct route from trigonic acid to cleonine is the
oxidation of the α-hydroxyl moiety to a ketone, followed by
transamination to form cleonine. Such an oxidation/trans-
amination mechanism is also proposed for the biosynthesis of
the polyketide-derived amino acid 2-aminocaprylic36 and the
fatty acid-derived amino acid 2-aminododecanoic acid.37
In addition to cleonine, L-methionine (the precursor to
DMSP) is known to be the source of other amino acids that
contain strained rings, such as 1-aminocyclopropane-1-
carboxylic acid11 and azetidine 2-carboxylic acid.38,39 However,
in each of these cases, L-methionine is first converted into S-
adenosyl methionine, creating an electrophilic carbon atom
that drives ring formation.
In the case of trigonic acid biosynthesis, however, the
sulfonium group of DMSP performs an analogous role to the
sulfur of S-adenosyl methionine, acting as a leaving group
during cyclopropanol formation.27 Cleonine is therefore the
first ring-containing amino acid derived from L-methionine that
does not have an S-adenosyl methionine intermediate.
Cleonine is further unique in being the first described amino
acid to require a PKS-NRPS hybrid enzyme (a BurA-like
enzyme) in its biosynthesis.
In contrast to the bur and val biosynthetic gene clusters, the
trigonic acid operon in S. mobararensis is separate from the
bleomycin A2 biosynthetic gene cluster. The implications of
such a separation remain to be seen but could indicate that
numerous trigonic acid/cleonine-containing products are
produced by S. mobaraensis (and in other species that contain
the trigonic acid operon separate from other biosynthesis
genes).
Over 109 tonnes of DMSP are produced by phytoplankton
and microalgae in the oceans annually,40 but its role in
secondary metabolism is only now coming to light. First with
malleicyprol biosynthesis and now here with valgamicin C and

produced by algae.41,42 Our findings here expand upon this

Prior to this study, the only cyclopropanol-synthase to be
functionally characterized in vitro was BurG from the
malleicyprol biosynthesis pathway.27 Here, we have performed
the first in vitro functional characterization of a BurG
homologue from a nonmalleicyprol-producing species
(MobG), confirming its ability to produce trigonic acid from
gonydiol. The trigonic acid operon is therefore a reliable
genetic handle for identifying natural products that contain a
cyclopropanol ring. The final products of the other trigonic
acid operon-containing gene clusters await discovery, but their
identification can be assisted by our discovery that
supplementing culture medium with DMSP specifically boosts
cyclopropanol ring production (Figure 4C).
■CONCLUSIONS
We have discovered that the cyclopropanol ring of the amino
acid cleonine is derived from DMSP, a sulfonium acid
primarily known for its role in global sulfur cycling.28 The
cleonine cyclopropanol ring and the cyclopropanol warhead of
the malleicyprols therefore have a common biosynthetic origin,
revealing a general mechanism in nature for cyclopropanol
biosynthesis across diverse natural product scaffolds. Our
findings have important implications for the biosynthesis,
engineering, and discovery of cyclopropyl alcohol-containing
natural products. Given the well-characterized reactivity of
cyclopropanol rings,1,13 cleonine could be valuable as a highly
“portable” cyclopropanol ring to introduce, via mutasynthesis
or synthetic biology approaches, into tailor-made non-
ribosomal peptides. Toward this goal, our study also provides
the groundwork for identifying the complete cleonine
biosynthesis pathway, paving the way to produce this unusual
amino acid in heterologous hosts. Finally, by fermenting other
bacteria species that contain the trigonic acid operon, we
demonstrated that the key DMSP- and gonyol-synthesizing
enzymes are all active. Taken together, our findings also
provide functional validation for the use of the trigonic acid
operon as a genetic handle for identifying more producers of
enigmatic, diverse, and therapeutically promising cyclopropyl
alcohol-containing natural products.
