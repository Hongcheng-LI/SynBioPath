Supporting Information
ABSTRACT: Three unique linear oligomeric depsipeptides,
designated as cavomycins A−C (1−3), were identified from
Streptomyces cavourensis, a gut bacterium associated with the
annelid Paraleonnates uschakovi. The structures of these depsipep-
tides were determined through a combination of spectroscopic
methods and chemical derivatization techniques, including
methanolysis, the modified Mosher’s method, advanced Marfey’s
methods, and phenylglycine methyl ester derivatization. The
unique dipeptidyl residue arrangements in compounds 1−3
indicate that they are not degradation products of valinomycin.
Compound 2 and its methylation derivative 2a exhibited
antiproliferative activity against PANC-1 pancreatic cancer cells
with IC50 values of 1.2 and 1.7 μM, respectively.
A
nimal hosts and their associated microbiomes have
coevolved in response to natural pressures over an
extended evolutionary period. Hosts offer crucial niches and
nutrients to their microbiomes, which in turn enhance the
immunity, growth, and productivity of the hosts. The
interactions between hosts and their microbiomes are complex
and dynamic and are influenced by a variety of factors.1,2
Studies of microbiomes in marine animals have become
increasingly prominent areas in marine science.3 Recent
advances in bioinformatics have enabled a deeper exploration
of the relationships between hosts and complex, multimember
microbiomes, beyond the focus on single host−symbiont
systems. Furthermore, investigating host−microbiome inter-
actions provides valuable insights into how marine animals
adapt to changing oceanic environments, particularly those
changes driven by climate-related and anthropogenic factors.4
Research on natural products, including investigations into
biological functions and chemical structure characterization, is
vital to understanding host−microbiome interactions. Marine
microbiomes are increasingly recognized as promising sources
for bioprospecting natural products that elucidate chemical
interactions between hosts and their microbiomes. In our
ongoing research on the microbiomes of marine ecosystems,
several bacterial strains were isolated from the gut of the
annelid Paraleonnates uschakovi. Among these, four bacterial
extracts demonstrated antiproliferative activity in MTT
bioassays. Subsequently, three unique linear depsipeptides,
named cavomycins A, B, and C, were isolated from the active
extract of the annelid-associated Streptomyces cavourensis
HK58. Determining the structures of these compounds was
challenging due to resonance overlap in NMR spectra and
ambiguous MS/MS daughter ion peaks. Nevertheless, their
structures were successfully elucidated through a combination
of spectroscopic analysis and chemical derivatization techni-
ques, including methanolysis, the modified Mosher’s method,
phenyl glycine methyl ester (PGME) derivatization, and the
advanced Marfey’s method. Although cyclic depsipeptide
oligomers have been extensively studied, this study represents
the first instance of determining the connection order and
absolute configuration of oligomeric depsipeptides using
chemical derivatization methods. Here, we present the
structural elucidation, biological activity, and discussion of
the biosynthetic aspects of these compounds.

■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Cavomycin A (1) was isolated as a colorless oil. Its molecular
formula was determined to be C52H88N6O19 by high-resolution
electrospray ionization (HR-ESI) mass and NMR data
(Figures 1 and S1−S6). Analysis of the
1H NMR and
multiplicity-edited HSQC data revealed six distinct exchange-
able proton resonances at δ 8.35, 8.30, 8.29, 8.25, 7.98, and
7.62 and four methyl doublets at δ 1.33, 1.30, 1.28, and 1.22.
In addition, the 1H NMR spectrum exhibited higher-frequency
methine proton resonances for 12 protons, overlapping
methine resonances centered at δ 2.09, and overlapping
methyl signals centered at δ 0.89. The 13C NMR spectrum of
compound 1 indicated the presence of 12 carbonyl carbons (δ
174.7−168.8), six oxygenated sp3 carbons (δ 77.8−67.1), six
nitrogen-bound sp3 carbons (δ 57.6−56.5), eight aliphatic
methine carbons (δ 30.5−29.9), and 20 methyl groups (δ
21.2−17.3). The linear peptide nature of the compound was
inferred from the 12 degrees of unsaturation, accounting for 12
carbonyl groups.
COSY and HMBC data facilitated the assignment of four
lactate (Lac), two α-hydroxyisovalerate (α-Hiv), and six valine
(Val) moieties in compound 1. The ROESY correlations
between the oxygenated methine protons and their adjacent
amide protons (δ 5.09 to 8.35, δ 5.06 to 8.30, δ 5.03 to 7.98, δ
4.78 to 8.25, δ 4.77 to 8.29, δ 4.03 to 7.62) confirmed the
connections between the lactate/α-hydroxyisovalerate and
valine moieties. This led to the deduction of four Lac-Val
and two α-Hiv-Val dipeptidol residues (Figure 1). However,
the high degree of similarity in the chemical shifts of the
carbonyl carbons and high-frequency methine proton signals
posed a challenge in establishing further connectivity among
the six dipeptidyl residues. Only the amide carbonyl carbons at
δ 174.7 of the N-terminal Lac-Val and at δ 172.9 of the C-
terminal Lac-Val residue were readily assigned via 2D NMR
correlations (Figure 1), indicating a reduced shielding effect at
the terminal moieties of the peptide chain. The assignment of
the C-terminal Lac-Val dipeptidyl residue was further
substantiated by the HMBC and ROESY correlations (Figure
1) of the methyl ester derivative of compound 1 (compound
1a), which was synthesized by treating compound 1 with
TMS-diazomethane.
To ascertain the sequence of the remaining dipeptidyl
residues in cavomycin A, the derivatization techniques,
including the modified Mosher’s method and PGME
derivatization,5−7 and high-resolution tandem mass spectrom-
etry (HR-MS/MS) analyses were employed. To avoid the
formation of di-Mosher esters, the methyl ester of compound 1
(compound 1a) was reacted with the Mosher reagents, R-(−)-
and S-(+)-α-methoxy-α-(trifluoromethyl) phenylacetyl
(MTPA) chloride. The S configuration of the lactate unit in
the N-terminal Lac-Val dipeptidyl residue was confirmed by
the 1H NMR analysis of Mosher derivatives. This analysis
revealed negative ΔδS−R values for the terminal methyl protons
and positive ΔδS−R values for all of the other protons (Figure
2). Additionally, positive ΔδS−R values were observed for
another Lac-Val residue, indicating that the N-terminal Lac-Val
dipeptidyl residue is linked to a second Lac-Val residue,
forming an N-terminal Lac-Val-Lac-Val tetrapeptide substruc-
ture. The connection of the C-terminus of Lac-Val to α-Hiv-
Val was determined by using the PGME method. The 1H
NMR data of the resulting PGME amide derivatives showed
positive ΔδS−R values for the isopropyl group of the C-terminal
valine and negative values for the other protons in the C-
terminal Lac-Val dipeptidyl residue and the protons of an α-
Hiv-Val dipeptidyl residue. Consequently, the existence of a C-
terminal α-Hiv-Val-Lac-Val tetrapeptide substructure, termi-
nating with L-Val, was deduced.
The connection order of the remaining two dipeptidyl
residues, α-Hiv-Val and Lac-Val, was determined in a
subsequent step. Although low-resolution MS/MS (LR-MS/
MS) was unable to resolve the connection order owing to their
structural similarity and identical m/z values (28 amu),
equivalent to a carbonyl group compared with the m/z
difference between the two dipeptidyl residues, high-resolution
MS/MS (HR-MS/MS) successfully clarified the undetermined
connection order based on the inference made from the
previous chemical manipulations. As illustrated in Figures
S87−S89, cleavage of the C−C(O), ester, and amide bonds
from the N-terminus produced fragment ions in descending
order of intensity. The identification of two sets of prominent
fragment ions (m/z 514.3116, 542.3084, and 443.2372 and m/
z 685.4007, 713.4114, and 614.3314) supported the presence
of a middle α-Hiv-Val-Lac-Val tetrapeptide substructure, with
its α-Hiv unit linked to the N-terminal Lac-Val-Lac-Val
tetrapeptide (Figure 3).
The absolute configuration of each stereogenic center in the
dipeptidyl residues was elucidated using the modified Mosher’s
and advanced Marfey’s methods following methanolysis of 1a.

Methyl ester 1a, rather than underivatized compound 1, was
employed for methanolysis to generate methyl esters of all
dipeptidyl residues, including the C-terminal dipeptidyl
residue. To determine the absolute configuration of the chiral
secondary alcohols, the methanolysis products, Lac-Val methyl
ester (M1) and α-Hiv-Val methyl ester (M2), were derivatized
with MTPA chlorides. The ΔδS−R value distribution pattern of
the resulting MTPA esters indicated an S configuration for the
Lac unit in M1 and an R configuration for the α-Hiv unit in
M2 (Scheme 1). To ascertain the absolute configuration of the
Val units, the MTPA esters of M1 and M2 were hydrolyzed
and the resulting products were derivatized with L- and D-1-
fluoro-2,4-dinitrophenyl-5-leucinamides (FDLA). LC/MS
analysis of the FDLA adducts’ elution order revealed the
presence of L-Val (L→D) in M1 and D-Val (D→L) in M2
(Tables S1 and S2).
Cavomycin B (2) displayed a protonated molecular ion at
m/z 1129.6482 in the HR-ESIMS spectrum, indicative of a
molecular formula of C54H92N6O19. The analysis of the 2D
NMR data (Figures S7−S12) revealed the presence of three
Lac-Val and three α-Hiv-Val dipeptidyl residues. The
characterization of the connection order and absolute
configuration of the six dipeptidyl residues was conducted in
a manner similar to that of cavomycin A (1).
Cavomycin C (3) gave a protonated molecular ion at m/z
930.5297 in the HR-ESIMS spectrum, appropriate for a
molecular formula of C44H75N5O16. Extensive analysis of the
2D (Figures S81−S84) NMR data indicated the presence of
three Lac-Val and two α-Hiv-Val dipeptidyl residues. The
subsequent characterization of the connection order was
completed by analysis of the HR-MS/MS data and comparison
of the spectroscopic data with those of 1 and 2.
In the MTT cell viability assay against PANC-1 pancreatic
cancer cells, compound 2 and its derivative 2a exhibited
antiproliferative activity with half-maximal inhibitory concen-
tration (IC50) values of 1.2 and 1.7 μM, respectively. In
contrast, compounds 1 and 3 exhibited either 10-fold weaker
activity or no activity. Moreover, we subsequently detected
compounds 1−3 from the endophytic Streptomyces sp. HBQ95,
which is associated with the medicinal plant Cinnamomum
cassia. We propose that these toxic compounds, when naturally
produced, might play a defensive role in the survival strategies
of their respective hosts.
Although compounds 1−3 comprised the same D-α-Hiv, D-
Val,
L-Lac, and
L-Val units as valinomycin, and the core
nonribosomal peptide synthetase (NRPS) biosynthetic gene
cluster (BGC) sequences, cvm1 and cvm2, of the current strain
are 97% similar to those (vlm1 and vlm2) in the valinomycin-
producing Streptomyces tsusimaensis ATCC15141 (Figure S92),
LC/MS analysis did not reveal any traces of valinomycin in the
extract. This finding raises questions about the absence of
valinomycin in the extract.
A recent biosynthetic study demonstrated that the wild-type
valinomycin thioesterase domain (Vlm TEwt) oligomerized D-
hiv-D-val-L-lac-L-val-SNAC (N-acetyl cysteamine thioesters8)
into a linear dodecadepsipeptide and then macrocyclized this
dodecadepsipeptide to produce valinomycin.9 Another study
found that only D-Hic-D-Ala-L-Hiv-L-Val-SNAC, not L-Hiv-L-
Val-D-Hic-D-Ala-SNAC, could be oligomerized and further
macrocyclized by the cereulide thioesterase domain (Ces
TE).10 Intriguingly, Ces TE was able to catalyze the formation
of dimer and trimer intermediates from the tetradepsipeptide
intermediate of valinomycin,
D-hiv−D-val-L-lac-L-val-SNAC,
and even produce valinomycin in trace amounts.10
Considering the structural resemblance between valinomy-
cin and cereulide, as well as the similarities in their biosynthetic
gene clusters,10,11 we temporally propose that both Vlm TE
and Ces TE may have stricter steric requirements for substrate
recognition. This could lead to a preference for
D-D-L-L
sequences over L-L-D-D sequences for further macrocyclization.
This hypothesis may be supported by previous findings where
only the noncanonical linear oligomers (L-Hiv-L-Val-D-Hic-D-
Ala)2 and (L-Hiv-L-Val-D-Hic-D-Ala)3 were isolated, presum-
ably because canonical linear oligomers were used up in
cyclization processes. Accordingly, we propose that the current
strain, S. cavourensis HK58, may be unable to produce
valinomycin due to the lack of suitable substrates containing
the required D-D-L-L tetrapeptide intermediates.
Compounds 1−3 display a distinct constitutional arrange-
ment of dipeptidyl residues (D-Hiv-D-Ala and
L-Lac-L-Val)
diverging from those typically suggested as intermediate
depsipeptides in valinomycin biosynthesis. Specifically, com-
pound 1 starts with a sequence of two L-Lac-L-Val residues, in
contrast with the expected alternating pattern of D-Hiv-D-Ala
and L-Lac-L-Val. This distinctive structural feature may suggest
that these linear depsipeptides are not degradation products of
valinomycin or its intermediates. Compounds 1−3 may be
produced through an alternative biosynthetic pathway, differ-
ent from those hypothesized for valinomycin. However,
another possibility cannot be ruled out that the oligomeriza-
tion by TE domains just randomly occurred between the D-D-L-
L tetrapeptide and L-L dipeptide units.
■EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
General Experimental Procedures. Optical rotations were
recorded in methanol using a 1.0 cm cell with a JASCO P-200
polarimeter (JASCO, Tokyo, Japan). UV spectra were obtained using
a Chirascan Plus Applied Photophysics spectrophotometer (Applied
Photophysics Ltd. Surrey, UK). Infrared (IR) spectra were acquired
using a Thermo Nicolet iS10 spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific
Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker
Ascend 700 spectrometer (Bruker, Billerica, MA, USA) using
dimethyl sulfoxide-d6 or acetone-d6 as the solvent. Chemical shifts
are reported with reference to the respective solvent peaks (δH 2.50
and δC 39.52 for DMSO-d6, δH 2.05 and δC 29.84 for acetone-d6).
Low-resolution electrospray ionization (ESI) mass spectra were
recorded on an Agilent Technologies 6120 quadrupole mass
spectrometer (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) coupled with an
Agilent Technologies 1260 Series HPLC system. High resolution
(HR)-MS data were collected using a JEOL JMS-700 double-focusing
instrument. HPLC was performed using a Waters HPLC (Waters
Corp., Milford, MA, USA) equipped with a Waters 2998 photodiode
array detector and a Waters 1525 binary pump. HPLC grade solvents
(Burdick & Jackson) were used for the HPLC analysis. NMR solvents
were purchased from Cambridge Isotope Laboratories, Inc.
Collection of the Specimens. Specimens of the annelids were
collected from the mudflat near Jung-gu, Incheon, Republic of Korea,
in July 2018. The specimens were more massive, 560 mm long, than
most other marine annelids. The body is dark green, with a lighter
color in the abdomen. The annelid was identified as Paraleonnates
uschakovi on the basis of its external morphological characteristics.
Bacterial Isolation. Two Paraleonnates uschakovi specimens were
washed with EtOH to prevent the isolation of microbes from their
exoskeletons and air-dried on a clean bench to remove the residual
EtOH. The intestinal tract was separated by a sterilized razor blade
and vortexed in 1 mL of sterilized distilled H2O. Suspension aliquots
of 100 μL were dropped and spread on various isolation agar media.
All plates were incubated at 27 °C for 2 weeks or longer. The

was carried out under the same conditions as seed cultivation for 4
days. The resulting culture medium of the strain was extracted using
EtOAc in a 6 L separation funnel. To isolate compounds 1−3, 100 L
was cultured, and 5.9 g of extract was obtained.

Cavomycins were detected in the 20% MeOH fraction. Further

Determination of the Absolute Configurations of the
Valine Units in M1 and M2 by Advanced Marfey’s Method.
The S- and R-MTPA esters of M1 and M2 were combined and
hydrolyzed in 6 N HCl (0.5 mL) at 115 °C for 1 h. The reaction
mixture was dried under vacuum, and the residual acid was removed
thrice by resuspending the dry material in 500 μL of distilled water.
The hydrolysate was divided into two portions, and each portion was
mixed with 1 N NaHCO3 (200 μL) and 100 μL of L- or D-FDLA (10
mg/mL in acetone). The reaction mixtures were maintained at 80 °C
for 3 min and then neutralized with 100 μL of 2 N HCl and dried
under a vacuum. The reaction products were dissolved in aqueous
acetonitrile (1:1) and analyzed by LC/MS using an analytical column
(Luna, 100 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm). The solvent system was delivered at a
rate of 0.8 mL/min, and UV detection was set at 340 nm. A gradient
solvent system of 20−70% CH3CN−H2O containing 0.1% formic
acid over 50 min was used as the mobile phase. In the selected-ion
monitoring chromatogram of the resulting derivatized hydrolysates,
the L- and D-FDLA adducts of M1 (m/z 412) were eluted at 29.95
and 37.67 min, respectively, and the L- and D-FDLA adducts of M2
(m/z 412) were eluted at 37.67 and 29.95 min, respectively.
