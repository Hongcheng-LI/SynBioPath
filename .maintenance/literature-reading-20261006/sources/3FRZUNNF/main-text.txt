ABSTRACT: Alkaloids with a phenylhydrazone architecture are rarely found
in nature. Four unusual phenylhydrazone alkaloids named talarohydrazones
A−D (1−4) were isolated from the deep-sea cold seep derived fungus
Talaromyces amestolkiae HDN21-0307 using the one strain−many compounds
(OSMAC) approach and MS/MS-based molecular networking (MN)
combined with network annotation propagation (NAP) and the unsupervised
substructure annotation method MS2LDA. Their structures were elucidated by
spectroscopic data analysis, single-crystal X-ray diffraction, and quantum
chemical calculations. Talarohydrazone A (1) possessed an unusual skeleton
combining 2,4-pyridinedione and phenylhydrazone. Talarohydrazone B (2)
represents the first natural phenylhydrazone-bearing azadophilone. Bioactivity
evaluation revealed that compound 1 exhibited cytotoxic activity against NCI-
H446 cells with an IC50 value of 4.1 μM. In addition, compound 1 displayed
weak antibacterial activity toward Staphylococcus aureus with an MIC value of 32 μg/mL.
Talaromyces, a genus belonging to the family Trichocomaceae,
produced a wide range of secondary metabolites, including
esters, polyketones, anthraquinones, terpenoids, meroterpe-
noids, steroids, and alkaloids.1,2 Most of these show significant
biological activities, such as antibacterial, antifungal, antitumor,
anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant activities.1,2 Talaromyces
amestolkiae has remained relatively unexplored for secondary
metabolites, and the secondary metabolites that have been
reported so far are relatively simple, including mostly
coumarins and terpenes (Figure S1).3−11 In our ongoing
investigations of bioactive compounds from marine-derived
fungi,12−14 T. amestolkiae HDN21-0307, a fungal strain isolated
from deep-sea cold seep sediments in the South China Sea, was
selected for further research. Employing the one strain−many
compounds (OSMAC) strategy for cultivating T. amestolkiae
HDN21-0307 led to unexpected findings, as it produced
compounds with significantly different UV absorption profiles
(series UV absorption around 220, 270, 480 nm/225, 280, 400,
540 nm, Figures S2 and S3), compared to the known
compounds of T. amestolkiae.3−11
MS/MS-based molecular networking (MN) on the Global
Natural Product Social (GNPS) platform (https://gnps.ucsd.
edu) offers a valuable approach to organize and analyze MS/
MS data based on chemical similarity.15 This method is
recognized as an efficient tool for expediting the dereplication
of natural products and the structure elucidation of low-
abundant natural products.16 However, only a limited number
of nodes in MN can be annotated due to the limited number of
MS/MS spectra in available reference GNPS spectral
libraries.17 To analyze nodes not annotated by GNPS,
researchers have developed other available annotation tools,
such as network annotation propagation (NAP)18 and the
unsupervised substructure annotation method (MS2LDA).19
NAP is an online MN analysis tool that can use a network
consensus scoring algorithm to account for structural similarity
between in silico candidates without the need for matching
experimental libraries.18 MS2LDA is a method developed for
unsupervised substructure discovery based on latent Dirichlet
allocation (LDA) that can find relevant substructures based on
the co-occurrence of mass fragments and neutral losses (called
Mass2Motifs) and group the molecules accordingly.19,20
MAGMa (Mass2Motif Graphical Model Annotation) serves
as a computational approach used to annotate Mass2Motif
features; its annotation information can be stored in MS2LDA
to enrich the metabolite identification process.20 Hence,
through the integrated utilization of GNPS, NAP, and
MS2LDA tools, researchers can access more comprehensive
insight into metabolites, encompassing structural novelty,
potential structural types, and shared structural fragments
along with their potential types.

To investigate the structural novelty and possible structural
types of new metabolites with distinct UV absorptions, we
performed an in-depth analysis of the extract from T.
amestolkiae HDN21-0307 by utilizing MN in conjunction
with NAP and MS2LDA. The results suggested that target
compounds were likely to be new alkaloids sharing the same
nitrogen-containing substructure. Subsequent HPLC-directed
isolation yielded four unusual phenylhydrazone alkaloids,
named talarohydrazones A−D (1−4). Talarohydrazone A
(1) possessed an unusual skeleton combining 2,4-pyridine-
dione and phenylhydrazone, while talarohydrazone B (2)
represented the first natural phenylhydrazone-bearing azado-
philone. Bioactivity analysis of these compounds showed that
compound 1 displayed cytotoxic activity against NCI-H446
cells with an IC50 value of 4.1 μM and weak antibacterial
activity toward Staphylococcus aureus with an MIC value of 32
μg/mL. Herein, details of the isolation, structure elucidation,
cytotoxicity, and antibacterial evaluation of these compounds
are presented.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
The metabolites of T. amestolkiae previously reported by other
research groups primarily consist of coumarins and terpenes,
predominantly isolated from solid-substrate media (Figure
S1).3−11 Hence, an OSMAC strategy using six different liquid
media under either static or shaking conditions was employed
to further explore the metabolic potential of T. amestolkiae
(Figure S2). HPLC and LC-MS chromatograms of the extract
from strain HDN21-0307 grown in Fungus 2# liquid media
under shaking conditions showed four major peaks with
distinctive UV absorptions (λmax ∼220, 270, 480 nm/225, 280,
400, 540 nm), and their molecular weights were 351, 564, 310,
and 326 Da, respectively (Figure S3). It is interesting to note
that none of the previously reported natural products from T.
amestolkiae displayed this distinct UV profile.3−11 This result
suggests that the Fungus 2# liquid media induced the
production of new metabolites in T. amestolkiae.
In order to prevent redundant isolation of known
compounds, GNPS MN was utilized to analyze these four
target metabolites. The MS/MS spectra of the fractions (Fr.
A−Fr. E) obtained from silica gel column chromatography of
the extract were uploaded to the GNPS platform. The GNPS
results showed a single molecular family in the MN containing
the spectral nodes of these four compounds at m/z 352.129/
565.145/327.097/311.102 ([M + H]+) (Figure 1). Interest-
ingly, there were no spectral library matches to nodes in this
molecular family (Figure S5), suggesting that these four
compounds may be new compounds.
To further unveil the structural types of these four
compounds, NAP and MS2LDA were utilized to analyze
spectral nodes in the molecular family. The NAP results
showed that the nodes of compounds 1, 3, and 4 were
annotated as alkaloids (highlighted in green), while the node of
compound 2 was connected to nodes annotated as alkaloids,
indicating that compounds 1−4 may be new alkaloids (Figure
1 and Figure S6). The MS2LDA results showed that 17
spectral nodes (including the nodes of compounds 1−4) in the
molecular family shared the same Mass2Motif (motif_538)
(highlighted in purple or orange) (Figure 1), indicating the
presence of the same mass fragment or neutral loss. Motif_538
did not match any MotifDB (a data set for storing annotated
Mass2Motifs with their MS/MS features)20 or previous
MS2LDA experiments, suggesting that the compounds
corresponding to these 17 spectral nodes sharing motif_538
may contain an identical new substructure. Additionally,
fragment 139.0625 (Figures S7 and S8), possibly a nitrogen-
containing architecture according to MAGMa substructure
annotation (Figure S7), was commonly present in the nodes
that shared motif_538 in the molecular family (Figure 1).
Therefore, these four compounds were likely to be new
alkaloids with the same nitrogen-containing substructure.
To expedite the structural characterization of compounds
1−4, we observed the proportion of the clustered MS/MS
spectra comprising each node arising from each chromato-
graphic fraction. These proportions are depicted as pie charts
in Figure S5. The analysis revealed that Fr. C predominated in
the nodes for these four compounds (Figure S5). Therefore,
four new phenylhydrazone alkaloids (1−4) were obtained
from Fr. C by HPLC tracking isolation. Ultimately, the
characteristic fragment_139.09 was determined to be the
cation of 4-amino-3-methoxyphenol, based on the mass
spectrometry fragmentation pathways of hydrazones,21,22 as
observed in the ESI-MS/MS (positive ion mode) spectra of
compounds 1−4 (Figure S9).
Talarohydrazone A (1) was isolated as an orange-red,
amorphous powder. The molecular formula of 1 was
determined to be C19H17N3O4 from the positive ion peak at
m/z 352.1286 [M + H]+ (calcd for C19H18N3O4, 352.1292) in
the HRESIMS spectrum, indicating 13 degrees of unsaturation.
Spectroscopic analysis of the
1H NMR data of 1 showed
signals for nine olefinic protons, two methylene protons, and
one methoxy group. Analysis of the 13C NMR and HSQC
spectra (Table 1) indicated two carbonyl carbons (δC 178.9,
161.5), 15 olefinic carbons, including six non-hydrogenated
carbons, one methylene (δC 38.3), and one oxymethyl (δC
56.1). The aforementioned information accounted for 10 out
of the 13 degrees of unsaturation, indicating that 1 was a
tricyclic compound. Additionally, a para-NH-substituted m-
methoxyphenol (A-ring) was determined through character-
istic signals of H-18 (δH 6.57, d, J = 2.4 Hz), H-20 (δH 6.53,
dd, J = 8.8, 2.3 Hz), and H-21 (δH 7.59, d, J = 8.8 Hz) and the
HMBC correlations from H-21 to C-17/C-19, from H-18 to
C-16/C-17/C-19/C-20, and from NH-15 (δH 17.09, s) to C-
16/C-21. The HMBC correlations from H3-22 (δH 3.88, s) to
C-17 (δC 150.9) and from 19-OH (δH 10.05, s) to C-18/C-19
(δC 158.8)/C-20 further indicated that the oxymethyl group
was connected to C-17 and the hydroxy group was located at
C-19. Another monosubstituted benzene ring (C-ring) can be
identified based on typical aromatic signals of H-9/13 (δH
7.34, 2H), H-10/12 (δH 7.35, 2H), and H-11 (δH 7.27, 1H)
and the HMBC correlations from H-11 to C-9/C-13 and from
H-10/H-12 to C-8 (δC 136.7). The key HMBC correlations
from H2-7 (δH 3.70, s) to C-5/C-6/C-8/C-9/C-13 indicated
that the double bond Δ5 in fragment B was directly connected
to C-7, thereby connecting to the C-ring. The NH-1 was
determined to be connected to C-6 based on its HMBC
correlations from NH-1 (δH 11.08) to C-5 (δC 104.3)/C-7.

There remained one nitrogen atom, one amide carbonyl (δC

hydrogenated carbon (δC 126.0) whose positions in the
structure had not been determined. Considering the HMBC

substituted with hydrazine was proposed. Finally, the structure
of 1, including the configuration of double bond Δ3,14, was
confirmed by single-crystal X-ray diffraction (Figure 3, CCDC:
2323822, Cu Kα). According to the MS2LDA analysis, the
fragment_139.0625 was commonly present in the nodes
(including 1−4) that shared motif_538. Further analysis of
mass spectrometry fragmentation pathways of 1 indicated that
the feature fragment_139.0625 corresponds to the cationic
peak of 4-amino-3-methoxyphenol (Figure S9). Therefore,
compounds 1−4 are likely to share the same para-NH-
substituted m-methoxyphenol substructure. In addition, the
pyridone moiety in natural products is mostly 2-pyridone or 4-
hydroxy-2-pyridone.23,24 Compound 1 contains a 2,4-pyridi-
nedione moiety rarely found in nature. Furthermore,
compound 1 represents the first natural product featuring a
phenylhydrazone−pyridinedione hybrid skeleton.
Talarohydrazone B (2) was obtained as an orange-red,
amorphous powder. The molecular formula, C28H24N2O11, was
deduced from its HRESIMS data (m/z 565.1452 [M + H]+,
calcd for 565.1453), indicating 18 degrees of unsaturation. The
13C NMR and HSQC spectra (Table 2) indicated four
carbonyl carbons (δC 190.3, 188.7, 168.1, 166.8), 12 non-
hydrogenated carbons, eight methines, one methylene (δC
63.4), and three methyls (δC 56.1, 23.2, 22.9). Similar to the
structural analysis of compound 1, a para-NH-substituted m-
methoxyphenol unit in 2 was determined according to the
characteristic MS/MS fragment_139.09 and NMR data,
especially the HMBC signals from NH-10 (δH 14.46, s) to
C-11/C-12/C-16 (Table 2 and Figure 2). The remaining
NMR data of 2 exhibited high similarity to that of

replacement of the two olefin carbons in (−)-mitorubrinic acid
by a non-hydrogenated carbon (δC 122.7, C-4) and an oxidized
methylene (δC 63.4, C-8) in 2. These differences indicated the
presence of a rearranged double bond Δ4a,8a of the
azadophilone unit in 2. This was confirmed by the HMBC

Talarohydrazones C (3) and D (4) were purified as deep-
purple amorphous powders with the molecular formulas
C17H14N2O4 and C17H14N2O5 based on the HRESIMS data,
respectively. The presence of characteristic fragment_139.09
and similar NMR data suggested that 3 and 4 contained the
same para-NH-substituted m-methoxyphenol fragment as 1
and 2 (Table 3 and Figure S9). The remaining 1D NMR
spectra of 3 and 4 (Table 3) indicated similar skeletons to that
for 1,2-naphthoquinone.26
The difference between 3 and 1,2-naphthoquinone lies in
the replacement of the olefinic proton (H-8) of 3 with a
hydroxy group (8-OH) supported by the HMBC correlations
from 8-OH (δH = 13.00) to C-7/C-8 (δC = 162.6)/C-8a.
Considering the molecular formula, there was only one
nitrogen atom that remained unexplained. This nitrogen
atom was determined to be connected to N-10 in the form
of a double bond Δ2,9 by the key HMBC correlation from NH-
10 (δH 16.01, s) to C-2 and the chemical shift of C-2 (δC
131.2), as shown in Figure 2.
The only difference between 4 and 3 was that the olefinic
proton (H-6) of 4 was replaced by a hydroxy group (6-OH),
based on the HMBC correlations from 6-OH (δH 10.63) to C-
5/C-6 (δC 164.2)/C-7. Thus, the planar structures of
compounds 3 and 4 were established.

The configuration of the double bond Δ2,9 could not be
determined due to the absence of NOESY correlations. Time-
dependent density-functional theory (TDDFT) was employed
to calculate NMR data of the two possible configurations E-
3a/4a and Z-3b/4b for 3 and 4, respectively (Figure S11). The
improved DP4+ probability analysis indicated that Z-3b/4b
appeared consistent with the NMR experimental data with a
probability of 100%, for each (Figure 5).27
The new compounds (1−4) were evaluated for cytotoxic
activities against five human cancer cell lines using SRB
staining28 and MTT methods29 with adriamycin as a positive
control. Compound 1 showed cytotoxic activity against NCI-
H446 cells with an IC50 value of 4.1 μM (Table 4).
Compounds 1−4 were also tested for antibacterial activities
toward seven indicated strains,30 including Bacillus cereus
ATCC 4342, methicillin-resistant coagulase-negative Staph-
ylococci (MRCNS), methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus
(MRSA), Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 29213, Pseudomonas
aeruginosa ATCC 27853, Bacillus subtilis ATCC 6051, and
Acinetobacter baumannii AB34. Compound 1 exhibited
antibacterial activity against S. aureus with an MIC value of
32 μg/mL (Table 5).
In summary, phenylhydrazones are commonly used in the
field of synthesis and exhibit a variety of biological activities,
such as antibacterial, antifungal, cytotoxic, and antioxidant
activity,31,32 but phenylhydrazones derived from natural
products are very rare.33−37 By integration of OSMAC and
MN coupled to NAP and MS2LDA, four new phenyl-
hydrazone alkaloids were targeted and isolated from T.
amestolkiae HDN21-0307. In addition to the phenylhydrazone
fragment, compound 1 also possesses a 2,4-pyridinedione
skeleton that is rare in natural products.23,24 Compound 2
represents the first natural phenylhydrazone-bearing azadophi-
lone. Furthermore, compound 1 showed cytotoxic activity
against the NCI-H446 cells. This work greatly enriched the
structural library of phenylhydrazones from natural sources and
offered a valuable structural template for the development of
antitumor drugs.
■EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
General Experimental Procedures. Optical rotations were
measured on a JASCO P-1020 digital polarimeter. UV spectra were
recorded on a Hitachi 5430 spectrophotometer. The ECD spectra
were measured on a JASCO J-715 spectropolarimeter. IR spectra were
measured on a Bruker Tensor-27 spectrophotometer by using KBr
discs. NMR spectra were obtained on a Bruker AVANCE NEO 400
MHz spectrometer, an Agilent DD2-500 spectrometer, or a JEOL
JNM-ECZ600R/S1 spectrometer with tetramethylsilane (TMS) as an
internal standard. HRESIMS and ESIMS spectra were obtained by
using a Thermo Scientific LTQ Orbitrap XL mass spectrometer in
positive ionization mode. Column chromatography (CC) was
performed with silica gel (200−300 mesh, Qingdao Marine Chemical
Inc.) and Sephadex LH-20 (Amersham Biosciences). MPLC was
performed on a Bona-Agela CHEETAHTM HP100 (Beijing Agela
Technologies Co.). HPLC spectra were recorded on a Hitachi
Primaide HPLC using an ODS column (HPLC (YMC-Pack ODS-A,
10 × 250 mm, 5 μm, 3 mL/min)) (YMC Co., Ltd.). LC-MS was
performed using an Acquity UPLC H-Class coupled to an SQ
Detector 2 mass spectrometer using a BEH C18 column (1.7 μm, 2.1
× 50 mm, 1 mL/min) (Waters Corporation).
Fungal Material. T. amestolkiae HDN21-0307 (GenBank entry
OQ954837) was isolated from deep-sea cold seep sediments in the
South China Sea. The strain was deposited at the Marine Medicinal
Bioresources Center, School of Medicine and Pharmacy, Ocean
University of China.

LC-MS/MS and Molecular Networking Analysis. LC-MS/MS
analyses were performed using a UHPLC system (Ultimate 3000,
Thermo Scientific) with a C18 column (ACQUITY UPLC BEH 1.7
μm, 2.1 × 100 mm, Waters) coupled to a hybrid Quadrupole-
Orbitrap mass spectrometer (QExactive, Thermo Scientific). In
positive-ionization conditions, the mobile phase consisted of 0.1%
formic acid in H2O (A) and HPLC-grade MeOH (B). The elution
gradient conditions for the LC mobile phase were as follows, based on
times (t): t = 0−1 min, hold at 10% B; t = 1−23 min, increased
linearly to 100% B; t = 23−26 min, hold at 100% B; t = 26−27 min,
returned to initial conditions; t = 27−30 min, hold at 10% B to re-
equilibrate the column, flow rate 0.25 mL/min, injection volume 3
μL. The acquisition of H-ESI was carried out in data-dependent scan
mode, sweeping from m/z = 70 to m/z = 1000. All MS/MS data were
converted to mzXML format files by using MS Convert software.
Molecular network analysis was performed using the GNPS data
analysis workflow and the spectral clustering algorithm. The spectral
networks were imported into Cytoscape (version 3.9.0) for visual-
ization.
Parameters for the MN generation were set as follows: both
precursor mass and MS/MS fragment ion tolerance were set as 0.02
Da, minimum pairs cosine score 0.65, minimum matched fragment
ions 6, minimum cluster size 1, and network TopK 10. The spectral
library searching was performed in the default parameter with
analogues search. The MN job is accessible with the following link:
https://proteomics2.ucsd.edu/ProteoSAFe/status.jsp?task=
3c2012ecf5134ed7a394021856220d15.
In the NAP workflow, a MetFrag in silico fragment analysis was
performed by matching the candidate structures with the exact mass
filter of 5 ppm from the structural databases of GNPS, HMDB,
SUPNAT, CHEBI, DRUGBANK, and FooDB. The MN job
employing NAP annotation is accessible with the following link:
https://proteomics2.ucsd.edu/ProteoSAFe/status.jsp?task=
e3f8c46f75da453ba97d5551f46c3ed0.
In the MS2LDA unsupervised substructure annotation workflow,
the fragmentation spectra were mined for co-occurring fragments on
the MS2LDA Web application. The working parameters were set as
follows: bin width (0.005, Q-Executive Data), number of LDA
iterations (1000), minimum MS2 intensity (100), LDA free motifs
(300); overlap score threshold (0.3), probability value threshold
(0.1), TopX in node (5).
The result of MS2LDA annotation is available at the following link:
https://proteomics2.ucsd.edu/ProteoSAFe/status.jsp?task=
df290f38f2be451280992d39b0056544.
Isolation and Purification. The extract was subjected to a VLC
column using a stepped gradient elution of MeOH−CH2Cl2 (0−
100%), which resulted in the separation of the extract into five
subfractions (Fr. A−E). Fr. C was separated by MPLC (ODS) using a
stepped gradient elution of MeOH−H2O (10:90 to 100:0 at 10 mL/
min) to yield four subfractions (Fr. C1−Fr. C4). Fr. C1 was purified
by semipreparative HPLC (YMC-pack ODS, 10 × 250 mm, 3.0 mL/
min) to afford 1 (5.0 mg, tR = 21 min). Fr. C2 was purified by
semipreparative HPLC to afford 4 (1.5 mg, tR = 25 min). Fr. C3 was
purified by semipreparative HPLC to afford 2 (1.5 mg, tR = 27 min).
Fr. C4 was purified by semipreparative HPLC to afford 3 (5.0 mg, tR
= 28 min).
