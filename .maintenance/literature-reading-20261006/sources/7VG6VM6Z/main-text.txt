The first step in the biosynthesis of taxol in Pacific
yew (Taxus brevifolia) is the cyclization of the univer-
sal diterpene precursor geranylgeranyl pyrophos•
phate to This parent olefin of
the taxane diterpenoids is then elaborated to taxol and
related compounds by a complex series of reactions
involving oxidations and side-chain acylations. Cycli-
zation activity is located principally in yew stem bark
and adhering cambium. The operationally soluble cy-
clization enzyme was partially purified (—600-fold) by
combination of anion exchange, hydrophobic interac-
tion, and dye—ligand chromatography. Nondenatur-
ing, followed by denaturing, polyacrylamide gel elec•
trophoresis, in combination with gel permeation chro-
matography, allowed the identification of taxadiene
synthase as a monomeric protein of molecular weight
79,000. In general properties (divalent metal ion re-
quirement, kinetic constants, molecular weight), the
taxadiene synthase of Pacific yew is similar to the dit-
erpene cyclase abietadiene synthase involved in resin
acid biosynthesis in other gymnosperms. However, in
pH optimum and response to inhibitors, these two dit•
erpene cyclases are distinctly different. The activity
(and enzyme protein) levels of Pacific yew taxadiene
synthase are much lower than those for abietadiene
synthase of lodgepole pine stem (constitutive) or of
grand fir stem (wound-inducible) and the enzyme is
not inducible to higher levels by stem wounding or
elicitor treatment. 1995 Academic Press, Inc.

The complex diterpenoid taxol (paclitaxel), 3 first iso-
lated from the bark of Pacific yew (Taxus brevifolia)
(I), is a potent antimitotic agent that acts to arrest the
cell cycle by stabilization of microtubule dynamics (2,
3). Taxol has recently been approved for the treatment
of ovarian and breast cancer, and its chemotherapeutic
application to a range of other cancers is certain to
follow (4).
The demand for taxol greatly exceeds the supply that
can be sustained by isolation from the original source;
the Pacific yew is very slow growing and sparsely dis-
tributed, the yields of the drug are low (0.01—0.1% dry
wt of bark), and the harvest is destructive (5). Hence,
an intensive search has been mounted for alternate
means of (6). Taxol has been prepared by
total synthesis (7—9); however, the yields are too low
to permit commercial production (10). Semisynthesis
of taxol and the analog taxotere (docetaxel)3 from more
abundant taxane diterpenoids (taxoids), for example
via the conversion of cephalomannine (II) or by C-13
side-chain attachment to baccatin III (12—15) (see
Scheme I), has provided an immediate alternative
source of the drug. These approaches, however, must
still rely on biological methods for the production of
suitable advanced precursors, thus requiring the sus-
tained, large-scale growth of Taxus species or, ulti-
mately, the generation of taxol and its progenitors in
cell culture (16). An understanding of taxol biosynthe-

We have undertaken a systematic study of taxol bio-
synthesis at the enzyme level, with the goal of elucidat-
ing the slow steps of the pathway for ultimate manipu-
lation. It was recently demonstrated (17) that the first
committed step of taxol biosynthesis involves the cycli-
zation of the universal diterpenoid precursor ( 18), gera-
nylgeranyl pyrophosphate, to taxa-4(5),11(12)-diene,
which undergoes extensive oxidative elaboration and
side-chain addition to afford taxol and related metabo-
lites (Scheme I). The very low levels of taxadiene syn-
thase activity in yew stem extracts and the trace
amounts of this diterpene olefin intermediate in bark
(5—10 ggkg) (17) suggest that this cyclization step to
establish the taxane skeleton is very slow, if not rate
limiting, in taxol biosynthesis and thus an obvious tar-
get for detailed study. In this paper, we describe the
isolation and partial purification of taxadiene synthase
and report on the properties of this diterpene cyclase.
In a number of characteristics, taxadiene synthase was
shown to resemble other terpenoid cyclase enzymes
from gymnosperms and angiosperms.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Demonstration of Taxadiene Synthase Activity
Pacific yew saplings were chosen as an experimental
system because the taxoid content of immature tissue
is relatively high (35, 36), sapling stems contain a high
proportion of phloem paranchyma cells in which taxol

is thought to be produced (37—39), and saplings can be
maintained in a greenhouse to minimize the effects of
environmental variation on taxoid content (40—42). A
crude enzyme extract was prepared from quick-frozen
stem sections by methods previously developed for the
isolation of other terpenoid cyclases from gymno-
sperm stems that minimize the deleterious effects
of coextracted resins and phenolic materials (21, 22).
Incubation of the preparation with 15 gM [1-31-1]-
geranylgeranyl pyrophosphate and I mM MgC12 yielded
a single olefinic product (—3 nmol, —4% conversion of
substrate in 1 h, >99% pure by radio- and capillary
gas liquid chromatography) that was identified as taxa-

chemical yield into taxol and other advanced taxoids
in vivo (17). Periodic drenching of the saplings with the
broad spectrum fungicide Banrot had no effect on the
level of extractable cyclase activity for up to at least I
month after treatment, suggesting that the source of
the enzyme was yew tissue itself and not the rare fun-
gal species Taxomyces andreanae, an endophyte of T.
brevifolia, that has been reported to possess the ability
to produce taxol (19).
Dissection of yew stems and comparison of the corre-
sponding extracts with those Of other yew sapling tis-
sues demonstrated that the taxadiene synthase activ-
ity resided almost exclusively in the peeled bark and
adhering cambium cells, with very little activity associ-
ated with the xylem and woody stem core of
bark), needles, or roots (6 and 3%, respectively, of stem
tissue). These results are largely consistent with the
distribution of taxol in Pacific yew tissue and with prior
in vivo labeling studies using basic precursors (36—38).
Centrifugal fractionation of the bark homogenate indi-
cated that the taxadiene synthase was an operationally
soluble enzyme (negligible activity was associated with
particulate fractions), and preliminary studies showed
the activity to be absolutely dependent on the presence
of a divalent metal ion. These characteristics are
shared by other terpenoid cyclases (18, 43—45). Boiled
controls evidenced negligible activity.

Properties of Taxadiene Synthase
The pH optimum for taxadiene synthase is 8.5 in
Hepes buffer, with half-maximal velocities at pH 7.2
and 9.3. This optimum is significantly higher than
that reported for most other terpenoid cyclases (43—
45), including the abietadiene synthases of pine and
fir (pH 7.2—7.5) (21), and is closer to the optimum for
the monoterpene cyclases of gymnosperms (pH 7.8)
(32, 50). The optimum for terpenoid cyclases from
angiosperms tends to be close to, or below, neutrality
(43, 44, 51-54).
All terpenoid cyclases require for activity a divalent
metal ion (18, 43—45) that is thought to bind the an-
ionic pyrophosphate group of the substrate and to as-
sist in the ionization step of the reaction (55, 56). In
the absence of divalent metal ion, the taxadiene syn-

A plot of reaction velocity versus substrate concen-
tration (0—20 VM range at I mM MgC12) yielded a typi-
cal substrate saturation curve from which a Km value
of 3 gM for geranylgeranyl pyrophosphate was deter-
mined by computer-assisted Lineweaver—Burk plot-
ting. The Km value for geranylgeranyl pyrophosphate
with the abietadiene synthases from pine and fir is also
3 AM (21). Monoterpene, sesquiterpene, and diterpene
cyclases of higher plants characteristically possess Km
values in the low micromolar range for their respective
prenyl pyrophosphate substrates (43—45, 52).
Treatment of the partially purified preparation of
taxadiene synthase at pH 8.0 with a range Of concentra-
tions of the histidine-directed reagent diethyl pyrocar-
bonate (0.1 to 1.0 mM) resulted in significant inhibition
of activity, and from the response curve (data not
shown) an 150 value of 0.75 mM was estimated. The
presence of nearly saturating levels of substrate ( 15 3.1M
geranylgeranyl pyrophosphate plus 1 mM Mg 24) during
preincubation with diethyl pyrocarbonate reduced the
level of inhibition by roughly half, and the activity of
the impaired enzyme (inhibited to 43% with diethyl
pyrocarbonate) could be almost completely restored fol-
lowing treatment with 5 mM hydroxylamine for 22 h,
conditions that selectively decarbethoxylate histidyl
residues (58). These results are reminiscent of the in-
fluence of diethyl pyrocarbonate on catalysis by mono-
terpene cyclases from angiosperms (31) and suggest
that an essential histidine residue is located at or near
the active site of taxadiene synthase. By contrast, the
monoterpene cyclases of gymnosperms are only weakly
inhibited by this reagent, and the presence of substrate
does not protect against this inhibition (32). In the case
of the diterpene cyclase, abietadiene syn-
thase, diethyl pyrocarbonate is an effective inhibitor of
the reaction (150 0.5 mM) and the substrate affords
a significant degree of protection against inactivation
(21). However, no detectable reversal of inhibition was
observed by treatment with hydroxylamine suggesting,
in this instance, the modification of other residues such
as tyrosine, lysine, or cysteine (59).
To examine the influence of thiol-directed reagents
on taxadiene synthase activity, dithiothreitol was first
removed from the partially purified preparation; this
procedure did not adversely affect activity. Although
the taxadiene synthase was reasonably stable in the
absence of thiol protectant, the enzyme was very sensi-
tive to N-ethylmaleimide, exhibiting an 150 value of
about 2 ,uM. Inhibition by thiol-directed reagents is typ-
ical of terpenoid cyclases of plant origin (30, 34, 43—
45, 51, 57). However, coincubation of inhibitor in the
presence of substrate and divalent metal ion afforded
no detectable protection against inactivation of taxa-
diene synthase. This observation contrasts with results
obtained with monoterpene synthases from angio-
sperms (30, 33) and abietadiene synthase from grand
fir (21) in which the substrate and metal ion cofactor
afford significant protection against inactivation with
thiol alkylators, suggesting the presence of an essential
cysteine residue at or near the active site of these terpe-
noid cyclases. However, the situation with taxadiene
synthase is reminiscent of that with monoterpene cy-
clases of gymnosperms in which sensitivity to N-ethyl-
maleimide (150 —50 VM) increased slightly in the pres-
ence of substrate and divalent metal ion (34). The lack
of substrate protection in these latter cases does not
eliminate the possibility that sulfhydryls play a role in
catalysis, but rather suggests that sulfhydryl groups
distal to the active site are also important for activity
(perhaps by maintaining structural integrity) or that
binding of substrate at the active site does not reduce
accessibility of the alkylating reagent to an active-site
thiol.
Several types of evidence (review in Ref. 34) have
implicated arginyl residues in binding of phosphory-
lated substrates in a wide variety of enzymes; however,
studies with terpenoid cyclases using the arginine-di-
rected, a-dicarbonyl reagents, phenylglyoxal and 2,3-
butanedione (60—62), are few and equivocal (43). To
examine the possibility that arginyl residues might
serve to assist in binding the geranylgeranyl pyrophos-
phate—metal ion substrate complex in taxadiene syn-
thase and in subsequent catalysis, the inhibition of the
reaction by phenylglyoxal was examined. The enzyme
was relatively sensitive to the reagent (150 mM)
but the substrate—metal ion complex provided no sig-
nificant protection against inactivation. The monoter-
pene synthases from lodgepole pine appear to possess
catalytically important arginyl residues at a substrate-
protectable region of these enzymes (presumably the
active site). Conversely, in monoterpene synthases

CONCLUSIONS
These studies on taxadiene synthase, which is re-
sponsible for the construction of the taxane skeleton,
constitute the first description of any enzyme on the
taxol biosynthetic pathway. In several properties, in-
2+
cluding operational solubility, requirement for Mg
and Km for geranylgeranyl diphosphate, the taxadiene
synthase from Pacific yew resembles other diterpenoid
cyclases from a diverse assortment of higher plants,
including the cis-abienol synthase of tobacco (53), the
kaurene synthase A and B activities of the gibberellin
biosynthesis pathway (52, 63), the casbene synthase of
phytoalexin production in castor bean (57) (which the
taxadiene synthase enzyme and cyclization reaction
most closely resembles), and the abietadiene synthases
of resin acid formation in conifers (21). In molecular
weight and subunit architecture, the taxadiene syn-
thase also appears typical of this enzyme class; the
known examples are all monomers of mass 50 to 80
kDa (21, 52—54, 57, 63). On the other hand, the pH
optimum is quite high (pH 8.5), resembling only a few
terpenoid cyclases of plant or microbial origin in this
regard (57, 64). Also, the responses to a selected set
of amino acid-directed reagents make the taxadiene
synthase quite distinguishable from other catalysts of
this type, implying that this enzyme of yew stem is
somewhat different in active site organization from the
other terpenoid cyclases that have thus far been exam-
ined at this level of detail. A notable distinction of the
taxadiene synthase is the low tissue levels of activity
and, seemingly, enzyme protein observed, and the ap-
parent lack of response to stem injury or elicitor chal-
lenge. These features have thus far constrained further
purification and more detailed characterization of the
enzyme. Nevertheless, the first, and perhaps rate-de-
termining, step of taxol biosynthesis has now been de-
scribed at the enzyme level. The final steps of the path-
way involving the assembly of the C-13 N-benzoyl phe-
nylisoserine side-chain have also been deciphered (65,
66). It would be of interest to determine if the fungus
Taxomyces andreanae (19) also synthesizes taxol by
similar processes.
