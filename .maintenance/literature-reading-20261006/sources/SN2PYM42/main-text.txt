ABSTRACT: α-Ketoglutarate-dependent dioxygenases (αKGDs)
are versatile biocatalysts for oxidative reactions, yet their role in
ribosomally synthesized and post-translationally modified peptide
(RiPP) macrocyclization remains unexplored. In this study, we
uncover the widespread co-occurrence of JmjC domain-containing
αKGDs (JMJDs) with diverse RiPP subfamilies in biosynthetic
gene clusters (BGCs) through bioinformatic analysis. Specifically,
we identify a conserved group of class II lanthipeptide BGCs
encoding a two-component JMJD pair. As a representative
example, heterologous reconstitution of the cor BGC from
Streptomyces corchorusii, encoding a class II lanthipeptide
synthetase CorM and the αKGD pair CorB−CorD, yields the
metal-binding lanthipeptide corsin, which features a methyllanthionine (MeLan) ring and a unique Cys(S)-Tyr(Cβ) cross-link.
Functional studies reveal that CorM installs a MeLan ring in the precursor peptide CorA, while the CorB−CorD pair catalyzes
oxidative Cys(S)-Tyr(Cβ) cross-linking. Mechanistic investigations demonstrate that CorD, a canonical JMJD, catalyzes the Tyr7
Cβ-H abstraction and generates a para-quinone methide (pQM) intermediate, which undergoes Michael addition with Cys3. CorB,
an inactive JMJD homologue, is essential for catalysis, unveiling a unique two-component αKGD system. This work expands the
RiPP enzymatic toolkit by introducing αKGDs as radical catalysts for macrocyclization and advances our understanding of the
diverse enzymatic strategies driving RiPP structural diversification.
■INTRODUCTION
Ribosomally synthesized and post-translationally modified
peptides (RiPPs) are an emerging class of natural products
with remarkable structural diversity and broad bioactivities.1,2
Their biosynthesis is typically initiated by the production of a
ribosomal precursor peptide composed of an N-terminal leader
peptide (LP) and a C-terminal core peptide (CP). This
precursor undergoes enzymatic tailoring to install structural
modifications, followed by proteolytic cleavage of the LP to
release the mature bioactive scaffold. The formation of
macrocyclic architecture is the defining feature of many
RiPPs, which enhances stability and functional potency,
including thioether bridges in lanthipeptides3 and linaridins,4
lactone/lactam cross-links in lasso peptides5,6 and graspe-
tides.7,8
Recent studies reveal the presence of macrocyclic structures
cross-linked between inert carbon and carbon or heteroatom in
RiPPs, which are usually catalyzed by radical-generating
enzymes, including radical S-adenosylmethionine enzymes
(rSAMs), cytochrome P450 enzymes (P450s), BURP
domain-containing enzymes (BURPs) and multinuclear iron
oxygenases (MNIOs) via carbon−hydrogen activation.9−16 For
example, radical-SAM enzyme DynA generates a 5′-deoxy-
adenosyl radical (5′-dA•) to abstract a Tyr C(β)-H, forming a
para-quinone methide (pQM) intermediate that enables
His(N3)-Tyr(Cβ) cross-linking in dynobactin (Figure
1A).17,18 Cytochrome P450 enzyme BltO utilizes heme and
oxygen to oxidize Tyr and His residues in the precursor
peptide by forming a Tyr(C3)-His(N3) bond in biarylitide
(Figure 1B).19−21 Similarly, BURP domain-containing enzyme
AhyBURP catalyzes the formation of Gly and Trp radical
species in an oxygen-dependent manner, which leads to the
generation of a Gly(Cα)-Trp(N1) cross-link in legumenin
(Figure 1C).22 Multinuclear iron oxygenase BufB employs
multinuclear iron clusters to abstract a Cys C(β)-H in peptide
BufA and eventually yields 5-thio-oxazole motifs in bufferin
(Figure 1D).23 These radical-mediated mechanisms under-
score a fundamental strategy in RiPP macrocyclization and
structural diversification.
Another important family of post-translational modification
(PTM) enzymes capable of activating C−H bonds via radical
mechanisms is the α-ketoglutarate-dependent dioxygenase

(αKGD) family, which is widely involved in the biosynthesis of
natural products and PTMs of peptides and proteins.24−28
αKGDs catalyze the activation of C−H bonds through an
Fe(IV)O intermediate, generating carbon radicals for
diverse oxidative reactions.26 αKGDs are classified into
subfamilies such as IPNS/DAOCS, CAS, AlkB, TET, the
prolyl hydroxylases, the JmjC domain-containing enzymes and
the emerging HEXXH motif-containing enzymes.25,29 While
αKGDs are well-documented in hydroxylating C(β) positions
of amino acids (His, Asn, Asp and Val) and catalyzing
epoxidation in RiPP biosynthesis, no αKGD-catalyzed macro-
cyclization has been reported (Figure S1).29−35 Compared
with the rich cross-linking reactions catalyzed by rSAM
enzymes, P450 enzymes, MNIOs and BURPs, this gap
highlights an unexplored territory and a promising frontier
for expanding RiPP chemical diversity and the enzymatic
machinery for RiPP biosynthesis.
In this study, we combine bioinformatic and co-occurrence
network analysis to uncover a previously unrecognized group
of αKGDs that catalyze Cys-Tyr cross-linking in RiPPs (Figure
1E). As a representative example, we report the discovery and
structural characterization of the metal-binding lanthipeptide
corsin from Streptomyces corchorusii, which features a
methyllanthionine (MeLan) ring and a unique Cys(S)-
Tyr(Cβ) cross-link. Reconstitution of the corsin biosynthesis
establishes that the αKGD enzyme CorD catalyzes the Tyr-Cys
cross-linking, which requires the cooperation of an inactive
αKGD homologue CorB. Mechanistic studies indicate that the
Cys(S)-Tyr(Cβ) cross-linking is initiated by the CorB-CorD-
catalyzed Tyr C(β)-H abstraction, generating a para-quinone
methide (pQM) intermediate that enables Michael addition
between Cys and the activated Tyr. This work expands our
understanding of the enzymatic toolkit for RiPP cross-linking
by introducing αKGDs as RiPP cyclases alongside established
radical-generating systems like rSAMs and P450s.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Bioinformatic Analysis of RiPP BGCs Encoding
αKGDs. The α-ketoglutarate-dependent dioxygenases exhibit
remarkable sequence and functional diversity, with over 50
thousand distinct αKGDs cataloged in the UniProt database.
This vast diversity poses significant computational challenges
and time constraints for systematic genome mining. To
streamline our investigation, we prioritized the JmjC domain-
containing protein subfamily (JMJD, IPR003347), a subgroup
of αKGDs known to modify peptides and proteins.36,37 These
properties render them strong candidates for involvement in
RiPP biosynthesis. Notably, JMJDs remain largely underex-
plored in prokaryotes despite a total of 20,985 homologues in
the UniProt database (as of December 2024). To the best of
our knowledge, only two histone hydroxylases, RmYcfD and
EcYcfD, and a RiPP hydroxylase, PoyI, have been functionally
characterized.32,38,39 Given that bacteria represent the major
reservoir of RiPPs, we focused our initial analysis on bacterial
taxa to investigate the potential role of JMJDs in RiPP
biosynthetic pathways.
We utilized the EFI-Enzyme Similarity Tool (EST) to
generate a sequence similarity network (SSN) of 20,743
bacteria JMJDs in the Uniprot database with an alignment
score of 75 and UniRef 50 filtration, followed by visualization
using the Cytoscape platform (Figure 2A).40,41 A large number
of clusters were identified, in which RmYcfD and EcYcfD are
mapped together in cluster 1 (#1), and Poyl appears as a
single-membered cluster 473 (#473), leaving the rest of
clusters completely unexplored. To further identify the JMJDs
involved in RiPP biosynthesis, the resulting SSN was
subsequently employed to generate genome neighborhood
networks (GNNs) for co-occurrence analysis with class-
defining RiPP post-translational enzymes, including class I
lanthipeptide dehydratase domains (PF04738, PF14028), class
II lanthipeptide dehydratase domain (DUF4135, PF13575),
class V lanthipeptide lyase domain (PF17914), lanthipeptide
cyclase domain (PF05147), lasso peptide cyclase domain
(PF00733), cyanobactin/thiopeptide cyclodehydratase domain
(PF02624), graspetide cyclase domain (PF21068) and
methanobactin/pearlin MNIO domain (PF05114). A large
number of putative RiPP biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs)
that contain both JMJDs and class-defining RiPP synthetases
were identified (Table S1). Among them, a total of 102 BGCs
consist of a JMJD sequence that co-occurs with a sequence for
a class II lanthipeptide dehydratase domain (PF13575), which
correspond to JMJD clusters 2, 7, 20, 39, 144, and 163. We
next focused on cluster 20 for further investigation as it
contains the most abundant number of BGCs with syntenic

To enhance the coverage of our genome mining analysis, we
enriched gene clusters containing both JMJDs and class II
lanthipeptide synthetases by using lanthipeptide dehydratases
in cluster 20 as seeds for homology search in the NCBI
database (E-value 50). The resulting candidate BGCs were
then filtered based on JMJD co-occurrence analysis and further
assessed for potential precursor peptides using RODEO.42 As a
result, a total of 292 putative BGCs encoding class II
lanthipeptide synthetases and JMJDs along with 299 putative
precursor peptides were identified (Figure S2A). The
composition and organization of these BGCs are highly
homologous, encoding one precursor peptide, one class II
lanthipeptide synthetase (LanM), and two JMJD homologue
proteins (Figure 2C). The precursor peptides were sub-
sequently clustered using MMseqs2, resulting in four major
precursor subgroups, which are primarily distinguished by their
N-terminal leader peptide segments (Figure S2B).43 Notably,
all precursor peptides share a highly conserved C-terminal
(Thr/Ser)-Cys-(Xaa)3−4-Tyr-Cys motif, suggesting its role as
an essential modification site (Figure 2D).
Discovery of Lanthipeptide Corsin with a MeLan Ring
and a Cys3(S)-Tyr7(Cβ) Cross-Link. To explore the function
of JMJDs in these newly identified BGCs, we selected the cor
BGC from Streptomyces corchorusii for further investigation
(Figure 3A), which encodes the precursor peptide CorA, the
class II lanthipeptide synthetase CorM, the JMJD enzyme
CorD and a hypothetical protein CorB. No putative protease,
transporter or regulatory genes were identified 5 kb upstream
or downstream of the cor BGC. Primary sequence and three-
dimensional structure analysis revealed a moderate sequence
similarity (27.8%) and structural overlap (RMSD 3.242)
between CorB and CorD (Figure S3A).44 Further sequence
alignment of CorB and CorD with known αKGDs indicated
that CorD is a typical αKGD with the conserved iron-binding
motif (His157xAsp159···His218) and α-ketoglutarate-binding
residue (Lys172) (Figure S3B). In contrast, CorB lacks a key
iron-binding His residue and the α-ketoglutarate-binding Lys
residue, suggesting that CorB is an inactive αKGD homologue.
To explore the function of the cor BGC, we attempted to
ferment the native producer under various conditions, but
failed to detect CorA-derived products. Thus, we constructed a
cosmid containing the full cor gene cluster, designated as
pCOR. Heterologous expression of the pCOR cosmid in the
phylogenetically related model host S. lividans TK24 resulted
in the production of a cyclic peptide product, named corsin,
with a mass of [M + H]2+ = 504.2444, which matched the
product of C-terminal 9 amino acid segment of CorA after
one-fold dehydration and one-fold oxidative dehydrogenation
(Figure S4A). Due to the inaccessibility of the authentic cor
BGC product from S. corchorusii and the absence of a

Corsin was purified using XAD16 nonionic macroporous
resin, size exclusion chromatography, and reverse-phase high-
performance liquid chromatography (RP-HPLC) (Figure
S4B). Treatment of corsin with thiol-selective reagent N-
ethylmaleimide (NEM) and α,β-dehydroamino acid-specific
reagent 2-hydroxy-1-ethanethiol (βME) resulted in no
modification, indicating the absence of free Cys residues or
dehydroamino acids in corsin (Figure S5). Marfey’s analysis
detected signals of L-Gly-FDAA, L-Ile-FDAA, L-Arg-FDAA, L-
Val-FDAA, as well as DL-MeLan-(FDAA)2. No signal
matching the Tyr-FDAA derivative was detected, implicating
potential modification at the Tyr7 residue (Figure S6).
To further elucidate the structure of corsin, we performed a
series of two-dimensional NMR analysis, including 1H−1H
TCOSY, 1H−1H ROESY, 1H−13C HSQC, 1H−13C HMBC
and
1H−15N HSQC (Figure 3B and Figures S7−S12).
Significant chemical shift differences in the Cβ and/or Cα
regions of Thr2 and Cys8 were observed in corsin compared to
typical Thr and Cys residues (Tables S5 and S6). The 2D
1H−13C HMBC clearly revealed the inner-residue crosspeaks
of MeLan2(Cβ)-MeLan8(Hβ) and MeLan8(Cβ)-MeLan2-
(Hβ), indicating the formation of a MeLan cross-link via the
MeLan8(Cβ)-MeLan2(Hβ) bond (Figure S11). This cross-
link was further confirmed by strong NOE correlations
between MeLan2(Hα)-MeLan8(Hβ), MeLan2(Hβ)-MeLan8-
(Hβ) and MeLan2(Hγ)-MeLan8(Hβ) (Figure S9). These data
provide compelling evidence that a MeLan cross-link is formed
between Thr2 and Cys8 in corsin. In addition, inter-residue
crosspeaks of Cys3(Cβ)-Tyr7(Hβ) and Tyr7(Cβ)-Cys3(Hβ)
were observed in the 2D 1H−13C HMBC spectra, indicating
the formation of a cross-link between Cys3(Cβ) and Tyr7(Cβ)
(Figure S11). The conjugation with the thiol group likely
results in a less electron-shielded Tyr(Cβ), as evidenced by the
chemical shift of Tyr7(Cβ) at 55.9 ppm, which is significantly
larger than the typical value for Tyr(Cβ) (37.3 ppm).
Additionally, strong NOE correlations between Cys3(Hβ)-
Tyr7(Hβ) and Cys3(Hβ)-Tyr7(Hε) further support the
formation of this cross-link (Figure S9).
With the structure of corsin established, we proceeded to
analyze its biological functions. Preliminary bioactivity
evaluation of corsin showed no antimicrobial activities against
a limited collection of strains, including Escherichia coli,
Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Salmonella and Staphylococcus aureus.

Cu(I)-binding of methanobactin and the Cu(II)-binding of
noursin and bufferin.23,47,48 We evaluated the interaction of
corsin with metal ions by using a competitive titration assay
against the CAS/EBT-metal complex.49−51 The addition of
corsin gradually decreased the characteristic absorbance of the
CAS-Cu(II) complex at 610 nm, indicating the Cu(II)-
chelating property of corsin (Figure 3C and Figure
S13F).52,53 Furthermore, the solution of corsin and CuSO4
yielded a new compound with a single charge and m/z 1069.8,

The CorB-CorD Combination Catalyzes the Cys3(S)-
Tyr7(Cβ) Cross-Linking in Corsin. To elucidate corsin
biosynthesis, we coexpressed His6-tagged precursor peptide
CorA with CorB, CorD and CorM in E. coli BL21 (Tables S2−
S4). The resulting peptide product, CorAMeLan‑CY, was purified
and subjected to GluC endoprotease digestion, yielding a
modified core peptide derivative CorAMeLan‑CY‑((−3)‑9). MALDI-
TOF-MS analysis showed that the product CorAMeLan‑CY has a
mass of 20 Da less than the unmodified core peptide fragment
CorA(−3)‑9. This mass difference corresponds to the combined
modifications of one MeLan formation (−18 Da) and one
Cys-Tyr cross-linking (−2 Da) observed in corsin (Figure 4).
These results demonstrated that the enzymes CorM, CorB and
CorD are sufficient to generate the bicyclic architecture of
corsin.
To understand the function of each PTM enzyme, CorA was
coexpressed with CorM, yielding the CorM-modified CorA,
denoted as CorAMeLan. CorAMeLan has a mass loss of 18 Da
compared to the precursor peptide CorA (Figure 4). Tandem
MS analysis of CorAMeLan showed no fragment between Thr2
and Cys8, suggesting the formation of a MeLanThr2‑Cys8 cross-
link (Figure S14). Treatment of CorAMeLan with NEM and
βME resulted in one-fold NEM alkylation and no modification,
respectively, further supporting the CorM-catalyzed MeL-
anThr2‑Cys8 cross-linking (Figure S15). Coexpression of CorA-
CorM-CorB and CorA-CorM-CorD in E. coli BL21 produced
CorAMeLan as well, indicating that CorB and CorD need to
work synergistically to install the Cys-Tyr cross-link in CorA
(Figure 4).
We further investigated the enzymatic functions of CorM,
CorB and CorD via in vitro enzymatic reconstitution. CorA,
CorM, CorB, and CorD were individually expressed and
purified as N-terminally His6-tagged fusion proteins from E.
coli BL21 (Figure S16). Incubation of CorM with CorA in the
presence of ATP and Mg2+ efficiently generated CorAMeLan
within 2 h, which is consistent with the in vivo coexpression
experiment (Figure 5A). Incubation of CorM with CorACP and
CorA leader peptide (CorALP) under identical conditions
resulted in no modification of CorACP, suggesting that CorM is
leader peptide-dependent and exhibits no in trans activity
(Figure S17).
Further treatment of CorAMeLan with CorB and CorD in the
presence of Fe2+ ions and α-ketoglutarate resulted in nearly
quantitative production of CorAMeLan‑CY (Figure 5A and Figure
S18), confirming the cyclase function of CorB-CorD for Cys-
Tyr cross-linking. In contrast, the combination of CorB and
CorD barely modified precursor peptide CorA under identical
conditions, with only trace amounts of Tyr-oxidized CorA
derivative observed (Figure 5A and Figure S19). These results
indicated that the cyclic peptide CorAMeLan, instead of CorA, is
the authentic substrate of CorB and CorD. Therefore, the
biosynthesis of corsin is initiated by the modification of CorM
to install the MeLan cross-link, followed by the modification of
CorB-CorD for Cys-Tyr cross-linking (Figure 5A).
As the CorB-CorD combination represents the first example
of αKGDs catalyzing RiPP macrocyclization, we further
characterized their catalytic properties. First, omission of
either CorB or CorD from the CorAMeLan-CorB-CorD reaction
completely abolished the conversion of CorAMeLan to
CorAMeLan‑CY, indicating that both CorB and CorD are
indispensable for the Cys-Tyr cross-linking (Figure S20).
The omission of α-ketoglutarate or addition of excess EDTA to
remove Fe2+ ions from the CorAMeLan-CorB-CorD reaction
mixture also completely abolished the production of
CorAMeLan‑CY, indicating that the cross-linking reaction is
strictly α-ketoglutarate and Fe2+-dependent (Figure S20).
Sequence analysis suggested that CorD is a typical αKGD,
whereas CorB is likely an inactive homologue due to the
absence of a key iron-binding residue and the α-ketoglutarate-
binding residue (Figure S3). To examine their catalytic
functions, the putative iron-binding residue His218 in CorD
and His195 in CorB were mutated to Ala residues.
Coexpression of CorB-CorDH218A with CorM and CorA in E.
coli produced only product CorAMeLan, and the Cys-Tyr cross-
linking product was not detected (Figure S21A). In contrast,
the H195A mutation of CorB had no impact on its
coexpression with CorA, CorM and CorD, producing the
fully modified product CorAMeLan‑CY (Figure S21B). These
results demonstrated that CorD is the functional αKGD for

Substrate Tolerance of CorB-CorD-Catalyzed Cys-Tyr
Cross-Linking. To investigate the substrate tolerance of the
CorB-CorD-catalyzed Cys-Tyr cross-linking, a series of CorA
variants were coexpressed with CorM, CorB and CorD in E.
coli BL21. Substitutions of Cys3 with Ala, Ser, His, and Met
residues completely abolished the Cys-Tyr cross-linking,
yielding only MeLan-containing products (Figures S22−S25
and Table 1). No modification on Tyr7 was observed either.
Similarly, the modification products of CorAY7A and CorAY7H
variants contained only the MeLan ring, demonstrating the
strict requirement for Cys3 and Tyr7 for CorB-CorD-catalyzed
cross-linking (Figures S26−S27 and Table 1).
We next assessed whether altering the MeLan ring size
influenced the efficiency of CorB-CorD catalysis. Although Ala
insertion at both positions 3 and 8 expands the MeLan ring,
CorB-CorD catalyzed the Cys-Tyr cross-linking only in
CorAinsA3 but not in CorAinsA8, indicating a moderate tolerance
of CorB-CorD to size alterations of the outer ring (Figures
S28−S29 and Table 1). In contrast, disrupting the spacing of
inner ring between Cys3 and Tyr7, either by inserting Ala at
position 5 (CorAinsA5) or deleting Val6 (CorAdelV6), dramat-
ically impeded modification by both CorM and CorB-CorD
(Figures S30−S31 and Table 1). Further analysis revealed that
replacing Arg5 with Glu or Phe had no impact on MeLan
formation or Cys-Tyr cross-linking (Figures S32−S33 and
Table 1). However, mutating Val6 to Pro disrupted both
processes, highlighting its structural importance (Figure S34
and Table 1). Together, these results demonstrated that the
corsin modification enzymes are highly sensitive to changes in
the spatial arrangement and local conformation of the Cys-Tyr
cross-linked ring but exhibit flexibility toward other regions of
the MeLan scaffold.

■CONCLUSIONS
The CorB-CorD pair represents the first reported α-
ketoglutarate-dependent dioxygenase (αKGD) system to
catalyze radical-mediated cross-linking in RiPP biosynthesis,
which positions αKGDs as a new family of radical biocatalysts
capable of installing macrocyclic architectures and expands
their enzymatic functions beyond hydroxylation during RiPP
maturation.
A key feature of the CorB-CorD system is its reliance on an
inactive αKGD homologue, CorB, for catalysis. While CorD
retains the conserved Fe2+- and αKG-binding residues essential
for activity, CorB lacks these motifs yet is indispensable for
cross-linking. AlphaFold3 structural prediction and SEC
analysis both suggest that CorB and CorD form a
heterodimeric complex (Figures S35 and S36 and Table
S7).54 Such two-component systems are rare αKGD
enzymology but reminiscent of auxiliary proteins of metal-
loenzymes, such as the M16B protease family.55 Mutagenesis
studies show that CorB-CorD exhibits strict substrate
specificity for the MeLan precyclization and some level of
tolerance toward alteration of residues between Cys3 and
Tyr7. The leader peptide (LP)-independence nature of CorB-
CorD may offer practical advantages for engineering. Detailed
enzymology of CorB-CorD, including their interaction
mechanism, substrate recognition and catalytic process,
requires further investigation.
Overall, this work establishes αKGDs as radical catalysts for
RiPP macrocyclization, which promotes further exploration of
their diversity for novel cross-linking chemistries in RiPPs. By
