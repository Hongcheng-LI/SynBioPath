Revision of the Formycin A and Pyrazofurin Biosynthetic Pathways
Reveals Specificity for D‑Glutamic Acid and a Cryptic N‑Acylation
Step During Pyrazole Core Formation

ABSTRACT: Formycin A and pyrazofurin are two naturally occurring
pyrazole-derived C-nucleosides with antibacterial and antiviral activities.
While earlier studies have established the chemistry of C-glycosidic bond
formation as well as the subsequent steps in the biosynthesis of formycin A
and pyrazofurin, how the pyrazole ring itself is constructed remains elusive.
While N−N bond formation in the pyrazole ring was previously reported to
involve coupling of N6-hydroxylated L-lysine and L-glutamic acid catalyzed by
the hydrazine synthetase PyfG, herein PyfG and its homologue ForJ are
shown instead to recognize
D-glutamate instead of
L-glutamate. The
hydrazine product of ForJ/PyfG catalysis then releases α-hydrazino
D-
glutamic acid upon processing by the NAD-dependent oxidoreductase ForL. Furthermore, N-acylation of α-hydrazino D-glutamate
with an amino acid catalyzed by the ATP-grasp ligase ForM/PyfJ is indispensable for recognition by the FAD-dependent
oxidoreductase ForR/PyfK to perform dehydrogenation of the Cα−N bond and thereby form a hydrazone intermediate. This work
not only demonstrates that D-glutamic acid is the correct substrate for hydrazine biosynthesis but also reveals a cryptic N-acylation
step in the assembly of the pyrazole core. These results thus provide significant insights into the biosynthesis of pyrazole rings that
are rarely seen in natural products.
■INTRODUCTION
Formycin A (1) and pyrazofurin (2) are two C-nucleoside
natural products produced by Streptomyces kaniharaensis SF-
557 and Streptomyces candidus NRRL 3601, respectively.1,2
Formycin A has also been isolated from Nocardia interforma as
well as Streptomyces sp. MA406-A-1.3,4 As N-nucleoside
analogues, both 1 and 2 exhibit potent biological activity
against bacteria and viruses. For example, formycin is used as
an antileishmanial agent and can inhibit enzymes involved in
purine metabolism and be incorporated into parasite RNA.5−7
Likewise, pyrazofurin is a known inhibitor for orotidine 5′-
monophosphate decarboxylase and can thus impede the
biosynthesis of uridine nucleotides causing inhibition of
DNA synthesis and cell replication.8
The chemical structures of formycin A and pyrazofurin
feature an unusual pyrazole-derived nucleobase, in addition to
a characteristic C-glycosidic linkage (Figure 1A). Previous
studies have shown that construction of the C-glycosidic bonds
is catalyzed by the C-glycoside synthases ForT and PyfQ,
which couple the pyrazole rings (3 and 6) to phosphoribosyl
pyrophosphate (PRPP, 5) to generate the ribonucleotides 4
and 7, respectively (3 →4, 6 →7, Figure 1B).9,10 The
subsequent installation of the pyrimidine ring in formycin A (4
→1, Figure 1B) and the formation of the amide moiety in
pyrazofurin (7 →2, Figure 1B) have also been previously
described.9,11,12 Despite the advancements made in elucidating
the biosynthesis of these two C-nucleosides, however, how the
shared pyrazole core in 1 and 2 is assembled remains largely
unknown.
The biosynthetic gene clusters of formycin A (i.e., the for
cluster)11 and pyrazofurin (i.e., the pyf cluster)9,13 have been
identified. They share a set of homologous genes that have
been postulated to govern the assembly of pyrazoles 3 and 6
likely in a conserved manner (Figure 2A). Among them, the
forJ gene in the for cluster and the pyf G gene in the pyf cluster
have been annotated as encoding hydrazine synthetases as the
gene products are homologous with Spb40 (34% and 33%
identity, respectively), which catalyzes nitrogen−nitrogen (N−
N) bond formation between N6-hydroxylated L-lysine (9) and
glycine to produce the hydrazine intermediate 10 during the
biosynthesis of s56-p1 (8) (Figure 2B).14 The predicted
function of ForJ/PyfG was first examined by Du and co-
workers, who reported the substrates of PyfG (referred to as
PyrN in their work) as 9 and L-glutamic acid (L-Glu) to
produce the hydrazine product L-13 (Figure 2C).15 While this

■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
In the case of s56-p1 (8), production of hydrazinoacetic acid
(11) concomitant with the release of L-2-aminoadipate 6-
semialdehyde (12) from the Spb40 product (10) is the next
step in the biosynthetic pathway (Figure 2B). This oxidation
reaction (10 →11), which is also observed in the biosynthesis
of several other N−N bond containing natural products,16−18
is catalyzed by flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD)-dependent
enzyme Spb39.14 In the for/pyf cluster (Figure 2A), there exist
multiple genes annotated as encoding oxidases including two
flavin mononucleotide (FMN)-dependent monooxygenase
genes (forD/pyf M and forE/pyf N), two FAD-dependent
oxidoreductase genes (forR/pyf K and forS/pyf O), one Rieske
oxygenase gene (forU/pyf B), and one nicotinamide adenine
dinucleotide (NAD)-dependent oxidoreductase gene (forL/
pyf I). Among them, ForL/PyfI have been annotated as
saccharopine dehydrogenases, which are known to mediate
the conversion of saccharopine (15) to L-2-aminoadipate 6-
semialdehyde (12) and L-Glu through an imine intermediate
16 (Figure 2D).19 Given the structural similarity between L-13
and saccharopine (15), both containing a lysine and a glutarate
moiety, ForL/PyfI have been proposed to catalyze the
conversion of L-13 to 12 and α-hydrazino L-glutamic acid (L-
14) (Figure 2C).9,13
The proposed function of ForL/PyfI was initially inves-
tigated by attempting the preparation of L-13 using ForJ/PyfG
according to the reported procedure with minor modifica-
tions.15 A reaction mixture containing 20 μM ForJ or PyfG, 1
mM chemically prepared compound 9, 1 mM L-Glu, 5 mM
MgCl2, and 2.5 mM ATP in 50 mM 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-
piperazineethanesulfonic acid (HEPES) buffer (pH 7.5) was
allowed to proceed at room temperature overnight. All
enzymes used in this and later experiments were cloned from
the corresponding gene clusters and heterologously expressed
in Escherichia coli as N-His6 tagged fusion proteins unless
stated otherwise (see Supporting Information for details).
However, the production of L-13 was not observed with either
ForJ or PyfG despite multiple attempts under the above
incubation conditions. It should be noted that the ForJ purified
in-house was shown to exhibit N−N bond forming activity in
the conversion of the ester 17 to hydrazine 18, which is an
essential transformation in the biosynthesis of valanimycin
(19) (Figure 2E).20 Thus, an inactive enzyme is unlikely the
cause behind the inability to obtain L-13 in the above reaction.
Due to the difficulty in preparing the hydrazine L-13 using
the biosynthetic enzymes, this step was instead circumvented
via synthesis of the proposed ForL/PyfI product L-14 without
the use of biocatalysts (see the Supporting Information). This
intermediate is proposed to be a key precursor of pyrazoles 3
and 6, which could be generated upon oxidative intramolecular
C4−N2′ cyclization of L-14 (Figure 2C). However, no new
product was detected upon incubation of the synthesized L-14
with any of the flavin-dependent or NAD-dependent
oxidoreductases or the Rieske oxygenase encoded in the
gene clusters (see Figure 2A). These setbacks suggested
reconsideration of the original biosynthetic model as the
reaction sequence may require additional priming steps prior
to the oxidation/cyclization reactions. This included a
reassessment of a pair of genes found in both clusters and
annotated as encoding an ATP-grasp ligase (forM/pyf J) and
an amidohydrolase (forQ/pyfA). Conservation of these genes
between the two clusters hinted that the transformation of L-14
to 3/6 may involve the formation of a cryptic amide bond
catalyzed by ForM/PyfJ, which could eventually be eliminated
upon hydrolysis catalyzed by ForQ/PyfA.
It was initially hypothesized that ForM/PyfJ may activate the
C5 carboxylate of L-14 and thus facilitate an intramolecular
cyclization of L-14 to form the six-membered ring product 20
(Figure 3). After dehydrogenation (20 →21), ForQ/PyfA
could catalyze hydrolysis of the amide bond in 21 (Figure 3)
and promote intramolecular five-membered ring formation to
yield dihydropyrazole 23, which could eventually become 3/6
upon several oxidation reactions (Figure 3). To test whether
ForM mediates intramolecular cyclization of L-14, 6 μM ForM
was incubated with 0.5 mM L-14, 100 mM KCl, 5 mM MgCl2,
and 2.5 mM ATP in 50 mM HEPES buffer (pH 7.5) at room
temperature overnight. However, the anticipated cyclization
product 20 was not observed. Moreover, the commercially
available 21 was found not to react with ForQ casting further
doubt on the proposed pathway shown in Figure 3. It was then

Both examples involve installation of an N-acyl group, which
may function effectively as a protecting group that is eventually
deprotected by a hydrolase. It is thus reasonable to hypothesize

pyrazofurin biosynthesis. Thus, ForM/PyfJ may catalyze
acylation of the terminal nitrogen (N2′) in L-14 prior to
subsequent modifications before ForQ/PyfA catalyzes amide
bond hydrolysis at some later stage.
ForM is annotated as a glycinamide ribonucleotide
synthetase (GARS),22 which catalyzes amide bond formation
between phosphoribosylamine (39) and glycine with con-
comitant production of ADP and phosphate (39 →40, Figure
5A). To test whether ForM also recognizes glycine, 1 mM
glycine was incubated with 6 μM ForM in an assay containing
1 mM L-14, 100 mM KCl, 5 mM MgCl2, and 2.5 mM ATP in
50 mM HEPES buffer (pH 7.5) at room temperature
overnight. LCMS analysis of the reaction mixture by extracted
ion chromatography (EIC) indeed showed a peak correspond-
ing to the ligation product between L-14 and glycine, namely,
L-41 (calcd for C7H14N3O5
+ [M + H]+: 220.0928, found:
220.0928) (bottom two traces, Figure 5C), which after
derivatization with fluorenylmethyloxycarbonyl chloride
(Fmoc-Cl) was confirmed to be Fmoc-L-41 by comparison
with the standard (Figure S2B). These results established that
ForM catalyzes the acylation of the N2′ nitrogen of L-14 with
glycine.
ForM-catalyzed acylation of the terminal nitrogen of the
hydrazino acid L-14 to yield L-41 may then facilitate further
biosynthetic processing including oxidation of the Cα−N bond
by an FAD-dependent oxidoreductase as in the case of triacsin
A and actinopyridazinone A biosynthesis (Figure 4).16,21 In
other words, formycin A biosynthesis may proceed via the

which is annotated as an FAD-dependent oxidoreductase
exhibiting 49% sequence identity to a glycine/D-amino acid
oxidase (GenBank: AVW82901.1).10 In order to test this
hypothesis, the aforementioned ForM reaction mixture

under an aerobic atmosphere at room temperature overnight.
However, no reaction was observed as the substrate L-41
remained unconsumed (top trace, Figure 5C).
The annotation of ForR as a glycine/D-amino acid oxidase
homologue suggested that ForR may be specific for substrates
with the D-configuration such as D-41 as opposed to the L-
configuration such as L-41. If true, however, then ForM should
also accept
D-14 as a substrate. To test this,
D-14 was
synthesized and incubated with ForM under the same
conditions as described above for L-14. As a result, D-41
(calcd for C7H14N3O5
+ [M + H]+: 220.0928, found: 220.0929)
was indeed generated (bottom two traces, Figure 5D). Fmoc-
derivatized D-41 (Fmoc-D-41) could thus be isolated from a 12
mL reaction with ForM and D-14 followed by workup with
Fmoc-Cl and NMR characterization (Table S2 and Figures
S3−S7).
Having established that ForM can also catalyze the
formation of D-41 from D-14, a ForM-ForR coupled reaction
with D-14 was conducted to test whether D-41 would be
recognized by ForR. LCMS analysis showed the depletion of D-
41 and the appearance of an EIC peak consistent with the
dehydrogenated product 42 (calcd for C7H12N3O5
+ [M + H]+:
218.0771, found: 218.0768) (top trace, Figure 5D). The ForR
product was purified from a 10 mL ForM-ForR coupled
reaction with D-14 and glycine in order to characterize its
structure, which was confirmed to be 42 by NMR analysis
revealing a characteristic 13C chemical shift of a hydrazone
carbon for C2 (149 ppm) (Table S3 and Figures S8−S12).21
Thus, ForR catalyzes the dehydrogenation of
D-41 to 42
(Figure 5B). Importantly, ForM can process both L-14 and D-
14, to form L-41 and D-41 (Figure 5C,D). In contrast, ForR is
specific to D-41 and unable to accept L-41. It was also noted
that the homologues of ForM and ForR in the pyf cluster, i.e.,
PyfJ and PyfK, respectively, were shown to catalyze the same
reactions as those of ForM and ForR (Figure S13).
To investigate whether ForM also accepts other amino acids,
the remaining 19 proteinogenic amino acids besides glycine
were screened against ForM. It turned out that ForM also
recognizes L-serine, L-alanine, L-threonine, and L-proline to
yield the ligation product with
D-14 (Figure S14).
Furthermore, these ligation products can all be converted to
the corresponding hydrazone products by ForR as well (Figure
S14). Steady-state kinetic analysis of ForM with these five
amino acids showed that L-threonine has the highest kcat/KM
value (10.9 ± 1.8 min−1·mM−1), which is about three- to eight-
fold greater than the other four amino acids (Figure S15).
The current models of formycin A and pyrazofurin
biosynthesis involve L-glutamate rather than D-glutamate as a
biosynthetic precursor (Figures 1 and 2C). Therefore, the
observation that ForR/PyfK is specific for D-41 implied the
necessity for an epimerization step during the early stages of
the biosynthetic pathway. However, neither the for cluster nor
the pyf cluster appears to encode an epimerase prompting the
alternative hypothesis that the hydrazine synthetase ForJ/PyfG
may also be specific for D-Glu instead of L-Glu. Therefore, 20
μM ForJ or PyfG was incubated with 1 mM 9,1 mM D-Glu, 5
mM MgCl2, and 2.5 mM ATP in 50 mM HEPES buffer (pH
7.5) at room temperature overnight. Indeed, the hydrazine
product D-13 (calcd for C11H22N3O6
+ [M + H]+: 292.1503,
found: 292.1499) was detected in the reactions with both ForJ
and PyfG (Figures 6B and S16). The Fmoc-derivatized
product was purified from a large-scale ForJ reaction and
found to be consistent with Fmoc-D-13 upon NMR character-
ization (Table S4 and Figures S17−S21). These results also
explain why L-13 was not observed when L-Glu was used in the
ForJ/PyfG reaction as discussed above (Figures 6B and S16);
however, they differ from the previous report that L-Glu is a
substrate.15 A possible explanation for the discrepancy is the
use of 20 mM L-Glu in the previously published experiments,
such that even 0.5% D-Glu would have yielded detectable
product in concentrations up to 0.1 mM. It should be pointed
out that the D-Glu used in the ForJ/PyfG reaction may come
from epimerization of L-Glu catalyzed by glutamate racemase
as
D-Glu is an essential component of peptidoglycan in
bacterial cell walls.23 Indeed, a glutamate racemase-encoding
gene (GenBank: MQS15335.1) is found in the genome of the
formycin A producer S. kaniharaensis.
Having established the N−N bond forming activity of ForJ,
the proposed ForL-catalyzed oxidation of D-13 to yield D-14
and L-2-aminoadipate 6-semialdehyde (12) was tested. While
ForL could not be expressed in E. coli, it could be expressed
and purified from Streptomyces lividans TK24 as an N-His6
tagged protein. Thus, a ForJ reaction was run, producing ca.
0.8 mM D-13 followed by deproteinization and addition of 20
μM ForL and 1 mM NAD before further incubation at room
temperature overnight. LCMS analysis of the reaction mixture
confirmed the formation of D-14 in the presence of both ForL
and NAD by comparison with the standard of D-14, whereas
omitting either ForL or NAD abolished the production of D-14
(Figure 6C). These results demonstrate that ForL catalyzes the
formation of D-14 from D-13 (Figure 6A).

oxidation by a total of six electrons. There remain four
conserved putative oxidase genes shared by the for and pyf
clusters (Figure 2A), which are potentially involved in the
pyrazole formation. There are two genes among them
annotated as encoding FMN-dependent monooxygenases
(forD/pyf M & forE/pyf N). These two genes may be

pyrazofurin, however, pyrazole 3 in formycin A has an amino

additional genes that are absent in the pyf cluster and encode
the putative hydroxy acid dehydrogenase ForF as well as the
PLP-dependent aminotransferase ForI.25 It is therefore
proposed that ForF catalyzes oxidation of the C3 hydroxyl
group to a carbonyl group that is then reductively aminated in
a reaction catalyzed by ForI to install the amino group at C3
(45 →46 →47, Figure 7).
The next step is likely hydroxylation at C4 catalyzed by the
Rieske oxygenase ForU/PyfB (47 →48, 45 →49, Figure 7),
followed by desaturation of the C3−C4 bond (48 →50, 49 →
51, Figure 7). The latter step could be catalyzed by ForS/
PyfO, which is annotated as a succinate dehydrogenase that
catalyzes the desaturation of succinate to generate fumarate.26
Finally, amidohydrolase ForQ/PyfA could catalyze hydrolysis
of the amide bond in 50/51 to expose the terminal N2′
nitrogen (50 →52, 51 →54, Figure 7), thereby facilitating
intramolecular nucleophilic addition of N2′ at the electrophilic
C4 carbon to yield pyrazole 3 or 6 with concomitant
elimination of the C4 hydroxyl group (52 →53 →3, 54 →
55 →6, Figure 7). It should be noted that alternative reaction
sequences such as C4 hydroxylation occurring prior to C3
hydroxylation or transamination taking place after C3 and C4
hydroxylation are also feasible. Likewise, the cyclization step
(52 →3, 54 →6) may also be a condensation process
involving tautomerization of 52/54 to its corresponding
ketone followed by Schiff-base formation with N2′ nitrogen
(see Figure S22).
■CONCLUSIONS
Several key steps toward pyrazole ring biosynthesis in formycin
A and pyrazofurin have been established (9/D-Glu →44,
Figure 7). In contrast to a previous report, ForJ/PyfG has been
demonstrated to catalyze N−N bond formation between 9 and
D-Glu rather than L-Glu. ForL/PyfI then liberates hydrazino
acid D-14 from D-13. ForM/PyfJ catalyzes transfer of an amino
acyl group (e.g., glycine or
L-threonine) to the terminal
nitrogen of D-14 to form 43. The C−N bond of 43 is then
dehydrogenated to yield 44 in the presence of ForR/PyfK.
This study not only demonstrates that D-Glu is the precursor
for pyrazole rings 3 and 6 but also reveals that a cryptic N-
acylation of the hydrazinoacid intermediate is involved in the
biosynthesis of formycin A and pyrazofurin. Based on these
results, the later steps to yield the pyrazoles 3 and 6 can be
proposed and are currently under investigation.
