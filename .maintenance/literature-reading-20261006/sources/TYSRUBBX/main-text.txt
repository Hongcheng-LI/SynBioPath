Photobiocatalytic Radical Hydroalkylation with C(sp3)−H Bonds
Enabled by Engineered Imine Reductase and Redox Buffering

ABSTRACT: Photobiocatalytic systems that leverage nicotinamide- or
flavin-dependent oxidoreductases have enabled diverse hydro-functionaliza-
tion of alkenes. However, these typically rely on preactivated radical
precursors and native reducing equivalents (e.g., reduced nicotinamide
adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH)). Direct hydroalkylation using
abundant C−H substrates remains attractive yet highly challenging. Here,
we report a photobiocatalytic “redox buffering” strategy that repurposes
imine reductase (IRED) to catalyze the hydroalkylation of enamides with
commercial alkanes. Cooperative external peroxide and synthetic photo-
catalyst activate C(sp3)−H bonds via hydrogen atom transfer (HAT), while
engineered IRED with its native NADPH precisely orchestrates subsequent C-radical addition to enamides and stereodetermining
HAT steps. This synergistic integration of an external oxidant with the native reductant, coupled with the merging of photoredox and
biocatalysis, unlocks unnatural enzyme functionalities, producing enantioenriched amines (with up to >99% ee, low to 0.02 mol % of
IRED).
■INTRODUCTION
In recent years, the integration of biocatalysis1 and visible-light
catalysis2 has emerged as a vital approach to unlock new
enzyme reactivity.3 From the viewpoint of catalysts, diverse
naturally occurring enzymes have been reshaped to enable
challenging asymmetric reactions. This includes nicotinamide-4
and flavin-5,6 dependent oxidoreductases, pyridoxal-7 and
thiamine-8 dependent enzymes, class I aldolases,9 as well as
nonheme iron enzymes.10 Among these, the ketoreductases
and ene-reductases have garnered the most attention and
demonstrated the largest number of reactions.11 Typically, they
facilitate stereocontrolled net-reductive hydro-functionalization
of alkenes, employing oxidative radical precursors (Figure 1A).
Key mechanistic steps involve single-electron reduction of
radical precursors by the cofactors (reduced nicotinamide
adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) or hydroquinone
state flavin mononucleotide (FMNhq)) for radical initiation
and an enantioselective hydrogen atom transfer (HAT) for
prochiral radical termination. So far, the radical precursors
encompass organic halides,4a,c,5a,b,g,6 N-(acyloxy) phthalimi-
des,4b,c pyridinium salts,4c etc.,4e,5c,h,i all of which require
preactivation. Regarding oxidoreductase-based photobiocatal-
ysis that typically relied on a native reductive equivalent (e.g.,
NAD(P)H) to complete the catalytic cycle, the direct
generation of C-radicals from ubiquitous alkanes12 remains
underexplored. The central challenge arises from an inherent
conflict between the oxidative activation of inert C−H
substrates and the subsequent reductive HAT13 for final
stereocenter construction. This paradoxical requirement for
simultaneous oxidant and reductant involvement is often
perceived as a counterintuitive design paradigm in catalysis.
Nature offers an evolutionary blueprint to transcend this
redox dichotomy. In biological systems, cytochrome P450
enzymes14 reconcile the four-electron oxidizing power of
dioxygen with two-electron reductants such as NAD(P)H,
enabling efficient C−H hydroxylation under mild physiological
conditions (Figure 1B). This compensatory redox pairing
operates as a self-balancing system, preserving cellular redox
homeostasis and avoiding harsh chemical regimes. Notably,
Stahl and co-workers15 recently applied a synthetic “redox
buffering” strategy, where an additional sacrificial reductant
promoted net oxidative copper-catalyzed radical C−H
functionalization. These precedents inspired our hypothesis
that deliberate synergy between an oxidant and a reductant
could unlock the new enzyme reactivity.
Here, we report a photobiocatalytic “redox buffering”
strategy that leverages the synergetic effects of photocatalysis
and biocatalysis, an oxidant and a reductant, to achieve a
challenging enantioselective radical hydroalkylation with C−H
bonds16 (Figure 1C). This strategy enables the direct C−H
activation of alkanes by introducing a peroxide as the HAT

■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Reaction Development. Our research group has been
dedicated to pushing the boundary of biocatalysis using
chemomimetic radical pathways.5d,8a,b,19 Very recently, we
successfully repurposed IRED for photobiocatalytic asymmet-
ric hydroalkylations using prefunctionalized radical precur-
sors.4c IREDs naturally catalyze reductive aminations of
carbonyls with amines through a two-electron hydride transfer
mechanism.17 We are motivated to broaden the catalytic
repertoire of IREDs with photoinduced single-electron radical
pathways. To validate the ideal of direct utilization of C(sp3)−
H bond as the radical precursor, we chose commercial
cyclohexane 2a as the radical precursor, tert-butyl perox-
ybenzoate (TBPB) as the oxidant (HAT reagent), and N-(1-
phenylvinyl)-acetamide 1a as the radical acceptor to begin our
study (Figure 2A). The natural turnover system consisting of
glucose dehydrogenase (GDH), NADP+, and glucose was used
to provide a terminal reducing equivalent. In 2021, Turner and
co-workers17b first reported pIR117 that enabled the reductive
amination of amines with ketones with excellent selectivity.
Using pIR117 (denoted as IRED1 herein) as the biocatalyst
and Ir(ppy)3 as the external photocatalyst, we found that the
targeted C−H alkylation furnished desirable amine product 3a
in 20% yield and 71% ee (Figure 2A, entry 1).
To further enhance the enantioselectivity and reactivity of
the reaction, we employed a protein engineering strategy.
Initially, we performed a docking study between the modeled
IRED1 protein structure and product 3a. This process
identified 14 potential key amino acid residues for the next
mutagenesis, specifically: S95, I120, L121, A122, T123, V174,
L175, M178, V212, L216, S237, P241, H242, and A245
(Figure 2B). To enable high-throughput and standardized
screening, we established a photobiocatalytic reaction setup
(Figure 2C), which is based on a 96-well plate with good light-
transmitting properties and combined with a 96-well LED
matrix light source. Based on our previous work, which showed
that the T123 site has a significant impact on the selectivity, we
prioritized performing saturation mutagenesis on T123 (Figure
S6). This resulted in the T123F mutant achieving 86% ee with

We also investigated other HAT reagents, such as
peroxides,21 redox-active esters,22 and pyridinium salts,23 and
the results showed that only TBPB could smoothly promote
the reaction (Figures 2A and S3). This proves that the choice
of oxidant is crucial for the compatibility and synergy of this
dual catalysis. Subsequent control experiments indicated that
the reaction could not proceed in the absence of IRED or the
NADPH regeneration system (Figure 2A, entries 3−4). In the
absence of a photocatalyst, under dark conditions, or in an air
atmosphere, the reaction yielded only a small amount of chiral
amine product (entries 5−7). A mixed cosolvent system
DMSO/MeOH, slightly improved the yield, compared to
DMSO alone (entry 8). We attributed this to the better
solubility of the alkanes in methanol. Noteworthy is that
reducing the enzyme loading to 0.2 mol % had minimal impact
on yield, keeping ee unchanged (entry 9). Remarkably, the
enzyme dosage could be further lowered to 0.02 mol %,
yielding 99% ee and an impressive TON value of 1700 (entry
10). Also, we monitored the residual amounts of cyclohexane
and found that a large proportion of the alkanes remained
unconsumed. Increasing the alkane loading did not improve
the reaction outcome (Table S12).

with different aryl substitutions transformed smoothly.

property of the substituted functional groups, the correspond-
ing chiral amine products were obtained with good to excellent
enantioselectivity (3a−3h, 92 to >99% ee). However, para-
substituted aryl enamides are more significantly affected. When

It is worth noting that the presence or absence of IRED did
not affect the formation of alkyl radicals. The outcomes
support the formation of the alkyl radicals and indicate that the
alkyl radical species could be generated outside the protein’s
active site. Next, we monitored the reactions in the presence or
absence of IREDHAT (Figure 4B). In the control reaction
without IREDHAT, trace amounts of trisubstituted enamide 8,
and a noticeable increasing trend toward the formation of
ketone 9, were observed. Enamide 8 and the corresponding
imine 7 are supposed to be the side-products of the
overoxidation of the prochiral benzylic radicals. Hydrolysis of
7 and 8 gives ketone 9. In contrast, under the standard
photobiocatalysis with IREDHAT, the total amount of 8 and 9
was almost undetectable. These results further suggest that
alkyl radicals can be generated without IREDs and underscore
the vital role of the enzyme’s active site in accelerating the
radical addition step and ensuring the second HAT step.
Then, Stern−Volmer quenching experiments were con-
ducted to understand the photoredox radical initiation step
(Figure 4C). Substrate 1a or NADPH failed to quench the
excited state Ir(ppy)3, thereby disfavoring the reductive
quenching pathway. In contrast, the HAT reagent-1 TBPB
demonstrated pronounced quenching effects, suggesting an
oxidative quenching pathway involving single-electron transfer
(SET) between the excited [IrIII]* and TBPB. Isotopic labeling
experiments were performed too (Figure 4D). When the
reaction was performed with a deuterated nicotinamide
cofactor (NADP-D) in situ formed with D-glucose-1-d1, the
newly formed stereogenic center was labeled with 84%
deuterium. No deuterium was incorporated when deuterated
cyclohexane or deuterated buffer was applied. These results
demonstrate that the hydrogen atom adjacent to nitrogen was
exclusively derived from NADPH. Furthermore, a kinetic
isotope effect of kH/kD = 1.55 was measured between the
parallel reactions with cyclohexane and deuterated cyclohexane
(Figure 4E). These imply that hydrogen atom abstraction
initiating the alkyl radical may be the rate-limiting step. Finally,
we also performed light on−off experiment,26 which showed
that continuous light irradiation is required for product
accumulation (Figure S13).
Based on these mechanistic studies and literatures,4c we
proposed a catalytic cycle as outlined in Figure 5. Initially, the
nicotinamide cofactor NADP+ is reduced to NADPH by the
GDH-catalyzed regeneration system (Int. A →Int. B).
Simultaneously, the HAT reagent TBPB is single-electron
reduced by the photoexcited Ir(ppy)3*. This process generates
oxidizing [IrIV] and a tert-butoxy radical. The tert-butoxy
radical then abstracts a hydrogen atom from the alkane,
producing a C-radical species. This alkyl radical migrates into
the active site of engineered IREDHAT and is captured by
IREDHAT-bound enamide to generate the prochiral α-amido
radical intermediate (Int. C →Int. D). Moreover, DFT
calculations suggest the prochiral α-amido radical intermediate
is relatively persistent, which could first be generated outside
the pocket and then migrate into the active site (Figures S17
and S18). Then, NADPH enantioselectively delivers a
hydrogen atom to the prochiral radical intermediate, resulting
in the formation of the enantioenhanced amine product (Int.
D →Int. E). Finally, the SET between [IrIV] and NADP·
completes both photoredox and biocatalytic cycles.4e Alter-
natively, SET from NADPH to [IrIV] and subsequent
stereodetermining HAT between the resultant NADP·+ and
the prochiral α-amido radical could also be in operation.

■CONCLUSION
In summary, we have successfully achieved the enantioselective
radical alkylation of C(sp3)−H bonds by developing a
synergistic system that harnesses the power of an oxidant
and a reductant, an engineered IRED and a photoredox
catalyst. This redox buffering strategy boasts several unique
features. (i) For the first time in non-natural biotransforma-
tions, oxidant and reductant are employed synergistically. They
operate in concert to broaden the enzyme boundaries and
provide new design concepts for challenging reactions. (ii) The
radicals are initially generated outside the protein’s active site
before entering the active site of enzymes. This reaction
pattern offers more possibilities for merging different radical
generation approaches and for accepting broader radical
precursors. (iii) The imine reductase, engineered via high-
throughput methods, fulfills dual roles: it not only ensures high
enantioselectivity but also modulates non-natural radical
reactivity by accelerating the philicity-mismatched addition of
electron-rich radicals to enamides.
