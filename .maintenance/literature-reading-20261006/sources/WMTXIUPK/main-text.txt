Biosynthesis of γ‑Alkylidenebutenolide Derivatives Reveals an
Atom-Deleting Lactone Ring Contraction by Dual-Enzyme Cascade

ABSTRACT: Ring contraction represents a characteristic atom-level skeletal editing strategy in natural products. While this
biosynthetic approach has garnered increasing attention, its role in modifying the lactone ring size remains underexplored. Here, we
report a novel δ- to γ-lactone contraction during γ-alkylidenebutenolide biosynthesis in Aspergillus candidus. This dual-enzymatic
cascade involves multifunctional oxidase PesD oxidizing a PKS-derived α-pyrone to a cyclic carbonate intermediate, followed by the
DUF3237 enzyme PesF excising a molecule of CO2 to achieve ring contractionan unprecedented function for this enzyme family.
Computational, crystallographic, and site-directed mutagenesis studies reveal that PesF employs a noncanonical acid−base
mechanism, with Glu99 serving as a key catalytic residue that fulfills an unusual dual role.
R
ing contraction constitutes a pivotal transformation in
natural product (NP) biosynthesis, enabling atom-level
scaffold remodeling.1−3 In recent years, remarkable ring
contractions have been progressively uncovered, including
those involved in the biosynthesis of branched-chain sugar D-
apiose,4 the antitumor compound oxetanocin A,5 and the
gibberellin phytohormones.6 In these transformations, a
diverse array of enzymes was engaged to facilitate the selective
extrusion or deletion of molecular fragments through
mechanisms such as oxygenation,7 metal-dependent C−C
bond cleavage,8 and radical-mediated rearrangement,5,9 there-
by reconstructing inherently complex scaffolds. Investigation of
these processes elucidates the chemical logic of NP scaffold
remodeling, while inspiring innovative biocatalyst develop-
ment.
γ-Alkylidenebutenolide, featuring an α,β-unsaturated γ-
lactone core with an exocyclic γ,δ-double bond, is a structural
motif broadly found in bioactive NPs, including the antifungal
chloranthalactone A and the anticancer EBC-329 (Figure
1A).10−13 Although chemical syntheses are well-estab-
lished,14−16 their biosynthetic pathways remain elusive. To
date, the sole confirmed route occurs in tetronate antibiotics,
where an atypical γ-alkylidenebutenolide fragment is synthe-
sized via intermolecular chain fusion (Figure 1B).17,18
However, the glycerol-derived building block intrinsically
precludes the δ-substituted products, while most γ-alkylidene-
butenolides possess substitutions at the δ-position. Shrestha
Chatterjee et al. hypothesized a biosynthetic route for δ-
substituted γ-alkylidenebutenolides based on this chain fusion
logic, but the speculative pathway involves at least eight
regioselective modifications (Figure 1B).11 Therefore, explor-
ing the biosynthesis of these significant yet challenging γ-
alkylidenebutenolides may deepen our understanding of
natural product assembly and inspire novel biocatalytic
strategies.
Here, we investigated an ∼21 kb orphan polyketide
biosynthetic gene cluster (BGC) from Aspergillus candidus,
designated pes (Figure 2A). Comparative metabolite profiling
of wild-type and ΔpesA strains confirmed that this cluster is
responsible for producing pestalotiolactone B19 (1) and three
new aspergilactones A−C (2−4), all bearing γ-alkylidenebu-
tenolide−cyclopentane scaffolds (Figures S1−S5; Tables S6−
S8). [1-13C]-Acetate labeling demonstrated 13C-incorporation
at C-3/5/7/9/11 of 1, evidencing cyclization and decarbox-
ylation of a linear polyketide precursor (Figure S13). Given the
absence of a chain-release domain in the polyketide synthase
(PKS), we propose spontaneous off-loading of the polyketide
chain yields an α-pyrone intermediate (Figure 1B).20 This
intermediate is hypothesized to undergo an unprecedented
enzymatic ring contraction in which the pyranone carbonyl is
excised to yield a γ-lactone scaffold (Figure 1B).
We used heterologous expression of the pes genes in
A.oryzae NSAR1 (Figure 2C) to investigate the biosynthesis of
aspergilactones.21,22 Coexpression of pesABDEFG yielded 1, 4,
9, and 10 but not 2 or 3, suggesting that the dehydratase
responsible for generating 2 and 3 may be encoded outside the
cluster (Figure 2C, i). Compounds 9 and 10 were identified as
double-bond isomers of 4 and 1, respectively (Table S13;
Figures S10 and S11), and underwent spontaneous conversion
to their corresponding isomers (Figure S14). Exclusion of pesB
(MFS transporter) and pesE (α/β-hydrolase) did not affect
product formation (Figure 2C, ii), indicating that they are not
required. Expression of pesADF led to the accumulation of E/Z

C7 or C11 enables intramolecular C−C bond formation via
single-electron transfer (SET), after which the intermediate
successively undergoes OH rebound and host-enzyme-
catalyzed hydration to yield the final products (Figure S15).
Radical trapping experiments using TEMPO detected adduct
11 (m/z 376.2482 [M + H]+; Figures 2D and S15), supporting
the SET mechanism. Incubation of 6 with PesG-containing
crude proteins yielded 1, 4, 9, and 10 (Figures 2D and S16),
further confirming that PesG catalyzes the cyclopentane-
forming step. Unlike canonical laccases mediating intermo-
lecular phenol coupling,24 PesG represents the first laccase
catalyzing intramolecular C−C bond formation, broadening
the catalytic scope of this enzyme family.
We subsequently investigated the upstream enzymes to
validate the proposed ring-contraction pathway for γ-
alkylidenebutenolides. Expression of pesA alone yielded 5 as
the sole product (Figure 2C, vi), which was confirmed as an α-
pyrone derivative resulting from a 1,3,5-triketo intermediate
(Figure S6; Table S9). Coexpression of pesAD generated ring-
opened tetranor compounds 7 and 8, whereas coexpression of
pesAF produced no additional metabolites (Figure 2C, iv and
v). These observations suggest that flavin-dependent mono-
oxygenase PesD and DUF3237-containing enzyme PesF both
play pivotal roles in the ring contraction process. Genome
mining uncovered widespread homologues of the PesD−PesF
pair within putative PKS clusters (Figure S17), suggesting a
conserved biosynthetic logic. The cooperative function of this
enzyme pair was further supported by in vitro reconstitution
assays, where purified PesD and PesF jointly converted 5 into
6 (Figure 2E, v). The lack of effect upon EDTA addition
confirmed that the PesF activity does not rely on metal ions
(Figure S20). Purified PesD converted 5 to 7 in the presence
of NADH or NADPH (Figures 2E, iii and S21), while 8 was
not detected. This suggests that PesD acts as a multifunctional
oxidase and that 8 derives from an endogenous enzyme in A.
oryzae. Ultrafiltration assay was subsequently performed, in
which the addition of PesF to the filtrate obtained from
preincubation of PesD with 5 yielded 6 and 7, whereas a one-
pot reaction containing equimolar PesD and PesF produced
only 6 (Figure 2E, v−vii). These results demonstrate that PesF
catalyzes the ring contraction of a PesD-released intermediate,
thereby competing with PesD-driven iterative oxidation.
Consistent with its observed multifunctionality, phylogenetic
analysis clusters PesD with the O-type Baeyer−Villiger
monooxygenases (BVMOs) and aromatic hydroxylases (Fig-
ures S22 and S23), supporting its bifunctional oxidative role.
Mechanistically, the reaction likely initiates with an electro-
philic attack by flavin C4a−OOH on the α-keto carbon of 5,
analogous to the 2,6-DHPH paradigm,26 generating Int-1,
followed by oxygen insertion at the C2−C3 bond to afford a
transient carbonate anhydride Int-2 (Figure 2B).27 Int-2
rapidly undergoes (pseudo)hydrolysis to 12, which then
decarboxylates and eliminates the hydroxyl group to yield
13. Subsequent oxidation by PesD furnishes C10 product 7
(Figure S24). Time-course experiments followed by MS2
fragmentation revealed transient oxidized derivatives (12−
18). However, Int-1 and Int-2 remained elusive, reflecting the
inherent challenge of detecting labile intermediates in
oxygenase reactions.28,29 Their progressive oxygenation and
chain cleavage provide direct evidence for the multifunctional
oxidative activity of PesD. Systematic molecular docking with
substrates (5, Int-1, Int-4, and 13−17) revealed that Tyr231
and Ser345 mediate substrate positioning via hydrogen
bonding (Figure S26). Alanine substitution at either residue
abolished activity (Figure S27A,B), confirming their catalytic
essentiality. Furthermore, in vitro assays showed that PesD
selectively cleaves the α-keto acid 19 (3-methyl-2-oxopenta-
noic acid) to 20, but not the β-keto analogue 21 (Figure
S27C), establishing the requirement of a vicinal carbonyl
group for BVMO-type activity.
The production of 7 indicates that PesF acts before full
oxidative degradation, thereby channeling the metabolic flux
toward butenolide. To elucidate the catalytic timing and
mechanism of PesF, we incubated 5 with PesD and PesF under
a 18O2 atmosphere, resulting in +4 Da mass shifts in 6 (m/z
225), while no incorporation was observed with H2
18O
(Figures 2B and S28). These results confirm both oxygen
atoms in 6 are sourced from O2, indicating that PesF catalyzes
a ring contraction via the anhydride intermediate Int-2.

9,000 members across 3,745 species.30 Nevertheless, their
functional roles in NP biosynthesis remain largely unexplored,

ring contraction. The discovery of the unique catalytic activity
of PesF expands the functional repertoire of this family,
prompting a structural investigation to elucidate its mecha-
nistic basis. We determined its crystal structure at 1.6 Å
resolution (PDB: 9M2Z), along with the cocrystal complex

engaging in hydrophobic interactions with residues such as
Val25, Pro68, and Val134, while the δ-lactone moiety forms
hydrogen bonds with Glu45, Gln79, and Arg138 (Figure S32).
Notably, Glu99 is the only acidic residue positioned near C-10,
suggesting its potential involvement in catalysis.
Molecular docking of Int-2 revealed high structural
congruence with cocrystallized 5, maintaining key electrostatic
interactions (Figure 3B). This similarity informs both the
hypothesis of a preorganized transition-state conformation for
Int-2 and subsequent density functional theory (DFT)
analyses. DFT calculations revealed a stepwise mechanism
initiated by C4−OH deprotonation via a general base,
generating an enolate intermediate that triggers conjugated
rearrangement (Figure 3A). Concurrently, Glu99 donates a
proton to C-11 through TS1e (ΔG‡ = 15.5 kcal/mol), yielding
the skipped diene IC3e (Int-3). Glu99 then acts as a general
base to activate a water molecule, which nucleophilically
attacks the carbonate carbonyl to trigger a ring opening.

is generated, which readily undergoes intramolecular nucleo-

Computational analysis shows that Michael additions afford
6a and 6b with similar energy barriers (Figure S34), consistent
with the observed coexistence of both products. An alternative
water-free pathway was also identified, which resembles the
hydrated case, except that Glu99 directly attacks the carbonyl
carbon to promote ring opening and stabilizes the acylium
cation via TS2a (ΔG‡ = 14.6 kcal·mol−1) (Figure S33).
Although this pathway has a relatively higher energy barrier, it
is still considered feasible in the enzymatic environment, as the
hydrophobic active pocket of PesF may restrict water
accessibility. We also considered the possibility of the off-
enzyme cyclization of IC4e. Computations indicate that,
although hydrolytic ring opening and decarboxylation generate
the C7-enol intermediate, the subsequent nonenzymatic
cyclization lacks a stable transition state and is therefore
unlikely to occur (Figure S35). Computational analysis further
ruled out Int-1 as the substrate of PesF and precluded Int-2
ring-opening prior to H-transfer due to prohibitive energy
barriers and intermediate instability (Figure S33).
Further mechanistic insights were obtained through site-
directed mutagenesis. Alanine substitutions at hydrogen-
bonding residues (Glu45, Gln79, Arg138) and hydrophobic
residues (e.g., Pro68, Pro81, Val143) retained activity (Figures
3B and S36), supporting a distributed base-catalysis model
with cooperative residue positioninganalogous to SpnF-
catalyzed Diels−Alder reactions.36 Notably, Glu99 mutations
(E99A and E99Q) abolished activity completely (Figure 3C),
highlighting the indispensable catalytic role of Glu99 as a
defined general acid. Interestingly, the E99D mutant remained
inactive despite retaining acidity (Figure 3C, v), suggesting
that the side-chain length is essential for C9 protonation. DFT
calculations indicated that following skipped diene formation
Glu99 reorients to facilitate ring opening and stabilize the key
intermediate (Figures 3A and S33). This bifunctional role
requires strategic positioning within the hydrophobic cavity to
access both the nonpolar substrate moiety and the electrophilic

Our study delineates a general biosynthesis pathway for δ-
substituted γ-alkylidenebutenolides. A novel δ- to γ-lactone
contraction constitutes the core transformation in this process,
facilitated by the unique atom-deleting function of the
DUF3237 enzyme. We demonstrate that this enzymatic
function is predicated on a noncanonical acid−base mecha-
nism, primarily achieved through the catalytic activity of the
acidic residue Glu99. Furthermore, the pathway employs a
laccase that unexpectedly generates a pentadienyl radical to
enable intramolecular C−C coupling, thereby expanding the
known catalytic repertoire of laccases.
