Aspcandine: A Pyrrolobenzazepine Alkaloid Synthesized by a
Fungal Nonribosomal Peptide Synthetase-Polyketide Synthase
Hybrid

ABSTRACT: Characterization of an orphan biosynthetic gene
cluster found in the fungus Aspergillus candidus CBS 102.13
resulted in the discovery of a pyrrolobenzazepine alkaloid,
aspcandine (1). The unique molecular scaﬀold of 1 is synthesized
by the nonribosomal peptide synthetase-polyketide synthase
hybrid AcdB, which unusually incorporates 3-hydroxy-L-kynur-
enine as a building block. AcdB subsequently performs one round
of chain elongation using malonyl-CoA, which is followed by the
chain release to furnish the tricyclic system of 1.
P
olyketide synthases (PKSs) and nonribosomal peptide
synthetases (NRPSs) are two major enzymes responsible
for the construction of the backbone structure of natural
products.1 In some biosynthetic processes, PKS and NRPS
work in a collaborative manner to yield a polyketide-peptide
(or amino acid) fusion,2 contributing to the structural
complexiﬁcation and diversiﬁcation of natural products. In
fungi, the majority of polyketide-peptide hybrids are generated
by PKS-NRPS hybrid enzymes, which consist of N-terminal
highly reducing (HR)-PKS and C-terminal single module
NRPS regions.3 In contrast to the wide occurrence of PKS-
NRPSs in fungi, NRPS-PKS hybrids are relatively rare in the
fungal secondary metabolism, and only a few fungal NRPS-
PKSs have been identiﬁed and characterized to date.
Representative examples include SwnK, AnATPKS, HispS,
and HppS, which are responsible for the biosynthesis of
swainsonine, pyrophen, hispidin, and 4-hydroxy-6-(4-hydrox-
yphenyl)-α-pyrone, respectively (Figure 1).4 These hybrid
enzymes have the adenylation (A)-thiolation (T) didomain at
the N-terminus, followed by the C-terminal PKS module. The
PKS modules generally consist of the ketosynthase (KS),
acyltransferase (AT), and acyl carrier protein (ACP) domains,
and SwnK additionally possesses ketoreductase (KR) and
reductase (R) domains. Although NRPS-PKSs are not
abundant in fungi, considering the accumulation of fungal
genome sequence data, NRPS-PKS hybrids for a novel
molecular scaﬀold could be discovered by mining available
fungal genomes.
To obtain an uncharacterized NRPS-PKS for a new
molecular skeleton, we searched for SwnK homologues in
the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI)
database by performing a BLAST search against ∼1500 fungal
reference genomes. Among ∼50 NRPS-PKSs identiﬁed herein,
a protein found in Aspergillus candidus CBS 102.13 with the A-
T-KS-AT-KR-ACP-R domain organization caught our atten-
tion because the gene cluster encoding this NRPS-PKS is
clearly distinct from the already characterized NRPS-PKS

clusters. The gene cluster was hereby designated as the acd
cluster (Figure 2A and Table S1), and the NRPS-PKS was
named AcdB. Interestingly, the acd cluster encodes the
indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO; AcdA) and the kynurenine
3-monooxygenase (KMO; AcdD), which are involved in
tryptophan metabolism.5 Thus, it was predicted that AcdA
and AcdD convert L-tryptophan into 3-hydroxy-L-kynurenine,
which would then be loaded onto the NRPS-PKS AcdB. To
the best of our knowledge, none of the A domains of
characterized NRPSs, including fungal NRPS-PKSs, directly
accept 3-hydroxy-L-kynurenine, although
L-kynurenine is
known to be incorporated into the NRPS assembly lines for
some fungal metabolites, such as terreazepine and nanangele-
nin A (Figure S1).6 Notably, these kynurenine-incorporating
metabolites feature a unique chemical scaﬀold, and therefore,
the metabolite derived from the acd cluster could also possess a
novel molecular architecture.
To obtain the natural product synthesized by the acd cluster,
we expressed the NRPS-PKS gene acdB along with the IDO
and KMO genes acdA and acdD in the fungus Aspergillus
oryzae NSAR1,7 which has been eﬀectively utilized for fungal
biosynthetic and genome mining studies.8 All three genes are
expressed under the control of the amyB promoter,9 and the A.
oryzae transformant was cultivated on a culture medium
containing dextrin to induce the gene expression. The HPLC
analysis of the metabolites derived from the A. oryzae
transformant expressing the three genes revealed the presence
of a new major peak 1, which is not present in the host strain
(Figure 2B, traces i and ii). We also noted that 1 exists in A.
candidus CBS 102.13 (Figure S2), which demonstrates that the
acd cluster is actively transcribed in the fungus and indicates
that 1 is indeed the product of the acd cluster. Compound 1
was isolated from large-scale cultivation of the A. oryzae
transformant and subjected to HR-MS and NMR analysis for
structural determination.
The molecular formula of 1 was established as C12H10N2O3
by HR-MS analysis, indicating nine degrees of unsaturation.
Accordingly, the 13C{1H} NMR spectrum of 1 revealed the
presence of 12 signals, which consist of one methylene, ﬁve
methines, including one oleﬁnic (C-3) and three aromatic (C-
6, C-7, and C-8) methines, four nonprotonated sp2 carbons
(C-3a, C-4a, C-5, C-8a), of which C-5 is oxygenated, one
amide (C-2), and one ketone (C-9). Furthermore, interpreta-
tion of the 1H and 1H−15N HMBC spectra conﬁrmed the
existence of two nitrogen-bound protons: one amide (1-NH:
δH 7.22) and one amino (4-NH: δH 9.13) hydrogen atoms.
The planar structure of 1 was determined by the analysis of the
2D NMR spectra (Figures 2C,D). First, the 1H−1H COSY
spectrum revealed the spin systems of 1-NH/H-10a/H-10 and
H-6/H-7/H-8. The HMBC correlations of H-6 (δH 7.04) and
H-7 (δH 6.86) to C-5, H-7 to C-8a, and H-6 and H-8 (δH 7.25)
to C-4a established the connections of C-4a to C-5 and C-8a,
C-5 to C-6, and C-8 to C-8a, illuminating the presence of the
trisubstituted benzene ring. Additional HMBC correlations of
4-NH to C-5, C-8a, and C-10a, H-8 to C-9, and H2-10 (δH
3.02/2.84) to C-3a, C-8a, C-9, and C-10a elucidated the
connections of C-3a to C-10a, N-4 to C-3a and C-4a, and C-9
to C-8a and C-10, which unveiled the presence of the
tetrahydroazepinone moiety fused to the benzene ring at the
C-4a and C-8a positions. Finally, the connections of N-1 to C-
2 and C-3 to C-2 and C-3a were established based on the
HMBC correlations of 1-NH to C-2, C-3, C-3a, and C-10a and
H-3 (δH 5.12) to C-2, C-3a, and C-10a. Thus, 1 features an
unprecedented pyrrolobenzazepine scaﬀold (Figure 2C), and 1
was hereby named aspcandine. Considering that 1 should be
derived from 3-hydroxy-L-kynurenine, it can be deduced that 1
possesses the 10aS conﬁguration as depicted in Figure 2C. To
conﬁrm the absolute conﬁguration of 1, the TDDFT
calculation of the electron circular dichroism (ECD) spectra
of (10aS)- and (10aR)-1 was performed at Cam-B3LYP/6-
31+G(2d,p) level of theory with PCM in methanol. The
calculated ECD spectrum of the (10aS)-enantiomer matched
well with the experimental counterpart (Figure 2E), thus
establishing the absolute conﬁguration of 1 as 10aS.
The structure of 1 suggested that the two reductive domains,
KR and R domains, of AcdB are not engaged in the
biosynthesis of 1. Since many fungal PKS-NRPS hybrids
harbor a nonredox R domain (known as R* domain)
responsible for the Dieckmann condensation and chain
release,10 it was suspected that the R domain of AcdB might

also have a similar role in the chain release and the
construction of the fused ring system. To investigate the
importance of the R domain, we expressed a truncated variant
of AcdB lacking the R domain [AcdB (ΔR); Figure S3], along
with AcdA and AcdD, in A. oryzae. As a result, the A. oryzae
transformant expressing AcdB (ΔR) still produced 1 (Figure
2B, trace iii), revealing that the R domain is not involved in the
formation of 1. We also created another AcdB variant in which
both KR and R domains are removed [AcdB (ΔKR, ΔR);
Figure S3], and its activity was assessed in the same manner as
that for AcdB (ΔR). This variant still produced 1 (Figure 2B,
trace iv), altogether illuminating that both KR and R domains
are not required for the formation of 1.
On the basis of the experimental results, the biosynthetic
route to aspcandine (1) can be proposed as follows (Figure 3).
Initially, the IDO AcdA accepts L-tryptophan and performs the
oxidative opening of the indole ring to yield N′-formyl-L-
kynurenine, which undergoes the spontaneous deformylation
reaction to provide
L-kynurenine. The KMO AcdD then
hydroxylates L-kynurenine to aﬀord 3-hydroxy-L-kynurenine,
which is activated by the A domain of the NRPS-PKS AcdB
and subsequently loaded onto the enzyme. The KS domain
conducts the decarboxylative condensation of the 3-hydroxy-L-
kynurenyl and malonyl moieties. Subsequently, nucleophilic
attacks by the two amino groups would occur nonenzymati-
cally at two distinct positions, achieving the chain release and
the construction of the tricyclic system. Finally, the
dehydration reaction completes the biosynthesis to yield 1.
Somewhat similar spontaneous ring closure and subsequent
dehydration are proposed for the tricyclic quinolone-γ-lactam
formation in the quinolactacin biosynthesis; however, unlike
AcdB, QulA, the key NRPS for the biosynthesis, possesses an
R* domain for Dieckmann condensation, which is responsible
for the construction of one of the rings in the tricyclic system
(Figure S4).11 It should be noted that the KR and R domains
of AcdB apparently have no catalytic roles in the formation of
1, as evident by the fact that the deletion of these domains had
no eﬀect on the formation of 1. The sequence comparison of
the KR domain with those of other fungal megasynthases
illuminated that the catalytic tyrosine residue12 is substituted
with aspartic acid in AcdB (Figure S5A). Likewise, one of the
glycine residues in the NAD(P)H binding site motif
(TGXXGXXG motif)13 of the R domain is replaced with
glutamic acid in AcdB (Figure S5B). Thus, these mutations
might have led to the functional losses of the KR and R
domains of AcdB. Interestingly, KR and R domains have been
only found in SwnK among characterized fungal NRPS-PKS
hybrids, and unlike AcdB, both domains are functional in
SwnK.4a,b Furthermore, the other fungal NRPS-PKSs with the
KS-AT-ACP domain organization all synthesize a pyrone
product,4c−e which is clearly distinct from the product of the
AcdB-catalyzed reaction. Thus, AcdB could be classiﬁed as a
new type of fungal NRPS-PKS. Notably, the BLAST search
using the sequence of AcdB as a query in the NCBI database
revealed that no close homologue of AcdB can be found in the
database (as of June 1, 2022). Although AcdB homologues
with a moderate similarity (<60% protein sequence identity)
are present, none of the genes encoding these proteins are
clustered with the 3-hydroxy-L-kynurenine biosynthetic genes.
Therefore, the acd cluster is apparently highly unique to the A.
candidus strain.
In conclusion, in this study, we obtained a novel fungal
natural product, aspcandine (1), which harbors an unprece-
dented pyrrolobenzazepine scaﬀold, by heterologously express-
ing an orphan fungal NRPS-PKS gene cluster. The biosynthesis
of 1, to the best of our knowledge, provides the ﬁrst example in
which 3-hydroxy-L-kynurenine is used as a building block of an
NRPS enzyme and thus expands the chemical diversity of
fungal metabolites derived from NRPS-PKS biosynthetic
machinery. Given that only a limited number of fungal
NRPS-PKSs have been characterized to date, genome mining
works targeting unexploited fungal NRPS-PKS gene clusters
would be a promising approach to discovering natural products
with a novel molecular skeleton.
