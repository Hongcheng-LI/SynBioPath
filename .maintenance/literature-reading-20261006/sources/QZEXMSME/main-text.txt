A Carbamoyl N‑Methyltransferase Catalyzes N‑Methylation of the
Primary Amide in Ansacarbamitocin Biosynthesis
Zhongyue Li, Wanting Yang, Zhonglin Sun, Haoxin Wang, Chunhua Lu, Deyu Zhu, and Yuemao Shen*

ABSTRACT: Primary amide-specific N-methyltransferases are extremely scarce in microbial secondary metabolism. Here, Asc-Orf2,
an N-methyltransferase involved in the biosynthesis of ansacarbamitocins, was identified to catalyze the methylation of the 3-O-
carbamoyl moiety. Structural analysis identified an unprecedented NPPH catalytic motif, offering a mechanistic basis to overcome
the chemical inertness of primary amides. The 3-O-(N-methyl)-carbamoyl maytansinoid derivatives, modified via Asc-Orf2-catalyzed
methylation, exhibited markedly enhanced antitumor activity, highlighting the magic methylation effect in bioactivity modulation.
Furthermore, structure-targeted engineering expanded the catalytic scope of Asc-Orf2, enabling the directed synthesis of an N-
allylated carbamoyl maytansinoid derivative optimized for antibody-drug conjugate payload.
A
mide N-methylation plays a crucial role in ensuring the
structural stability and bioactivity of natural products.1
However, the inherent planarity and low chemical reactivity of
amide nitrogen present significant challenges for site-specific
methylation, particularly in complex natural products.2,3 While
amide N-methyltransferases have been increasingly identified
in the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, the majority
catalyze N-methylation of lactams rather than primary
amides.4,5 Ansacarbamitocins, a unique class of maytansinoids,
feature an N-methylated 3-O-carbamoyl group, yet the
biocatalyst responsible for this methylation remains elusive
(Figure 1A).6
Compared to ansacarbamitocins, other members of the
maytansinoid family, maytansine and ansamitocins, are well-
known antitumor agents and precursors for antibody-drug
conjugates (ADCs).7,8 Maytansinoids competitively bind to
the β-tubulin maytansine site to suppress microtubule
assembly, with their 3-O-acyl side chains serving as critical
structural determinants. In contrast to the uncharacterized
biosynthetic origin of maytansine and the Asm19-mediated 3-
O-fatty acylation in ansamitocins, the 3-O-(N-methyl)-
carbamoyl substitution in ansacarbamitocins was proposed to
be assembled through a stepwise enzymatic sequence.9 Our
previous studies demonstrated that the carbamoyl moiety was
established by the carbamoyltransferase Asc21b,10 the nearly
abolished cytotoxicity of unmethylated intermediates under-
scored the necessity of a subsequent N-methyltransferase for
bioactivity.11 The structural characteristics of ansacarbamito-
cins suggested that this N-methyltransferase specifically
methylated the 3-O-carbamoyl moiety rather than the 7,9-
carbamoyl or 4’-O-carbamoyl groups.11
To identify the carbamoyl N-methyltransferase gene, we
constructed a genomic fosmid library of Amycolatopsis alba
DSM 44262 and reassembled the asc gene cluster (GenBank
PQ786037) responsible for ansacarbamitocin biosynthesis,
which corrected the misassemblies presented in the original
version (Figures 1B and S1).10 Ansacarbamitocins share the
same macrolactam scaffold as ansamitocins encoded by the
asm biosynthetic cluster, but differ in their post-PKS
modifications.12 Comparison of the putative methyltransferases
encoded by the asc and asm biosynthetic clusters revealed a
divergent enzyme, Asc-Orf2, probably involved in ansacarba-
mitocin biosynthesis.
Asc-Orf2 exhibits minimal homology not only to the amide
N-methyltransferase Asm10 in ansamitocin biosynthesis, but
also to the only known carbamoyl N-methyltransferase PsmA
(Table S4).13,14 Phylogenetic analysis revealed that Asc-Orf2
clustered within the same evolutionary lineage as AerE, an
enzyme responsible for the N-methylation of D-asparagine in
aeronamide A (Figure 1C),15 and both belonged to the Pfam
family PF05175, whose characterized members predominantly
functioned in post-translational modifications of primary
metabolism rather than secondary metabolism (Figure S2).
To investigate the function of Asc-Orf2, the A. alba DSM
44262Δasc-orf 2 mutant strain was constructed. The deletion
of asc-orf2 abolished the production of the original major
metabolites 1 and 2, and resulted in the accumulation of 3 and
4 that lacked 3-O-carbamoyl N-methylation (Figures 2A and
S3). However, in vitro enzymatic assays revealed that 3 and 4
could not be methylated by Asc-Orf2 (Figure S4). Compound
5, isolated from an A. pretiosum mutant (Figure 2B legend),
could be efficiently methylated by Asc-Orf2 to produce 6,
while the presence of a 20-O-methyl group in 7 did not alter
the catalytic activity (Figures 2B and S5). In contrast, 9, which
carries an additional 4,5-epoxide modification, exhibited only
weak methylation by Asc-Orf2. This substrate selectivity of

Asc-Orf2 suggested that the carbamoyl N-methylation
occurred after the 3-O-carbamoylation and preceded the
lactam N-glycosylation and the 4,5-epoxidation. The kinetic
parameters of Asc-Orf2 were determined using 5, showing a
Km value of 10 ± 1 μM and a kcat value of 2.5 ± 0.1 min−1
(Figures 2C and S6). The stringent site-selectivity and
substrate specificity of Asc-Orf2 precluded its application
toward other carbamoyl-containing natural products, such as
geldanamycins and novobiocins (Figure S7), which demanded
detailed structural characterization to enable future engineering
efforts aimed at expanding substrate specificity.
The X-ray crystal structure of Asc-Orf2 in complex with S-
adenosyl homocysteine (SAH) was resolved at 2.3 Å resolution
(Figures 3A and S8, Table S5). Asc-Orf2 adopted the
canonical Rossmann fold characteristic of class I SAM-
dependent methyltransferases, with the SAH binding site
formed by 48CCGPA52 loop other than the conserved GxGxG
motif (Figure S9A).21,22 Unlike homologous enzymes includ-
ing PrmC and AerE, Asc-Orf2 lacks the N-terminal
subdomains that are typically present and essential for
substrate recognition (Figure S9B). To gain deep catalytic
insights into Asc-Orf2, cocrystallization and soaking experi-
ments were performed with substrate 5 under various
conditions, whereas the electron density of 5 was fragmented
and exhibited low occupancy (Figure S10A). Guided by the
partial electron density, molecular docking analysis revealed
that the carbamoyl group of 5 was positioned in close
proximity to the methyl donor site of SAM, with the substrate
backbone inserted into a hydrophobic pocket (Figures 3B and
S10B). The docked conformation of 5 aligned well with the
residual electron density observed in the cocrystal structure
(Figure S10C). Molecular dynamics simulations coupled with
MM/PBSA energy calculations revealed that the amino acid
residues L136, Y142, F133, M120, and F205 bound to the ansa
chain of 5 through hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen
bonding (Figure S11). This binding geometry and the
hydrophobicity of the ansa chain collectively accounted for
the inability of Asc-Orf2 to methylate other carbamoyl groups
in ansacarbamitocins.
The primary amide nitrogen of the carbamoyl group was
positioned 2.5 Å from the methyl carbon of SAM, optimally
aligned for an SN2 nucleophilic attack (Figure 3B). Distinct
from other members of the PF05175 methyltransferase family,
Asc-Orf2 harbored a unique 116NPPH119 catalytic motif in
place of the conserved NPPF(Y) sequence, suggesting a
potentially novel catalytic paradigm (Figure 3C).15,23 Unlike
AerE, Asc-Orf2 appeared to function independently of metal
ions, as supported by EDTA chelation assays and ICP-MS
measurements (Figure S12). Replacement of H119 resulted in
a complete loss of catalytic activity (Figures 3D and S13),
underscoring this basic residue as the key catalytic base.
Structural analysis, together with the reduced catalytic
efficiency observed for the N116D and N116A variants,
suggested that N116 acted as a supporting residue that
facilitated the function of H119 (Figure 3E). The backbone
carbonyl of P117 formed a hydrogen bond with the 3-O-
carbamoyl moiety, anchoring the substrate within the active
site. The side chain carbonyl of N116 engaged in hydrogen
bonding with the Nε2-H of H119, stabilizing its deprotonated
Nδ1 form and thereby facilitating efficient proton abstraction
from the primary amide (Figure 3F).

Cytotoxicity assays against various tumor cell lines revealed
that 8 exhibited the most potent activity, particularly against
the human prostate cancer LNCaP cells (IC50: 0.37 nM, Table
1). Structure−activity relationship analysis highlighted the
critical role of carbamoyl N-methylation catalyzed by Asc-Orf2,
as 8 displayed IC50 values 1−2 orders of magnitude lower than
that of 7. In contrast, 4,5-epoxidation diminished antitumor
activity, with 8 showing 10-fold higher potency than its
epoxide counterpart 9, while the lactam amide N-methylation
had minimal impact on cytotoxicity, as evidenced by the
comparable bioactivity of 10 and 11. This established that the
conserved 4,5-epoxide and lactam N-methylation in classical
maytansinoids conferred no activity enhancement to 3-O-(N-
methyl)-carbamoyl derivatives. Although 8 exhibited lower
potency than ansamitocin P-3 (AP-3), Asc-Orf2-catalyzed N-
methylation offered enzymatic plasticity for further engineering
in ADC design, circumventing the stringent sequence of the
post-PKS modifications incurred by Asm19-catalyzed fatty
acylation in ansamitocins.
Consistent with the binding modes of maytansine and AP-3
at the β-tubulin site, molecular docking demonstrated that 8
adopted a similar binding conformation, with the methylation
of the 3-O-carbamoyl group enhancing side-chain hydro-
phobicity and maintaining potent antimicrotubular activity
(Figures 5A and S14).9,25 As an expandable enzymatic

Using the catalytic compatibility of Asc-Orf2 with the allyl
donor S-adenosyl (S)-2-amino-4-(allylthio)butanoic acid
(SAAll), compound 7, which originally reacted with SAM to
yield the highly potent derivative 8, was instead converted into
13 with a 3-O-(N-allyl)-carbamoyl substitution (Figure 5B).
The suboptimal conversion rate reflected the restricted
catalytic adaptability of Asc-Orf2 toward non-native alkyl
donors (Figure S15). Structural analysis of the Asc-orf2
uncovered the binding region for potential alkyl donors was
enclosed by the
116NPPH119,
18LDGG21 and
139ELVF142
segments (Figure 5C). Upon SAM binding to the cofactor-
binding cavity, the V141 side chain forms a 3.5 Å steric gate
with its methyl group, restricting access to donors larger than
the van der Waals radii. Therefore, the V141G mutation of
Asc-Orf2 alleviated steric hindrance, leading to a 20-fold
increase in allyl transfer efficiency compared to that of the
wild-type enzyme (Figures 5D and S15). The engineered 3-O-
(N-allyl)-carbamoyl derivative 13, as a potential ADC payload,
offered the possibility of direct conjugation with ADC linkers
through thiol−ene click chemistry or Heck coupling reaction.
In summary, this study identified a unique carbamoyl N-
methyltransferase involved in ansacarbamitocin biosynthesis,
and elucidated the mechanism of primary amide N-
methylation that increased the antitumor activity of carbamoyl
N-methylated products remarkably. The catalytic compatibility
of Asc-Orf2 enabled N-allylation of the 3-O-carbamoyl moiety,
providing a potential conjugatable handle for novel maytansi-
noid ADC payload. These findings offered valuable insights
into the understanding and functional expansion of novel
amide N-methyltransferases.
