ABSTRACT: α-Tertiary amino acids (ATAAs) are versatile building blocks for the
synthesis of biologically active compounds. Although synthetic and enzymatic
approaches to ATAA synthesis have been developed, additional methods for ATAA
production remain in high demand. Here, we report detailed mechanistic and
structural analyses of TqaM, a non-heme iron-dependent oxygenase that catalyzes the
key oxidative decarboxylation of a β-amino acid to generate 2-aminoisobutyric acid, a
representative ATAA. In vitro analyses revealed that TqaM strictly recognizes the stereochemistry at the C2 position of the substrate
and initiates the reaction by abstracting a C2-hydrogen atom with one equivalent of molecular oxygen. Structural analysis and site-
directed mutagenesis suggested that an active site histidine residue functions as an acid/base catalyst and the N-terminal loop of the
enzyme plays a critical role in substrate selectivity. Finally, we demonstrate TqaM’s remarkable substrate promiscuity toward α-
hydroxy-β-amino acids, enabling the efficient synthesis of diverse ATAAs with potential biocatalytic applications.
■INTRODUCTION
α-Tertiary amino acids (ATAAs) are non-proteinogenic amino
acids characterized by two alkyl substituents on the α-carbon.
ATAAs are widely observed as crucial structural motifs in natural
products, pharmaceuticals, agrochemicals, and organocatalysts
(Figure S1).1−3 The incorporation of ATAAs into molecules in
place of natural amino acids induces significant changes in
solubility, lipophilicity, pharmacokinetics, and physiological
properties.4 Due to these features, ATAAs are used as versatile
building blocks for the design and synthesis of biologically active
compounds. However, the asymmetric construction of quater-
nary stereocenters in ATAAs remains challenging.5 The inherent
steric hindrance typically necessitates functional group
protection and multistep synthetic routes; therefore, various
strategies have been developed.6,7 For example, light-driven
radical-coupling reactions, transition-metal catalysis, and photo-
induced metal-free cross-dehydrogenative coupling reactions
have been reported for the synthesis of ATAAs.8−15 Recently,
the facile synthesis of unnatural amino acids using enzymes has
also been extensively explored.16,17 These biocatalytic methods
are highly advantageous, as they utilize the chiral environment of
enzymes’ active sites to achieve high stereoselectivity while
operating under protecting group-free conditions. As a result,
these studies have achieved the unprotected synthesis of many
non-proteinogenic amino acids, including ATAAs, using
pyridoxal 5′-phosphate (PLP)-dependent enzymes such as
tryptophan synthases,18−20 threonine aldolases,21−24 and
threonine transaldolases.24−26 Furthermore, very recently,
non-heme iron enzymes have also been repurposed for the
synthesis of ATAAs via 1,3-nitrogen migration reactions.27
Despite these advances, additional methods for ATAA synthesis
are still very much in demand.
2-Aminoisobutyric acid (AIB, 1) and L-isovaline (L-2) are
ATAAs found in fungal natural products.28,29 In their biosyn-
thesis, three enzymes are involved in the formation of a tertiary
amino group from the proteogenic amino acids L-valine and L-
isoleucine, respectively (Figure 1A).30−32 First, the non-heme
iron and 2-oxoglutarate-dependent (Fe/2OG) oxygenase,
TqaL, catalyzes the formation of pleurocybellaziridine and
(2S,3R)-3-ethyl-3-methylaziridine-2-carboxylic acid from L-va-
line and L-isoleucine, respectively, via C−N bond formation.
Subsequently, a haloalkanoic acid dehalogenase-like enzyme,
TqaF, catalyzes the hydrolysis reaction to open the aziridine
ring, producing α-hydroxy-β-amino intermediates ((R)-3 and
(2R,3S)-4). Finally, oxidative decarboxylation by the non-heme
iron oxygenase TqaM generates 1 and L-2, respectively. We
hypothesized that repurposing the TqaL/F/M sequence could
be an alternative strategy for the synthesis of ATAAs. Therefore,
in this study, we investigated the catalytic properties,
mechanism, and substrate scope of TqaM as the first step
toward this goal.

and novobiocin, respectively,33−35 as well as in reactions

well as precise in vitro investigations of its reaction mechanisms,
have not been conducted. Therefore, the specific enzymatic
mechanism of TqaM and the mechanistic differences between
TqaM and other enzymes remain unclear. Here, we report
mechanistic and structure−function analyses of TqaM and the
enzymatic synthesis of non-proteinogenic amino acids, includ-
ing ATAAs, by TqaM. Enzymatic reactions with substrate
analogs with different stereochemistries at the C2 position,
kinetic isotope effect (KIE) measurements, and stoichiometric
analyses of oxygen in the reaction provided detailed insights into
the catalytic mechanism of TqaM. Structural analysis and site-
directed mutagenesis suggested that a histidine residue in TqaM
plays a key role as an acid/base catalyst in the reaction
mechanism. Additionally, the truncation of the N-terminal loop
of the enzyme considerably expanded the substrate scope.
Finally, we demonstrate the substrate promiscuity of TqaM
toward α-hydroxy-β-amino acids, laying the foundation for the
future use of TqaL/F/M in ATAA synthesis.
■RESULTS
In Vitro Mechanistic Analysis of TqaM. To investigate
how TqaM catalyzes the conversions of (R)-3 and (2R,3S)-4
into 1 and L-2, we conducted in vitro mechanistic analyses. First,
to examine the stereoselectivity at the C2 position, (S)- and (R)-
3 were synthesized and tested using TaTqaM, a homologue
from Trichoderma atroviride (Figure 2). The enzymatic reaction
products were analyzed using LC-MS. In comparison with
authentic standard 1, only the reaction using (R)-3 showed
product formation (Figure 2B). Notably, the enzymatic reaction
using the α-amino-β-hydroxy amino acid 5 did not generate any
product. These results indicated that the enzyme strictly
recognizes both the stereochemistry and substituents at the
C2 position to catalyze the oxidative decarboxylation reaction.
Furthermore, to investigate whether C2-hydrogen abstraction is
a partially rate-limiting step during the reaction cycle, substrate
isotopologue 2-D-(R)-3 was synthesized. Compared with 2-H-
(R)-3, slower rates of 1 formation and (R)-3 consumption were
observed. The comparison between regular and deuterated
substrates indicated isotope effects of approximately 1.4 and 1.5,
respectively. (Figure S3). These observations indicated that the
chirality at the C2 position is critical for the enzyme reaction,
and the hydrogen atom abstraction is at least partially rate-
limiting.
To further investigate the stereoselectivity at the C3-position,
(2R,3S)-3-amino-2-hydroxy-3-methylpentanoic acid ((2R,3S)-
4) and (2R,3R)-4 were synthesized and subjected to enzymatic
reactions. Both compounds were recognized as substrates to

Structural Analyses of TqaM Homologues. To clarify
the molecular basis for the TqaM-catalyzed oxidative decarbox-
ylation reaction, the X-ray crystal structures of TaTqaM bound
with Fe2+ and in complex with Fe2+ and 1 were solved at 2.60 and
2.15 Å, respectively, and the structure of the N-terminal 26
residue-truncated TqaM from Penicillium aethiopicum (PaT-
qaM-Δ26) in complex with Mn2+ and substrate (R)-3 was also
solved at 2.17 Å (Table S1 and Figure S6). Since previous
studies on metal ion dependency demonstrated that the TqaM
catalyzed reaction is strictly Fe2+-dependent, Mn2+ was used in
the PaTqaM−substrate complex to prevent catalytic turnover
during crystallization. A comparison between the PaTqaM and
TaTqaM structures (70% sequence identity) revealed that the
overall structure is well conserved with an RMSD of 1.1 Å for
283 Cα atoms (Figure S7). The overall structure of TqaM is an
α/β-sandwich fold, closely resembling the class II zinc-
dependent fuculose-1-phosphate aldolases FucAs from Glacio-
zyma antarctica PI12 (PDB ID: 4XXF)38 and Escherichia coli
(PDB ID: 2FUA)39 (Figure S7D and E).
TqaMs form functional homotetramers, and the monomers
are highly homologous to each other (RMSD of 0.3 Å for 278
Cα atoms), which is consistent with the solution state (Figures
S8 and S9). The N-terminal loops are located at the active site
entrances of PaTqaM-Δ26 and TaTqaM and adopt similar
conformations, suggesting that they cap the active site, and the
26 residues of the N-terminal loop of PaTqaM are not essential
for substrate accommodation, as TaTqaM is naturally truncated
up to 21 residues (Figure S10). Indeed, with (R)-3, the enzyme
activities of PaTqaM-Δ20, Δ23, and Δ26 are comparable to that
of full-length PaTqaM (Figures S11 and S12 and Table S2).
Comparisons of the apo structures of TaTqaM, (R)-3-bound
TqaM-Δ26, and 1-bound TaTqaM revealed structural differ-
ences in the loop region between Ser178 and Ala187 in
PaTqaM-Δ26 (Asp158-Ala167 in TaTqaM) (Figure 3). While
these loops are positioned near the metal-binding site and play a
role in closing the active site in the structures of (R)-3-bound
TqaM-Δ26 and 1-bound TaTqaM, the corresponding loops in
the ligand-free monomers of TqaM-Δ26 and TaTqaM-apo are
either missing or located approximately 8 Å away on the
opposite side of the metal-binding site (Figure 3A−C). These
observations suggested that the conformational change in the
loop facilitates substrate accommodation, prevents the release of
intermediates and undesired water-mediated reactions, and
promotes product release after the oxidative decarboxylation
reaction.
The active site is located at the interface between each
monomer, where metal ions are coordinated by three catalytic
His residues (Figure 4). The C2-hydroxy and C1-carboxy
groups of (R)-3 coordinate to the metal ion (Figure 4). A water
molecule is positioned opposite His147, which is expected to be
replaced by O2 during catalysis to initiate the enzyme reaction.
(R)-3 forms hydrogen bonds with active site residues to fix its
conformation in the active site. The C1-carboxy group interacts
with His74, Asn123, and the main chain amide of Gly73, while
the C2-hydroxy group forms a hydrogen bond with Gln166. The

and with Gln166 and Asp167. The C2-hydrogen atom is
oriented on the same side as the water molecule coordinated to
the metal ion, suggesting that C2-hydrogen atom abstraction by
a superoxide radical initiates the reaction (Figure 4A). This
conformation explains why the enzyme cannot accept (S)-3 as a
substrate. In the (S)-3 binding mode, the C2-hydrogen atom
should be positioned opposite the metal-bound water molecule,
which prevents superoxide-mediated hydrogen abstraction and
thus blocks the initiation of the reaction. To evaluate the
importance of these interactions, alanine scanning mutagenesis
was performed on the active site residues of PaTqaM, including
Ser72, His74, Asn123, Gln166, Asp167, Trp271, and Glu275. As
a result, all variants except N123A and W271A exhibited
drastically reduced activities (less than 25% of those of the wild
type), indicating the critical roles of these interactions in
substrate recognition (Figure 4C and Figure S13).
In the TqaM reaction, it is hypothesized that the hydro-
peroxide in intermediate II is deprotonated to form Fe−O−O−
and the oxygen atom proximal to the Fe in intermediate IV is
protonated during the cleavage of the O−O bond (Figure 1B).30
In the structure of PaTqaM in complex with (R)-3, the distance
between the water molecule coordinated to the metal center and
His74 is 3.3 Å, suggesting that His74 is close enough to the O−
O bond to facilitate deprotonation and protonation during its
cleavage (Figure 4A). Indeed, the H74A variant significantly
reduced the activity to 12% of that of the wild type, suggesting
that His74 would play a crucial role in the deprotonation/
protonation process (Figure 4C).
Product 1 binds in a manner similar to that of (R)-3, while
only the C1-carboxy group coordinates to the metal center on
the opposite site of His190 (His210 in PaTqaM) (Figure 4B).
The C1-carboxy group and C2-amino groups interact with
His54 and Asn103, respectively. These fewer interactions with
the product and the open conformation of the loop between
Asp158−Ala167 are potential driving forces for product release
from the active site.
Role of the N-Terminal Region in the Substrate
Promiscuity of TqaM. The active site of the substrate-binding
structure of PaTqaM has sufficient space to accommodate
substrates with larger side chains, indicating the potential
promiscuity of the enzyme (Figure S14). To investigate the
substrate scope of TqaM toward side chain variations of 2-
hydroxy acids, we first tested the enzymatic reactions of full-
length, Δ20, Δ23, and Δ26 PaTqaM with (R)-3-phenyllactic
acid ((R)-6) and (R)-2-hydroxy-4-phenylbutyric acid ((R)-7)
(Figure 5). Surprisingly, while all variants showed activities
comparable to those of the full-length enzyme toward (R)-3,
only the PaTqaM-Δ23 variant exhibited high activity toward
(R)-6 to generate 8 and 9. Furthermore, the truncated variants
accepted (R)-7 to produce compound 10, whereas the full-
length enzyme lacked significant activity (Figures 5 and S15).
Therefore, we next performed the enzymatic reaction using
PaTqaM-Δ23 with C3-tertiarily substituted C2-hydroxy com-
pounds 11-15 as substrates (Figure S16 and Figure S17). As a
result, the enzyme accepted all compounds as substrates to
generate compounds 16-20. These results suggested that the N-
terminal region controls the substrate selectivity and PaTqaM-
Δ23 shows substrate promiscuity toward the side chains of C2-
hydroxy acid compounds.
To further understand the function of the N-terminal region
of PaTqaM, we performed steady-state kinetic analyses and
structural studies of the truncated variants. The kinetic data
indicated that truncation of the N-terminal loop did not
significantly affect the reaction toward 3, whereas for (R)-6, only
PaTqaM-Δ23 exhibited measurable activity. Other truncated
variants showed much lower reactivity and did not reach
saturation even at high substrate concentrations, supporting the
interpretation that their reduced reactivity results from poor
substrate binding caused by the structure of the N-terminal
residues. For compound 7, although PaTqaM-Δ23 showed the
highest activity, its KM value exhibited an inverse trend relative
to the reactivity, suggesting that different loop conformations
may alter substrate positioning or product release (Table S2 and
Figure S10).
We also determined the crystal structure of PaTqaM-Δ23 in
complex with (R)-6 and Mn2+ (Table S1 and Figure S6C). The
binding mode of the 2-hydroxy carboxylic acid moiety of (R)-6
is almost identical to that of (R)-3, while the aromatic ring of
(R)-6 is accommodated in the hydrophobic pocket formed by
Leu28, Ile71, Pro125, Ile184, Ala266, and Trp271 (Figure 6).
The conformation of the Ser178-Ala187 loop in the complex
structure with (R)-6 is nearly identical to that observed in the
complex structure with (R)-3 (Figure 3). In contrast, the side
chain of Leu28, which caps the active site and forms a
hydrophobic pocket, is shifted approximately 1 Å outward due
to steric hindrance with the aromatic ring of the substrate
(Figure 6). Therefore, the N-terminal loop likely acts as a lid-like
loop that modulates substrate recognition and promiscuity.
Variations in the length or conformation of this region are
expected to alter the positioning of hydrophobic pocket
residues, leading to disrupted substrate binding and reduced
enzymatic activity.

■DISCUSSION
Oxidative decarboxylation reactions play a crucial role in carbon
excision with carboxyl group retention, and are widely observed
in natural product biosynthesis.40 Our mechanistic analyses of
TqaM with stereospecific substrates revealed that the enzyme
strictly recognizes the stereochemistry at the C2-hydroxy group
while exhibiting broad substrate specificity toward the stereo-
chemistry and substituents at the C3 position. Kinetic analyses
with C2-deuterium-labeled substrates showed a KIE, suggesting
that the C2-hydrogen atom abstraction and subsequent C−H
bond cleavage comprise the partially rate-limiting step. Overall,
these in vitro assays clearly support the previously proposed
reaction mechanism.30 Similar oxidative decarboxylation is also
catalyzed by enzymes such as CloR, NovR, HPPD, and HMS
(Figure S2).33,35−37 Notably, while these enzymes catalyze
oxidative decarboxylation with hydroxylation to α-keto acids,
TqaM does not catalyze hydroxylation. Structural analysis and
site-directed mutagenesis experiments suggested that the active
site His74 residue likely plays a key role in the reaction
selectivity. A BLAST search suggested that the His74 residue is
highly conserved in the TqaM homologues. In contrast, His74 is
replaced by a hydrophobic residue (Leu54) in CloR and NovR
(Figure S19A−C). Similarly, in the HPPD and HMS structures,
no basic residues are located close to the metal center to
deprotonate the hydroperoxyl intermediate and protonate the
peroxide adduct, which leads to the formation of an Fe(IV)O
species to catalyze hydroxylation in these enzymes (Figures
S19D−G and 20).41−43 In the second step of the CloR reaction
from 3-dimethylallyl-4-hydroxymandelic acid to 3-dimethylallyl-
4-hydroxybenzoate (Figure S2), oxidative decarboxylation
proceeds similarly to TqaM. Here, the reason why hydroxylation
cannot occur is probably because the decarboxylated product
lacks a carbon chain for hydroxylation. Instead, benzoic acid is
directly released, and the remaining Fe(IV)O species is likely
quenched by the solvent. Future structural and mechanistic
studies of CloR would provide further insights into the
selectivity of its oxidative decarboxylation reactions.
The N-terminal loop of PaTqaM is not essential for
accommodating the substrate (R)-3, as truncated variants
exhibit comparable activity. However, loop truncation affects
substrate promiscuity. This likely reflects differences in the
interactions between the substrate and active-site residues. For
C3-amino substrates such as (R)-3, hydrogen-bonding networks
involving the amino group would reduce the contribution of the
loop. In contrast, for substrate 6, which lacks the amino group,
binding depends more directly on the loop conformation. Thus,
the N-terminal loop is dispensable for recognition of the natural
substrate but important for accommodating alternative
substrates. The N-terminally truncated PaTqaM variant exhibits
broad substrate specificity, accepting C2-hydroxy acids and β-
amino acids with moderately bulky substituents. Although the
synthetic substrates 11−13, 15, 24−26, and 28−29 were
prepared as mixtures of two diastereomers and two enantiomers,
substrates with a 2R-configuration ((2R,3S) and (2R,3R))
should be accepted by TqaM, based on its stereoselectivity
toward (R)-3 and 25. The enzyme did not accept 3-substituted
β-amino acid 23 with a highly hydrophilic side chain. In the
complex structure of PaTqaM with 3, the active site region
where the dimethyl group is located still has sufficient space to
accommodate substrates with aliphatic or aromatic side chains.
However, this pocket is composed of hydrophobic residues,
including Leu28, Pro125, and Ile184, which likely disfavor
interactions with hydrophilic substrates. Therefore, compound
23 is not accepted by TqaM due to the incompatibility between
the hydrophilic side chain and the hydrophobic nature of the
active site. Future engineering of these hydrophobic active site
residues could alter the substrate specificity, enabling the
development of biocatalysts capable of accepting a broader
range of β-amino acids or achieving a kinetic resolution process
to produce a single product enantiomer from a mixture of
substrate diastereomers. Notably, the catalytic efficiency differed
significantly between 11−15, bearing a C3-hydroxy group, and
24−29, bearing a C3-amino group, despite the otherwise
identical substituents. The C3-amino group forms a hydrogen
bond network with Ser72, Trp271, and Glu275. When the C3
group is substituted with a hydroxy group, it is hypothesized that
the strength and geometry of hydrogen bonding with these
residues are altered, potentially leading to a different binding
mode. Alternatively, the C3-hydroxy group may coordinate with
a metal ion, thereby preventing the substrate from adopting an
active conformation.
To date, the enzymatic synthesis of ATAAs has been reported
using PLP-dependent enzymes and one non-heme iron enzyme,
by leveraging either their substrate promiscuity or catalytic
promiscuity in new-to-nature radical reactions.18−27 In this
study, we set our sights on the TqaL/F/M sequence for the
enzymatic synthesis of ATAAs and focused our efforts on the
catalytic properties and substrate promiscuity of TqaM toward
various α-hydroxy-β-amino acids, which shed light on the facile
and efficient production of diverse ATAAs. Since TqaL/F/M
homologues are widely distributed in nature, further functional
analyses and characterizations of these enzymes may uncover
homologues with different substrate specificities, which could be
utilized as biocatalysts for the enzymatic synthesis of ATAAs.
■CONCLUSION
Our mechanistic and structural analyses of TqaM revealed the
intimate structural details underlying the oxidative decarbox-
ylation reaction to generate 1, via a mechanism distinct from
those of other enzymes that catalyze oxidative decarboxylation
reactions with hydroxylation. Furthermore, we have provided
initial demonstrations on the utility of this non-heme iron
enzyme system for ATAA synthesis. These findings not only
provide insights into the catalytic mechanisms of non-heme
iron-dependent enzymes but also demonstrate their potential as
