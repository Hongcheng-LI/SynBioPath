ABSTRACT: The biosynthetic gene cluster of γ-aminobutyric acid
(GABA)-containing fungal cyclic heptapeptides unguisins A (1) and B
(2) was identified in the fungus Aspergillus violaceofuscus CBS 115571.
In vitro enzymatic reactions and gene deletion experiments revealed that
the unguisin pathway involves the alanine racemase UngC to provide D-
alanine, which is then accepted by the first adenylation domain of the
nonribosomal peptide synthetase (NRPS) UngA. Intriguingly, the
hydrolase UngD was found to transform unguisins into previously
undescribed linear peptides. Subsequently, heterologous production of
these peptides in Aspergillus oryzae was achieved, in which we
established a methodology to readily introduce a large NRPS gene
into the fungal host. Finally, genome mining revealed new unguisin
congeners, each containing a (2R,3R)-β-methylphenylalanine residue.
N
onribosomal peptides (NRPs) are a major class of
microbial secondary metabolites, and filamentous fungi
are prolific producers of diverse NRPs,1,2 as represented by
penicillin antibiotics, the immunosuppressant drug cyclosporin
A, and the antifungal echinocandins. NRP synthetases
(NRPSs) are the central enzymes for NRP biosynthesis and
are generally large multimodular enzymes that often
incorporate nonproteinogenic amino acids as biosynthetic
building blocks.3,4 A typical NRPS module contains
adenylation (A), thiolation (T), and condensation (C)
domains, which are occasionally accompanied by additional
domains, such as epimerization (E) and N-methyltransferase
domains. Notably, many fungal NRPSs utilize a terminal
condensation (CT) domain for macrocyclization and peptide
chain release,5,6 whereas thioesterase (TE) domains are
generally responsible for such reactions in bacterial NRPSs.7
The modular nature of NRPSs has inspired researchers to
rationally engineer NRPS assembly lines to provide important
insights into successful NPRS design, particularly in bacterial
systems.8,9 Nevertheless, there is a long way to go before we
are able to create designer NRPSs with any desired molecular
architecture. To that end, further characterization of NRP
biosynthesis, particularly in less studied fungal biosynthetic
machinery, will contribute to a deeper understanding and
future engineering of NRPSs.
In this study, we identified and characterized the
biosynthetic gene cluster of the fungal cyclic heptapeptides
unguisins A (1) and B (2), which was designated as the ung
cluster. Interestingly, the cyclic peptides 1 and 2 were accepted
by the hydrolase UngD to yield previously undetected linear
peptides. Furthermore, we established an efficient method-
ology to express a large NRPS gene in a fungal host, achieving
the heterologous production of both cyclic and linear peptides.
Finally, we searched gene clusters homologous to the ung
cluster in the publicly available genome database and found
that one of the identified clusters is responsible for the
synthesis of new unguisin analogues, unguisins H (5) and I
(6), both of which contain a (2R,3R)-β-methylphenylalanine
residue.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
During our metabolite analysis of the fungus Aspergillus
violaceofuscus CBS 115571,10 from which we discovered a
polyketide-NRP hybrid pyranoviolin A,11 we found that this
fungus also produces the two peptide natural products
unguisins A (1) and B (2) (Figure 1A and B).12 Compounds
1 and 2 are cyclic heptapeptides that were originally isolated
from Emericella unguis and are the first γ-aminobutyric acid
(GABA)-containing cyclic peptides obtained from nature.12
They also feature five D-amino acid residues. GABA residues

cluster of the octapeptide fusaoctaxin A containing a GABA
residue has recently been identified in Fusarium graminea-

which a GABA unit is found within a cyclic peptide
structure.12,16−19 The GABA residue is believed to provide
conformational flexibility, and 1 reportedly serves as an anion
receptor, which exhibits the highest affinity for phosphate and
pyrophosphate.20 The unique molecular skeleton of 1 has
attracted scientists to achieve its total synthesis;21 however, the
biosynthesis of unguisins has never been investigated despite
their potential to facilitate engineered biosynthesis of GABA-
containing cyclic peptides.
To understand the biosynthetic mechanisms of 1 and 2, we
initially sought to identify the biosynthetic gene cluster of these
peptides in the genome of A. violaceofuscus. Consequently, one
candidate NRPS gene involved in unguisin biosynthesis, which
is colocalized with three additional genes, was discovered, and
the gene cluster was designated as the ung cluster (Figure 1C
and Table S1). The ung cluster encodes the pyridoxal 5′-
phosphate (PLP)-dependent enzyme UngC and the β-
lactamase-like hydrolase UngD, both of which may also be
required for unguisin biosynthesis. The NRPS UngA possesses
the A-T-C-A-T-E-C-A-T-C-A-T-E-C-A-T-E-C-A-T-E-C-A-T-
CT domain organization (Figure 2). This is consistent with
the structures of 1 and 2, except that the first module, which
should generate a
D-alanine residue, lacks an E domain.
Therefore, we reasoned that the A domain of the first NRPS
module directly accepts D-alanine and that an alanine racemase
provides D-alanine, as seen in the biosynthesis of cyclosporins
and HC-toxin.22,23 Given that known alanine racemases are
PLP-dependent enzymes, UngC may serve as an alanine
racemase, although it displays no obvious amino acid sequence
similarity with previously characterized alanine racemases. The
biosynthetic role of the hydrolase UngD remained undeter-
mined at this point.
We then focused on the functional characterization of
UngC, which was expected to be an alanine racemase. To this
end, the enzyme was purified from an Escherichia coli
expression system as an N-terminal His-tagged protein and
was incubated with L-alanine in vitro. The reaction product was
derivatized using 1-fluoro-2,4-dinitrophenyl-5-D-leucinamide
(D-FDLA) and then analyzed by liquid chromatography−
mass spectrometry (LC-MS), which revealed the production of
D-alanine by UngC (Figure 3A, traces i and ii). Similarly, when
D-alanine was incubated with UngC, L-alanine was produced
(Figure 3A, traces iii and iv), demonstrating that UngC is
indeed an alanine racemase. To further understand the
importance of UngC in unguisin biosynthesis, we deleted
ungC from the A. violaceofuscus genome (Figure S1). As
expected, the production of 1 and 2 almost completely ceased
in the gene deletion mutant (Figure 3B, traces i and ii),
whereas unguisin production was restored when D-alanine was

These observations confirmed that UngC is responsible for the
D-alanine supply in unguisin biosynthesis.
Next, we aimed to clarify the function of the hydrolase
UngD, which was apparently not required for unguisin
biosynthesis. To investigate whether UngD is involved in
unguisin biosynthesis, we deleted ungD from the A.
violaceofuscus genome. The mutant strain lacking ungD still
produced 1 and 2 (Figure 3B, trace iv), demonstrating that
UngD is not involved in the biosynthesis of 1 and 2. Thus, we
hypothesized that UngD accepts 1 and/or 2 to yield other
metabolites. To examine this hypothesis, UngD was purified
for in vitro enzymatic reactions. Interestingly, it efficiently
transformed 1 into a new product 3 (Figure 3C, traces i and
ii), the molecular formula of which was established as
C40H56N8O8, corresponding to a hydrolyzed form of 1. To
determine the structure of 3, it was isolated from a large-scale
enzymatic reaction. The NMR and MS/MS analyses revealed
that 3 is a linearized form of 1 in which hydrolysis occurred
between the D-tryptophan and GABA residues (Figures 2 and
S2). Likewise, UngD converted 2 into the corresponding linear
peptide 4 (Figure 2; Figure 3C, traces iii and iv),
demonstrating that UngD is a peptidase that transforms cyclic
unguisins into linear peptides (Figure 2). The structures of 3
and 4 were further confirmed using the advanced Marfey’s
method24 (Figure S3) and comparisons with synthetic peptides
exhibiting the proposed structures of 3 and 4 (Figure S4). As
such linear peptides have never been previously isolated, we
examined the metabolites of A. violaceofuscus, but 3 and 4 were
not present at detectable levels (Figure S5).
To further confirm the proposed biosynthetic pathway
leading to 3 and 4 (Figure 2), we conducted heterologous
expression experiments in A. oryzae NSAR1,25 which is a
robust platform for the heterologous biosynthesis of fungal
natural products.26−29 Due to its large size (∼29.3 kb), it was
difficult to introduce the entire NRPS gene ungA into a
standard fungal transformation vector. In a previous study, a
large fungal NRPS gene was separated into two fragments and
introduced into A. oryzae over two rounds of transformation.30
However, to more efficiently accomplish the heterologous
expression of a large NRPS, we sought to establish a new
method to integrate such a gene in a single transformation
event (Figure S6). To this end, the first and latter halves of
ungA were introduced into two different plasmids, each
containing a different selection marker. The two ungA
fragments had ∼1 kb of overlapped sequence to allow for in
vivo recombination to generate the complete ungA sequence,
which was further integrated onto the chromosome of A.
oryzae using CRISPR-Cas9-mediated homologous recombina-
tion.31 The resultant A. oryzae transformant successfully
produced 1 and 2 when
D-alanine was supplied in the
cultivation medium (Figure 3D, traces i to iii). Subsequently,
another transformant coexpressing ungA and ungC was
constructed, which expectedly formed 1 and 2 (Figure 3D,
trace iv). Further introduction of ungD yielded the linear
peptides 3 and 4 (Figure 3D, trace v), confirming that these
linear peptides can also be produced in vivo.
Thereafter, we investigated the occurrence of gene clusters
similar to the ung cluster by cblaster32 analysis, which revealed
the presence of homologous gene clusters in four other fungal
strains (Figure S7; as of October 22, 2022). Among these
clusters, the cluster from Aspergillus campestris IBT 28561
(CBS 348.81),33 designated as the ung′ cluster, was notable
because it encodes a methyltransferase that is uniquely found
in this cluster (Figure 4A and Table S1). The methyltransfer-
ase UngE′ is homologous to MppJ (56% amino acid sequence
identity) involved in mannopeptimycin biosynthesis.34 MppJ
methylates phenylpyruvate to yield β-methylphenylpyruvate,
which is converted into β-methylphenylalanine (βMePhe) and
subsequently loaded onto the NRPS assembly line. Notably,
some of the unguisin congeners, namely, unguisins E and F
(Figure 4C),17,18 possess a βMePhe residue, which suggests
that the ung′ cluster is responsible for biosynthesizing
βMePhe-containing peptides. Metabolite analysis of A.
campestris CBS 348.81 revealed the presence of two peptide
molecules, 5 and 6, with the molecular formulas C42H58N8O7
and C40H54N8O7, respectively, which are identical to those of
unguisins F and A (1), respectively (Figure 4B). The structure
determination of 5 and 6 was performed using NMR and MS/
MS analyses and the advanced Marfey’s method24 (Figures S2
and S3). It was found that 5 was not identical to unguisin F but
rather was a new unguisin analogue in which D-Val4 of 2 is
replaced with a (2R,3R)-βMePhe residue (Figure 4C).
Similarly, compound 6, an isomer of 1, also contains a
(2R,3R)-βMePhe residue at the same position as 5; however,
D-Val2 of 5 is substituted with a D-alanine residue in 6 (Figure
4C). The two new peptides 5 and 6 were named unguisins H
(5) and I (6), respectively, and are probably biosynthesized in
a manner similar to that of 1 and 2 (Figure S8), given the
similarity between the ung and ung′ clusters. Meanwhile, the
hydrolyzed forms of 5 and 6 were not observed in A. campestris
(Figure 4B); however, it was found that both 5 and 6 can be

To obtain insight into the differences in the product
selectivity of UngA and UngA′, which possess the same
domain organization with 57% protein sequence identity, we
compared the nonribosomal codes of the A domains35 of each
module (A1 to A7) of these two NRPSs (Figure S10). The
nonribosomal codes of the A domains for the invariable
residues (A1, A5, A6, and A7) were completely identical or
similar between the two NRPSs. However, interestingly, the
nonribosomal codes of A7 of the two NRPSs are significantly
different from that of the A domain of NRPS9 involved in the
biosynthesis of fusaoctaxin A, which also accepts GABA as a
substrate.14 Meanwhile, the nonribosomal codes for the
variable residues (A2, A3, and A4) were somewhat different.
In particular, the nonribosomal code of A4, which is for L-Val
in UngA and (2S,3R)-βMePhe in UngA′, most substantially
differed between UngA and UngA′, which is consistent with
the product selectivity of the two enzymes. The NRPS
involved in the biosynthesis of unguisins E and F, which has
yet to be identified, likely has the same domain organization as
UngA and UngA′ with greater overall similarity to the former.
However, A3 of the unguisin E/F synthetase likely accepts
βMePhe as A4 of UngA′. Rational engineering of UngA and
UngA′ (e.g., replacement of A3 of UngA with A4 of UngA′)
could allow for the creation of an artificial NRPS that can
synthesize unguisins E and F without identifying their genuine
biosynthetic enzymes.
In summary, we identified the biosynthetic gene cluster of
the GABA-containing cyclic peptides unguisins A (1) and B
(2) and found that 1 and 2 are further transformed into
previously unreported linear peptides 3 and 4, respectively, by
the hydrolase UngD. It is unclear why these linear peptides are
not observed in the unguisin-producing fungus, but the
hydrolysis of 1 and 2 may be linked to a biological event,
which remains under investigation in our laboratory. Addi-
tionally, in this study we established a methodology by which a
large NRPS gene can be readily introduced into an A. oryzae
host. This can facilitate the characterization of NRPS-encoding
orphan biosynthetic gene clusters and NRPS engineering in a
fungal host. Finally, we used a genome mining approach to
discover two new unguisin congeners, unguisins H (5) and I
(6), each containing a βMePhe residue. Our study provided
insights into how fungi synthesize diverse peptide natural
products and into future NRPS engineering.
■EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
General Experimental Procedures. Optical rotations were
measured with a P-2000 digital polarimeter (JASCO Corporation).
NMR spectra were obtained at 600 MHz (1H)/150 MHz (13C) with a
Bruker Ascend Avance III HD spectrometer, and chemical shifts were
recorded with reference to solvent signals (1H NMR: DMSO-d6 2.49
ppm;
13C NMR: DMSO-d6 39.5 ppm). Samples for LC-HR-MS
analysis were injected into a SCIEX ExionLC AD system with a
SCIEX X500R Q-TOF mass spectrometer. Analytical HPLC was
performed on a Dionex Ultimate 3000 UHPLC system (Thermo
Scientific). Preparative HPLC was performed on a Waters 1525
binary HPLC pump with a 2998 photodiode array detector (Waters
Corporation). Flash chromatography was performed using an Isolera
Spektra One flash purification system (Biotage). Organic solvents
were purchased from Anaqua Co. Ltd., and other chemicals were
purchased from Wako Chemicals Ltd., Thermo Fisher Scientific,
Sigma-Aldrich, or J&K Scientific Ltd., unless noted otherwise.
Oligonucleotide primers (Table S3) were purchased from Beijing
Genomics Institute. PCR was performed using a T100 thermal cycler
(Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc.) with the Phanta Max super-fidelity DNA
polymerase (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd.) or Phire plant direct PCR
master mix (Thermo Scientific).
Strains. Aspergillus violaceofuscus CBS 11557 and Aspergillus
campestris CBS 348.81 were purchased from the Westerdijk Fungal
Biodiversity Institute and used as a source for the cloning of each gene
in the ung and ung′ clusters, respectively. Aspergillus oryzae NSAR1
(niaD−, sC−, ΔargB, adeA−)25 was utilized as the fungal heterologous
expression host. Standard DNA engineering was performed with
Escherichia coli DH5α (Takara Bio Inc.). E. coli Transetta(DE3)
(TransGen Biotech Co., Ltd.) was used for the expression of UngC
and UngD.
LC-MS Analysis of Metabolites from A. violaceofuscus, A.
campestris, and A. oryzae Transformants. To analyze the
metabolites produced by A. violaceofuscus CBS 11557 and A.
campestris CBS 348.81, the fungal strains were cultivated on YES
agar plates [2% yeast extract, 15% sucrose, 0.05% MgSO4·7H2O, 2%
agar supplemented with 1 mL/L of a trace element solution (10 g/L
ZnSO4·7H2O, 5 g/L CuSO4·5H2O), pH 6.5] at 30 °C for 7 days.
Meanwhile, A. oryzae transformants were cultivated on a DPY agar
plate [2% dextrin, 1% hipolypepton (Nihon Pharmaceutical Co.,
Ltd.), 0.5% yeast extract, 0.5% KH2PO4, 0.05% MgSO4·7H2O, and
1.5% agar] at 30 °C for 7 days. When necessary, 10.0 g/L of D-alanine
was added in the culture media.
After cultivation, small pieces of fungal mycelia along with agar
were cut from the plate, soaked in EtOAc, and extracted using an
ultrasonic bath. The EtOAc layer was transferred to a new tube, and
the solvent was evaporated using nitrogen gas flow.

Isolation of Unguisins A (1) and B (2). A. violaceofuscus CBS

Expression and Purification of UngC and UngD. To obtain
the intron-free ungC and ungD genes, total RNA was extracted from A.
violaceofuscus CBS 11557, which had been statically cultivated in YES
liquid medium for 5 days at 25 °C, with TransZol Plant (TransGen
Biotech Co., Ltd.), and cDNA synthesis was then performed using the
HiScript III 1st strand cDNA synthesis kit (Vazyme Biotech Co.,
Ltd.). The intron-free ungC and ungD were amplified from the
synthesized cDNA, using the primers described in Table S3 and Table
S4. Each amplified gene was then introduced into the pET-28a(+)
vector (Novagen), using a ClonExpress Ultra one step cloning kit
(Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd.) (Table S4).
For the expression and purification of UngC and UngD, E. coli
Transetta (DE3) was transformed using the above-constructed pET-
28a(+)-based plasmids. The E. coli transformants were first cultivated
using shaking at 37 °C/200 rpm in LB medium containing 50 mg/L
kanamycin sulfate and 34 mg/L chloramphenicol. When the cultures
reached an OD600 of 0.55, 0.05 mM IPTG was added to induce the
gene expression, followed by further incubation for 20 h at 16 °C/200
rpm. The cells were then collected by centrifugation and resuspended
in lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM
imidazole, 5% glycerol). After cell lysis by sonication and subsequent
centrifugation, the supernatant was loaded on a Ni-NTA affinity
column, which was then washed with 30 column volumes (CV) of
wash buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 10 mM
imidazole, 5% glycerol). His-tagged proteins were eluted from the
column using 5 CV of elution buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 150
mM NaCl, 300 mM imidazole, 5% glycerol). The protein solution was
ultrafiltrated and concentrated using a 10 kDa Amicon Ultra-15
device (Merck) with imidazole-free buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5,
150 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol). The purity of each purified protein was
examined by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electro-
phoresis (SDS-PAGE) (Figure S11), and the protein concentrations
were determined with a NanoDrop OneC spectrophotometer
(Thermo Scientific).
Enzymatic Reaction Assay of UngC. Enzymatic reactions of
UngC were performed in reaction mixtures containing 50 mM Tris-
HCl buffer (pH 7.5), 1.0 mM L- or D-alanine, 100 μM pyridoxal 5′-
phosphate (PLP), and 15.4 μM UngC, in 50 μL reaction mixtures at
30 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, the reaction mixtures were dried under
nitrogen gas flow and then dissolved in 200 μL of 1 N NaHCO3, to
which 100 μL of a 1% acetone solution of 1-fluoro-2,4-dinitrophenyl-
5-D-leucinamide (D-FDLA) was added. The mixture was incubated at
80 °C for 3 min, and 100 μL of 2 N HCl was then added for
neutralization. Authentic amino acid samples of L- and D-alanine were
also derivatized using D-FDLA as described above. Each reaction
mixture was diluted with 300 μL of 50% aqueous MeCN solution and
analyzed by LC-MS using an Accucore C18 column with a solvent
system of 20 mM formic acid (solvent A) and MeCN containing 20
mM formic acid (solvent B), at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min and a
column temperature of 40 °C. Separation was performed using a
linear gradient from 10:90 (solvent B/solvent A) to 100:0 for 10 min,
100:0 for the following 3 min, and a linear gradient from 100:0 to
10:90 in the following 2.0 min, and then 10:90 for 2.5 min of
equilibrium.
Enzymatic Reaction Assay of UngD. Enzymatic reactions of
UngD were performed in reaction mixtures containing 50 mM Tris-
HCl buffer (pH 7.5), 0.25 mM of unguisin A (1) or B (2), and 10.0
μM UngD, in 50 μL reaction mixtures at 30 °C for 1 h. The reactions
were terminated by the addition of 50 μL of MeOH and vortex
mixing. The supernatant obtained after centrifugation was analyzed by
HPLC (35% aqueous MeCN containing 20 mM formic acid, 1.0 mL/
min), using an Accucore C18 column (4.6 i.d. × 100 mm; Thermo
Scientific).
Gene Deletion Experiments. For the deletion of ungC or ungD,
approximately 1 kb of the upstream and downstream regions of the
targeted gene were amplified from the genomic DNA of A.
violaceofuscus CBS 11557. The two DNA fragments and the
glufosinate-resistant gene (bar) amplified from the pBARI vector36
were then ligated into the SmaI-digested pUC19 vector with a
ClonExpress Ultra one step cloning kit, in which the bar gene is
flanked by the 5′ and 3′ regions of the targeted gene. Primer
sequences and a detailed method for the construction of the plasmids
for the gene deletions, designated as pUC19-ungCΔ and pUC19-
ungDΔ, respectively, are summarized in Table S3 and Table S4.
The transformation of A. violaceofuscus CBS 11557 was performed
using the CRISPR-Cas9 system in a similar manner to that for the
deletion of pyvA from the same fungus,11 except that Yatalase -Plus-
(Takara Bio Inc.) was used as fungal cell lytic enzymes and that a
different pair of CRISPR RNAs (crRNAs) was used (protospacer
sequences for ungC: 5′-ATCATGATGATGCTCACTCG-3′; 5′-
GATCCACCACAAGACAGCGG-3′; protospacer sequences for
ungD: 5′-GAGGCCAACACACTGTTCTA-3′; 5′-CCACAC-
CTATCTCCGACTGA-3′). The successful deletion of targeted
genes was confirmed by colony-direct PCR of each transformant
(Figure S1).
Isolation of Compounds 3 and 4. To isolate compounds 3 and
4 from the in vitro enzymatic reactions, the purified UngD (final conc
1.0 μM) was added to 40 mL of reaction buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl
buffer pH 7.5) and incubated with 0.2 mM 1 or 2, respectively, at 30
°C for 3 h. The reaction mixture was loaded onto a Sep-Pak C18 Plus
short cartridge (Waters Corporation) and eluted stepwise using a
H2O−MeOH (containing 0.05% TFA) gradient (100:0 to 0:100).
Fractions that contained 3 or 4 were then combined to yield 3.2 mg of
3 and 6.7 mg of 4.
Construction of A. oryzae Transformation Plasmids. Initially,
a variant of the pTAex3A vector37 was created, in a similar manner to
that for the construction of the pTAex3-HR vector from pTAex3.31
The new vector, designated as pTAex3A-HR (Figure S12), is
intended for the homologous recombination-based transformation
and targets the sC locus of A. oryzae.38
Upon the construction of the transformation plasmids, the database
sequence of the ung genes was manually revised (Table S2). For the
heterologous expression of ungA, the gene was split into two
fragments, each containing approximately 1 kb overlapped sequence
for in vivo homologous recombination. The first half of ungA was
ligated into the PCR-linearized pTAex3A-HR vector lacking the amyB
terminator (TamyB) and the rear side of the sC locus. Meanwhile, the
latter half of ungA was ligated with TamyB, the adeA gene, and the
rear side of the sC locus. These two plasmids were named pUngA-1

Transformation of A. oryzae. Transformation of A. oryzae was

(protospacer sequences for the sC locus: 5′-GAGTTTCTCTTAAAG-

3′; 5′-AATCAGTCCAAGAACTGCCT-3′). The Cas9 ribonucleo-

was inoculated on 100 YES agar plates and cultivated for 9 days at 25

mm; Waters Corporation), to yield 110.0 mg of 5. Meanwhile,
approximately one-third of the fractions containing 6 were purified by
preparative HPLC (40% aqueous MeCN containing 0.05% TFA, 10.0
mL/min), using an XBridge BEH C18 OBD Prep column (100 Å, 5
μm, 19 i.d. × 250 mm; Waters Corporation), to yield 25.5 mg of 6.
Advanced Marfey’s Method to Determine the Absolute
Configurations of Compounds 3 and 4 and Unguisins H (5)
and I (6). To determine the absolute configurations of 3, 4, and
unguisins H (5) and (6), 0.3 mg of 3, 0.7 mg of 4, 0.5 mg of 5, or 0.6
mg of 6 was first hydrolyzed in 0.5 mL of 6 N HCl at 115 °C for 1 h.
The reaction mixture was cooled on ice for 3 min, and the
hydrochloric acid was then evaporated under nitrogen gas flow. The
residue was dissolved in 0.5 mL of H2O, which was lyophilized to
remove the residual hydrochloric acid. The obtained hydrolysates, as
well as authentic amino acid samples L- and D-alanine, L- and D-valine,
L- and D-phenylalanine, L- and D-leucine, L- and D-tryptophan, and
(2R,3R)-, (2R,3S)-, (2S,3R)-, and (2S,3S)-β-methylphenylalanine
(=2-amino-3-phenylbutyric acid) (J&W Pharmlab), were derivatized
using D-FDLA and analyzed by LC-MS as described above for the
enzymatic reaction products of UngC (Figure S3). The derivatives of
(2R,3R)- and (2R,3S)-β-methylphenylalanine were also analyzed
