ABSTRACT: A biosynthetic gene cluster that produces a putative
antibacterial natural product was identified from the genome of
Vibrio ruber DSM 16370 based on its colocalization with a gene
encoding an aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase homologue. Biological
assays confirmed that the biosynthetic pathway produced an
antibacterial metabolite while also verifying assignment of the
aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase as a self-resistance mechanism. The
structures of five closely related compounds of mixed polyketide
nonribosomal peptide origin were elucidated. Sequence analysis,
mutagenesis and stable isotope feeding studies led to decoding of a
plausible biosynthetic pathway for these natural products. The
primary products, a trio of interconverting isomers designated vibriomycins A, B, and B′, were found to be unusual allosteric
inhibitors of bacterial threonyl-tRNA synthetases. Our study highlights the feasibility of discovering new anti-infective chemotypes
via genome mining.
■INTRODUCTION
Polyketide synthases (PKSs) and nonribosomal peptide
synthetases (NRPSs) synthesize natural products in an
assembly line manner.1,2 PKSs and NRPSs are composed of
multimodular protein complexes, with each module respon-
sible for selecting and extending the growing chain by one unit
followed by a prescribed series of modifying reactions. Many
antibiotics in nature are synthesized by assembly line PKSs,
NRPSs, or hybrid PKS−NRPS systems.3
Antibiotic resistance among human pathogens is becoming
widespread, necessitating development of new anti-infective
agents against multidrug resistant pathogens.4−6 While more
than half of FDA-approved antibacterials over the past 40 years
are natural product derivatives,7−9 traditional approaches to
bioactivity-guided discovery have been hampered by the
problem of antibiotic rediscovery.10−12 Genome mining can
address this problem by targeting biosynthetic gene clusters
(BGCs) distantly related to ones that make known antibiotics.
However, the mere identification of a novel BGC does not
guarantee that it produces an antibiotic.13 Encouragingly,
bacteria often encode self-resistance mechanisms in close
proximity to a BGC if its product has antibacterial activity.14
The presence of a putative self-resistance gene can thus not
only flag a BGC as a potential source of an anti-infective agent
but may also hold clues to the compound’s mode of action.
Common self-resistance mechanisms include pumps for
exporting the antibiotic from the cell,15 enzymes that modify
the antibiotic or its target to reduce susceptibility,16 and
orthologs of the drug target that are unsusceptible to antibiotic
inhibition.17
We recently curated a list of ∼9000 nonredundant assembly
line PKS and hybrid PKS−NRPS BGCs.12 Most of these
BGCs were designated “orphans” because their products were
unknown and could not be deduced from sequence
information alone. By querying this library of BGCs, using
self-resistance guided genome mining,18 with 14 known self-
resistance mechanisms,19 several putative antibiotic biosyn-
thetic pathways were identified. Among these, an orphan
hybrid PKS-NRPS from Vibrio ruber was selected for further
analysis because: (i) it did not share similarity to any other
entry within our list of nonredundant PKSs and PKS−NRPSs;
and (ii) it was limited to a bacterial genus not particularly
known for its ability to synthesize complex polyketides. Here
we describe the structures and antibiotic properties of four
related compounds, vibriomycins A (1), B′ (2), B (3), and C
(4), from V. ruber DSM 16370.

■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
We mined our BGC library for homologues of selected self-
resistance genes within 10 kb of a PKS gene.12 Three hybrid
NRPS−PKSs with identical architectures and >90% protein
sequence identity were identified in distinct Vibrio strains
(Tables S1 and S2). All three clusters contained a threonyl-
tRNA synthetase homologue (vbrJ); in each case a house-
keeping (putatively drug-susceptible) threonyl-tRNA synthe-
tase gene was found elsewhere in the genome. Our BGC of
interest encodes three NRPS modules, six PKS modules and a
thioesterase that are collectively encoded by nine genes (Figure
1; Table S2). Experimental analysis of this biosynthetic
pathway focused on V. ruber DSM 16370 (hereafter referred
to V. ruber).
To test if our BGC of interest produces an antibacterial
metabolite, we genetically engineered four V. ruber strains. V.
ruber TMP1 possessed an arabinose-inducible, glucose-
repressible pBAD promoter upstream of our BGC of interest.
V. ruber TMP2 was a derivative of TMP1 in which the
ketosynthase (KS) domain of Module 2 was deleted (ΔKS2) to
inactivate the biosynthetic pathway. V. ruber TMP3 and TMP4
were derivatives of TMP1 and TMP2, respectively, in which
the putative self-resistant vbrJ gene was deleted (ΔvbrJ).
Construction of TMP1−4 is detailed in the Supporting
Information (Figures S1−S4).
Using a disk diffusion assay with Bacillus subtilis as a model
bacterium, spent media from V. ruber TMP1 and TMP2
cultures revealed that our BGC of interest did in fact produce
an antibacterial substance, designated vibriomycin (Figure S5).
In the presence of glucose, no significant growth differences
were observed between V. ruber TMP1, 3, and 4 due to
suppression of transcription by the pBAD promoter (Figure
S6). In contrast, in the presence of arabinose, the growth of
TMP3, but not TMP4, was attenuated compared to TMP1
(Figure S6). Growth inhibition of TMP3 could be reversed
through genetic complementation with plasmid-borne vbrJ
(Figure S7). These findings implied that aminoacyl-tRNA
synthetase inhibition may be the mode of action of
vibriomycin.
To detect vibriomycin, we subjected V. ruber TMP1 and
TMP2 cultures to comparative untargeted metabolomic
analysis using LC−MS (Figure S8). At least three compounds,
with identical masses but distinct chromatographic retention
times were identified in TMP1 but not TMP2, and were
designated vibriomycin A (1), vibriomycin B (3), and
vibriomycin C (4) in order of their retention times (Figures
S8 and S9). Further examination of the vibriomycin B peak
revealed that it comprised of two isomers (vibriomycins B′ (2)
and B (3)) (Figure S8B). All isomers were secreted into the
culture medium (Figure S10).
Vibriomycin C (4) was present at relatively low levels in
fresh cultures of V. ruber TMP1 but accumulated over time in
samples enriched in vibriomycins A (1), B′ (2), and/or B (3),
especially when enrichment procedures involved acidic
conditions. For example, incubation of a mixture of
vibriomycins A (1), B′ (2), and/or B (3) at pH 3 for 48 h
led to their complete disappearance with a concomitant
increase in vibriomycin C (4) (Figure S11), suggesting 4 was
nonenzymatically derived from one or more of the other
compounds. In contrast, vibriomycins A (1), B (2), and B′ (3)
were interconvertible with a mixture of the three isomers being

Due to the stability of vibriomycin C (4) across a wide pH
range, we first investigated its structure (Figure S13).
Compound 4 had a molecular formula of C23H35N2O6S [M
+ H]+, as established by high-resolution electrospray ionization
mass spectrometry (HRESIMS) analysis (calcd: 467.2216,
obsd: 467.2215, 0.2 ppm error) (Figure S36). We elucidated
its structure via one- and two-dimensional NMR experiments,
including 1H and 13C NMR, COSY, HSQC, HMBC, and 15N
HSQC experiments (Figure 2, Table S3, Figures S14−S35).
The 13C NMR spectrum revealed signals for 23 carbon atoms
(Figures S25 and S26); in combination with the 1H spectrum,
we inferred the presence of four methyl, five methylene and
eight methine groups along with six quaternary carbons. N-
HSQC confirmed the presence of two nitrogen atoms, NH-1′
(δN 119.5 ppm, δH 8.74 ppm) and NH-2′ (δN 112.8 ppm, δH
7.67 ppm) (Figure S23). COSY analysis allowed identification
of two spin systems (H-7 to H-13 and NH-2′ to H-15), which
was corroborated by HMBC NMR analysis (Figure 2). One
methyl group, C-10 (δC 19.7 ppm) was attached to C-9 and
another methyl group, C-18 (δC 22.3 ppm) was attached to C-
16. HMBC correlations from H-12, H-13, H-15, and H-16 to
C-14 (δC 93.6 ppm) (Figure S31) positioned the quaternary
carbon C-14 (δC 93.6 ppm) between C-13 and C-15, thereby
connecting the two identified spin systems. The characteristic
deshielded chemical shifts of C-14 (δC 93 ppm) and methine
C-17 (δC 86.4 ppm),20−23 along with the HMBC cross-peak
from H-17 (δH 3.81 ppm) to C-14 (Figure S31), confirmed the
presence of an ether bridge between C-14 and C-17, forming
an oxolane ring. An HMBC correlation from H-20 (δH 2.52
ppm) to C-14 suggested a fused ring structure. The absence of
an HMBC cross-peak from H-19 (δH 3.79 ppm) to C-14 and
also from H-13 (δH 1.81 ppm) and H-15 (δH 2.77, 1.69 ppm)
to C-20 indicated the presence of an additional atom between
C-14 and C-20. The methylene carbon shift of C-20 (δC 28.1
ppm) and its diastereotopic proton shifts (δH 3.51, 2.52 ppm)
were consistent with a sulfur atom bridging C-14 and C-20,
forming a fused thio ring system in compound 4. Shared
COSY correlations between methine H-19 and NH-2′ (Figure
S18) and a HMBC correlation from NH-2′ (δH 7.92 ppm) to
nonprotonated carbonyl C-21 (δC 174.1 ppm) supported the
presence of an amide functional group (Figure S30). Shared
COSY correlations between the oxygenated methine H-22 (δH
4.13 ppm) and methyl H-23 (δH 1.33 ppm) (Figure S19) and a
COSY cross-peak from OH-22 (δH 5.7 ppm) to H-22 (Figure
S18), coupled with HMBC correlations from both H-22 and
methyl H-23 to amide carbonyl C-21 (Figures S27 and S29),
ascribed a terminal 2-hydroxypropanamide moiety to com-
pound 4. Chemical shifts of quaternary carbons C-1 (δC 175.5
ppm), C-4 (δC 99.7 ppm), and C-6 (δC 173.8) of 4 were
characteristic of the enolic form of a β-diketone.24−26 A
HMBC cross-peak from H-7 (δH 7.10 ppm) to C-6 (Figure
S30) confirmed the connection of the enolic β-diketone to C-7
(δC 120.2). The 13C chemical shifts of C-1 (δC 175.5 ppm), C-
4 (δC 99.7 ppm), and C-3 (δC 196.6 ppm) were in good
accordance with a tetramic acid moiety.27,28 Moreover, HMBC
correlations from H-2 (δH 3.98 ppm) to C-1 and C-3 (Figure
S29) and from NH-1′ (δH 8.40 ppm) to C-2, C-3, and C-4
(Figure S30) confirmed the presence of the terminal tetramic
acid in compound 4. The observed shared COSY correlation
between methyl H-5 (δH 1.28 ppm) and methine H-2 (Figure
S19) allowed placement of the last remaining methyl group. 1H
NMR spectrum obtained at −85 °C revealed an additional
broad proton resonance at ∼14.6 ppm that integrated to ∼1 H
atom, suggesting a hydrogen bonded OH proton at C-6
consistent with an enol proton in the enolic form of a β-
diketone (Figure S24).29 Upon addition of 0.1% formic acid to
compound 4, alkene protons H-7 (δH 7.10 ppm) and H-8 (δH
7.10 ppm) were noticeably overlapped (Figure S17). To
measure the J coupling constant, acetate (pH 7) was added to
the sample, resulting in a coupling constant of 16.5 Hz,
indicative of an H-7-H-8 trans double bond (Figure S32). The
MS/MS fragmentation pattern was consistent with our
proposed structure of vibriomycin C (4) (Figure S37).

nearly identical 2D NMR correlations to those observed for 4
in their C-1 to C-13 substructures (Figure 2, Tables S3 and
S4). COSY analysis identified a spin system from H-15 to H-
20 in 1−3 (Figures S52 and S53). The oxolane ring remained

Compared to 4, compounds 1−3 exhibited two key
differences: the absence of an HMBC correlation between
H-20 (δH 3.24 ppm (1), 3.32 ppm (2), 3.28 ppm (3)) and C-

with its attachment to a sulfur atom. This methylene also
showed HMBC correlations to carbon C-19 and to a
deshielded resonance C-21 (δC 177.4 ppm (1−3)) (Figures
S47 and S50) that was assigned as a nonprotonated carbon
attached by a single bond to sulfur, a double bond to nitrogen,
and a final single bond to a distal carbon atom. Together, these
features resulted in assembly of a thiazoline ring in compounds
1−3. The ring was further corroborated by signature thiazoline

single protonated nitrogen atom NH-1′ (δH 6.63 ppm, δN
109.2 ppm) in all three compounds 1−3 (Figure S61). The
remainder of the proposed structure was supported by
observed shared COSY correlations between the oxygenated
methine H-22 (δH 4.56 ppm) and methyl H-23 (δH 1.39 ppm)
(Figure S53), coupled with HMBC correlations from both H-
22 and methyl H-23 to C-21 (Figures S47 and S51), ascribing
a terminal hydroxymethyl moiety to compounds 1−3.
To distinguish between the structures of 2 and 3, we focused
on the most significant chemical shift differences between the
two isomers. These differences were localized around the 5-
membered oxolane ring, leading to the speculation that 2 and 3
were epimers at C-14. To detect exchangeable hydroxy
moieties, the sample was desalted, and the pH was reduced
from 7.6 to 5.6. Using DMSO instead of acetone, the
exchangeable protons could be detected (Table S5, Figures
S57−S58). 2D experiments including N-HSQC, HMBC and
COSY revealed the presence of exchangeable protons at NH-1′
(δH 6.63 ppm (1−3)), OH-14 (δH 5.49 (2), 5.40 ppm (3)),
and OH-22 (δH 5.64 ppm (1−3)) (Figures S58 and S61). A
ROESY experiment confirmed the epimeric relationship
between compounds 2 and 3 by detecting the opposite set
of cross-peaks involving the C-14 hydroxy substituent in the
two compounds (Figure S63). OH-14 in both compounds 2
(δH 5.49 ppm) and 3 (δH 5.40 ppm) showed ROESY
correlations to diastereotopic H-12 (δH 1.35 ppm, 1.31 ppm)
and H-13 (δH 1.50 ppm, 1.62 ppm) protons (Figure S63).
However, the remaining ROESY correlations from OH-14
differed between compounds 2 and 3. OH-14 in compound 2
showed ROESY correlations to H-16 (δH 2.35 ppm), H-19 (δH
4.49 ppm), H-20 (δH 3.24 ppm), and H-15b (downfield
diastereotopic proton) (δH 1.92 ppm) (Figure S63). In
contrast, OH-14 in compound 3, showed ROESY correlations
to CH3-18 (δH 0.84 ppm), H-17 (δH 3.62 ppm), and H-15a
(upfield diastereotopic proton) (δH 1.45 ppm) (Figure S63).
These opposing ROESY correlations from OH-14 around the
oxolane ring confirm that compounds 2 and 3 are epimers at
C-14. The epimeric relationship at C-14, however, does not
provide sufficient information to determine their elution order
by LC; thus, their spectroscopic descriptions are interchange-
able. Studies have demonstrated that α- and β-anomers of
sugars exhibit different propensities for dehydration during
mass spectrometry due to differences in stereochemistry at the
anomeric center.32,33 The stereochemical variation alters the
spatial arrangement of hydroxyl groups, which in turn affects
the energy barriers for dehydration. By analogy, we postulate
that vibriomycin B′ has a lower dehydration energy barrier
than vibriomycin B, leading to preferential dehydration of
vibriomycin B′ under LC−MS conditions. The elution of 1
before 2 or 3 in LC is consistent with the anticipated higher
polarity of the ketone in 1 (Figures S8 and S69). MS/MS
fragmentation analysis of vibriomycins A, B′ and B is also
consistent with their proposed structures (Figures S66 and
S68).
Guided by the structures of vibriomycins A (1), B′ (2), B
(3), and C (4), we revisited their hybrid PKS-NRPS
biosynthetic pathway (Figure 1). Isotopic labeling with 3-13C
L-lactate established that L-lactate was the likely primer unit of
the enzymatic assembly line (Figure S70), implying that the
cryptic KR domain in the loading NRPS-like module was
inherently inactive. The presence of a cyclization-competent
condensation domain (Cy) in Module 1 (Figure S71) along
with the absence of an oxidative domain is consistent with the
predicted formation of a thiazolinyl-PCP intermediate by this
module. Five of the following six PKS modules (Modules 2, 4,
5, 6, and 7) harbor malonyl-CoA utilizing cis-AT domains and
catalyze chain elongation with varying degrees of β-
ketoreduction. Modules 2 and 5 also contain S-adenosylme-
thionine (SAM) dependent C-methyltransferase domains,
consistent with the formation of methyl-branched substituents
at the corresponding points in the biosynthetic pathway.
Indeed, isotopic labeling studies with deuterated methionine
(d3-Met) confirmed the presence of two enriched CD3 methyl
groups in vibriomycin A/B′/B (1−3) (Figures S72 and S73).
Module 3 lacks an acyltransferase (AT) domain, and the BGC
does not include a putative trans-AT homologue as might be

Based on sequence analysis, the vibriomycin BGC in V. ruber
was predicted to encode two tailoring enzymesa cytochrome
P450 monooxygenase (vbrI) and a SAM-dependent methyl-
transferase (vbrL) (Table S2). Because the closely related
BGC in Vibrio gazogenes lacks the vbrL gene, we anticipated
that the putative methyltransferase was dispensable for
antibiotic biosynthesis. In fact, the ΔvbrL derivative of V.
ruber TMP1 retained its ability to produce all vibriomycin
isomers, confirming that this methyltransferase was not
involved in vibriomycin biosynthesis (Figures 3 and S77). In
contrast, a derivative of V. ruber TMP1 lacking the cytochrome
P450 monooxygenase gene (ΔvbrI) was unable to synthesize
any vibriomycin compound (Figures 3 and S78), and showed
much weaker antibiotic activity in the disk diffusion assay
against B. subtilis compared to vibriomycin (Figure S79).
Comparative metabolomic analysis of the ΔvbrI strain revealed
a metabolite (5) that differed by 14 Da from vibriomycins A/
B/B′ ([M + H]+, calcd C23H37N2O5S 453.2418, obsd
453.2420) (Figure S80) instead of the expected difference of
16 Da due to the predicted C−H insertion reaction.
Preliminary MS/MS analysis was consistent with 5 lacking
the ketone/hemiketal presumably formed by oxygenase
modification of the olefin installed by module 3 on the
polyketide backbone; instead, the olefin appeared to be
hydrogenated (Figure S94). The structure of 5 was further
confirmed via
1H,
13C, COSY, HSQC, and HMBC NMR
experiments of the purified compound 5 (Table S6, Figures
S81−S92). NMR analysis of 5 yielded similar chemical shifts
and nearly identical 2D NMR correlations to those observed
for 1, with one key difference-ketone carbonyl C-14 in 1 (δC
210.2 ppm) was replaced by a methylene carbon C-14 (δC 27.3
ppm) in 5 (Table S6). As a result, in 5 we also observed two
additional diastereotopic methylene protons H-14 (δH 1.26
ppm, 1.42 ppm) (Figure S88). H-14 as expected, also had
shared COSY correlations with H-13 (δH 1.3 ppm) and H-15
(δH 1.68 ppm) (Figure S86). Only one set of alkene protons
H-7 (δH 7.71 ppm) and H-8 (δH 6.56 ppm) were observed in
the 1H spectrum (Figure S84), further validating that the olefin
installed by module 3 was hydrogenated in 5. Moreover,
HRESIMS, MS/MS fragmentation (Figures S93 and S94),
biosynthetic logic and UV absorbance of 5 (Figure S93) are
consistent with our proposed structure. While further studies
will be required to elucidate the timing and mechanism of this
unusual cytochrome P450 monooxygenase, we propose that it
catalyzes an anti-Markovnikov oxidation of the α,β-enoyl-ACP
product of Module 3 to a carbonyl via an oxo-transfer, and a
1,2-hydride migration.36,37 In turn, this allows for the
nonenzymatic formation of the oxolane ring with α and β
anomeric configurations at C-14 in compounds 2 and 3.
By combining the above spectroscopic evidence with well-
precedented biosynthetic logic, the following stereochemical
features of the vibriomycins can be proposed. Because the
tetramic acid moiety is derived from L-alanine, C-2 is predicted
to have an S stereoconfiguration. (The terminal NRPS module
lacks an epimerase domain, rendering Ala racemization
unlikely.) A trans-alkene between C-7 and C-8 is established
by the 16.5 Hz coupling constant. Because Module 1
incorporates
L-cysteine, C-19 is predicted to have an R
stereoconfiguration. (This NRPS module also lacks an
epimerase domain.) The relative stereochemistry around the
cyclic hemiketal of vibriomycins B and B′ could be established.
The stronger ROESY correlation between hydrogen atoms
attached to C-17 and C-18 compared to C-16 (Figure S65)
implied that H-17 and the C-18 methyl substituent are cis to
each other. As seen in Figure S63, in 2 the hydroxy substituent
at C-14 is trans to both H-17 and the C-18 methyl substituents
whereas the corresponding relationship is cis in 3 (Figure S63).
Thus, both C-17 and C-16 have either an R or an S
stereoconfiguration. Given J coupling constants for H-17−H-
19 and H-17−H-16 of 7.5 and 6 Hz, respectively, we
undertook density functional theory (DFT) calculations to
predict these J coupling constants. If C-17 and C-16 are
assigned as S stereocenters, DFT predicts coupling constants of
1.9 and 9.1 Hz for H-17−H-19 and H17−H16, respectively,
consistent with the H-17−H-19 conformation being gauche
(Figure S95). In contrast, if C-17 and C-16 were assigned as R
stereocenters, the relative conformation of H-17 and H-19 is
predicted to be anti with coupling constants of 9.2 and 4.5 Hz

To prove that vibriomycin was a threonyl-tRNA synthetase
(ThrRS) inhibitor, we sought to investigate if overexpression
of the gene encoding the housekeeping ThrRS could reverse
growth inhibition seen in TMP3 (V. ruber pBAD ΔvbrJ)
(Figure S96) Indeed, growth of TMP3 was significantly
attenuated relative to TMP1 (V. ruber pBAD), whereas TMP3
harboring pSK23 (a plasmid overexpressing the V. ruber
housekeeping tRNA synthetase gene) reversed this growth
inhibition (Figure S96).
The minimal inhibitory concentration of the vibriomycin A,
B′, and B (1−3) mixture was 16 μg/mL against B. subtilis and
0.25 μg/mL against Escherichia coli WO153, a mutant strain
with reduced ability to synthesize Lipid A and also harboring a
deletion in the tolC efflux pump.38 Complementation of E. coli
WO153 with plasmid pSK23 led to an increase in the MIC of
vibriomycin A, B′, and B (1−3) by 32-fold to 8 μg/mL, further
corroborating the proposed mechanism of action of these
antibiotics.

We also investigated the effect of a mixture of vibriomycin A,
B, B′ (1−3) on the activity of the housekeeping ThrRS from V.
ruber in vitro. ThrRS ligates threonine onto its cognate tRNA
with concomitant release of pyrophosphate, which can be
spectrophotometrically quantified (Figure S97). While vibrio-
mycin C (4) did not show detectable inhibitory activity in this
assay, the vibriomycin A, B, B′ (1−3) mixture showed strong
inhibitory activity at 25 μM (Figure 4). In contrast,
vibriomycin A/B/B′ (1−3) did not inhibit the resistant
ThrRS even at 100 μM (Figure 4).
Steady state kinetic analysis was undertaken to probe the
mechanism of action of vibriomycin A/B′/B (1−3). The
antibiotics were found to be noncompetitive with threonine
(Ki ∼8 μM) and ATP (Ki ∼15 μM) (Figure 5A,C), implying
an allosteric inhibition mechanism. Because allosteric binding
sites are generally not well-conserved, vibriomycin A/B′/B
(1−3) may have evolved with narrow host-range selectivity.
In summary, we used resistance-guided genome mining to
identify and characterize a novel antibiotic from V. ruber. This
work has revealed an unprecedented chemotype only distantly
related to 3-enoyltetramic acids found in some bioactive
natural products.39−41 Our approach can be used to discover
other anti-infective agents from understudied bacteria.
