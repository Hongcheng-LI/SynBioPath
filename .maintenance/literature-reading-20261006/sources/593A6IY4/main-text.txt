ABSTRACT: Terpene synthases produce a remarkable structural
diversity from acyclic precursors through complex carbocation
cascades. Here, we report the crystal structure of the bacterial
sesterterpene synthase StvirS bound to geranylfarnesyl thiopyr-
ophosphate (GFSPP), revealing a preorganized active site that
enforces a defined folding of the C25 backbone. Guided by this
structure, active-site engineering at 11 positions yielded 23 enzyme
variants and 13 new sesterterpenes. Specific substitutions altered
reaction trajectories, stereochemistry, or induced premature
termination, emphasizing the sensitivity of StvirS to subtle
structural changes. Isotopic labeling established the absolute
configurations of 11 products and experimentally confirmed a
conserved cyclization pathway. These results illustrate how precise noncovalent interactions govern terpene biosynthesis and
highlight the promise of structure-based design to reprogram terpene cyclase reactivity.
■INTRODUCTION
Despite their vast structural diversity, the more than 100,000
terpenoids discovered to date are all biosynthesized from just
two simple C5 precursors: dimethylallyl pyrophosphate
(DMAPP) and isopentenyl pyrophosphate (IPP).1−3 Their
finely balanced reactivity with DMAPP being an electrophile
and IPP a nucleophile allows their coupling by prenyltrans-
ferases (PTs)4 to successively yield geranyl (C10, GPP),
farnesyl (C15, FPP), geranylgeranyl (C20, GGPP), geranylfar-
nesyl (C25, GFPP) and even larger oligoprenyl pyrophos-
phates. Their further conversion is catalyzed by terpene
synthases (TSs) that can introduce an astonishing structural
complexity in a single enzymatic step, turning an achiral and
acyclic precursor molecule into a chiral compound with usually
several stereogenic centers and rings. The subclass of
sesterterpenes is made from GFPP by the action of
sesterterpene synthases (StTS). Several StTSs are known
from fungi in which bifunctional enzymes with a PT and a TS
domain are found.5−19 Plants make use of discrete enzymes for
sesterterpene biosynthesis instead, whose encoding genes are
usually clustered.20−24 The same situation is found in bacteria,
and type I sesterterpene synthases were discovered for
sestermobaraenes from Streptomyces mobaraensis,25 for sester-
violenes from Streptomyces violens,26 for sesterviridenes from
Kitasatospora viridis,27 for subrutilane from Streptomyces
subrutilus,28 and for lentzeadienes as well as atacamatrienes
from Lentzea atacamensis.29
Recent studies have demonstrated that terpene synthases
can be functionally reprogrammed through protein engineer-
ing.30 For example, a quadruple mutant of PfNS redirected
product selectivity toward tricyclic ophiobolin F, instead of the
bicyclic nigtetraene formed by the wild-type enzyme.31 Even
single-residue substitutions have proven sufficient to switch
enzyme function. An A163M mutation in GdolS from
Streptomyces citricolor shifts the product profile from
germacradien-4-ol to the hydrocarbon germacrene A.32
Conversely, a G305E variant of SdS from Streptomyces
pristinaespiralis redirects product formation from selina-
4(14),7(11)-diene to selin-7(11)-en-4-ol.33 Beyond specific
point mutations, active-site expansion through the substitution
of bulky residues by smaller ones has enabled the
accommodation of larger terpene precursors, thereby expand-
ing the accessible product space.34−37 Yet, structure-based
mutagenesis approaches have proven particularly effective in
guiding such modifications, as demonstrated for various
sesquiterpene (StSs),38−40 diterpene (DTSs),41−43 and
StTSs.44 Here we report on the X-ray structure of
sesterviridene synthase from K. viridis (StvirS), a StTS that
produces the three hydrocarbons sesterviridenes A −C.27
Based on structural data, 11 active site residues were selected
for the construction of 23 rationally designed enzyme variants,

■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
The Catalytic Mechanism of StvirS. Isotopic labeling
and density functional theory (DFT) calculations have
previously outlined a mechanistic model for StvirS,27 which
we briefly revisit as a basis for our current structure-based
analysis. The reaction cascade starts with ionization of the
substrate GFPP to the allyl cation A that undergoes a 1,15−
14,18-cyclization to B (Scheme 1). A long-range 1,5-hydride
shift then leads to C that can react in a 6,10-cyclization to D,
followed by two sequential 1,2-hydride shifts to E and F.
Another 2,12-cyclization results in G, further reacting by 1,2-
hydride migration to H, 2,11-cyclization to I and skeletal
rearrangement to J. The cascade is terminated through a 1,5-
hydride shift to K, a 1,2-methyl group migration to L and
alternative deprotonations to the main product sesterviridene
A (1) or the side product sesterviridene B (2). The third
product sesterviridene C (3) arises by deprotonation of G. The
stereochemistry observed in the products requires a GFPP
starting conformation with all Me groups Me21 −Me24
pointing down. This will result in Me22 pointing down and
H14/H18 up in B, and also explains the configurations at C6
and C10 in the cyclization to D with both hydrogens at these
carbons pointing up.
Enzyme Crystallization and Structural Analysis.
Recombinant StvirS from Kitasatospora viridis27 was purified
using Ni2+ affinity and size-exclusion chromatography. Its
crystallization in complex with the synthetic substrate analogue
geranylfarnesyl thiopyrophosphate (GFSPP) enabled structure
determination at 2.4 Å resolution (Table S1, PDB ID: 9RS2).
The enzyme adopts the conserved α-helical fold of class I
terpene synthases45,46 in a closed conformation, comprising 12
helices (A-L) connected by distinct interhelical loops (Figure
1a). The catalytic cavity is defined by helices B1/2, C1/2, F,
G1/2, H, and K, and is fully occupied by the ligand (Figure
S1). The active site of StvirS shares the canonical bipartite
architecture characteristic of class I terpene cyclases,
comprising a hydrophilic region that coordinates the
pyrophosphate (OPP) group and three Mg2+, and a hydro-
phobic pocket that accommodates the prenyl backbone. As
expected, no water molecules are detected within the sealed
reaction chamber, reflecting the necessity to exclude bulk
solvent to protect the highly reactive carbocation intermediates
from premature quenching. The thiopyrophosphate (SPP)
moiety of GFSPP is coordinated by three magnesium ions: two
adjacent Mg2+ are positioned via the aspartate-rich motif
(ARM,
84DDxxxD89) on helix C2, which shows a slight
deviation from the canonical DDxxD motif due to an
additional residue between the second and third aspartate.
The third Mg2+ is ligated by the NSE triad (226NxxxSxxxE234)
on helix H (Figure S1). Beyond metal coordination, the SPP
group of GFSPP engages in a network of specific polar
interactions that stabilize the reactive geometry. The β-
phosphate additionally interacts with Lys233 (part of the
NSE triad) and the conserved 312RY313 motif between helices
K and L. Notably, Arg312 forms hydrogen bonds with Asp89,
resulting in a tight hydrogen-bonding network that connects
the ARM and NSE segments through the SPP group and the
metal cluster. The α-thiophosphate, introduced as a non-
hydrolyzable surrogate, is anchored by Arg180 on helix G1,
which serves as the OPP sensor.47 This interaction is further
stabilized by Asp226 from the NSE triad, contributing to a
complex electrostatic environment that is essential for catalysis.

points toward the C1 atom of GFSPP (3.7 Å) and aligns with
the 2,3-double bond of the surrogate, thereby promoting OPP

bound conformation of GFSPP closely mimics a catalytic

While the arrangement of the SPP moiety and the Mg2+
cluster reflects conserved mechanistic features of class I
terpene synthases, the C25-hydrocarbon chain adopts a distinct
serpentine-like conformation within the hydrophobic chamber.
This bent geometry is enforced by side-chain contacts
involving Trp76, Trp154, and Ile303, which constrain the
central portion of the chain and define its dihedral angles
(Figure 1b, Figure S1c). As a result, the reactive centers are
precisely aligned along a trajectory that favors regio- and
stereoselective carbocation cascades during cyclization. The
two isoprene units proximal to the α-thiophosphate are
extended linearly, followed by a 180° rotation of the third
unit. The fourth is oriented antiparallel to the first two, while
the fifth undergoes a second 180° flip. Together, these
rotations generate a folded, product-like structure that closely
matches the conformation needed to position Me21 −Me25
downward, consistent with the observed product stereo-
chemistry (Scheme 1). This preorganized GFSPP conforma-
tion, stabilized exclusively by side-chain interactions, thus plays
a central role in guiding the complex ring-forming trans-
formations (see below).
To explore how the pocket geometry adapts to prenyl
substrates of different lengths, we compared StvirS with
cattleyene synthase CyS:GGPP (C20, geranylgeranyl pyro-
phosphate; PDB ID: 7Y88),41 selinadiene synthase SdS:2,3-
DHFPP (C15, 2,3-dehydro-farnesyl pyrophosphate; PDB ID:
4OKZ),47 and 2-methylisoborneol synthase MibS:2-FGPP
(C10, 2-fluorogeranyl pyrophosphate; PDB ID: 3V1X).48
Despite sharing less than 10% sequence identity, all enzymes
display highly conserved catalytic core domains (Cα RMSD <
0.8 Å) with structural divergence largely confined to terminal
extensions and surface loops (Figure 1c). In contrast, the shape
and size of the substrate-binding pocket differ significantly
across the structures: while the depth is relatively constant at ∼
5 Å, the pocket width varies from 8 to 11 Å, and the height
ranges from 4 to 10 Å (Figure S2). These differences reflect
the increasing chain length and conformational complexity of
the respective prenyl substrates. In StvirS, the C25-backbone of
GFSPP folds into two successive turns: a broader arc from C1
to C14, followed by a tighter bend starting at C15. This
conformation closely resembles the GGPP-bound state in CyS,
albeit with a longer and more extensively folded substrate in
StvirS (Figure 1c).
To further dissect the catalytic mechanism, we solved the
structure of ligand-free StvirSapo at 1.45 Å resolution (Table S1,
PDB ID: 9RRZ). Unlike SdS, for which an induced-fit
mechanism involving large-scale rearrangements upon sub-
strate binding has been demonstrated, StvirSapo was caught in
an intermediate conformation between the open and the
closed states (Figure 1d,e). Although the substrate and all
three catalytic Mg2+ are absent, key functional elements are
already positioned as observed in the GFSPP-bound complex.
These include the OPP sensor Arg180, the helix-break-helix
motif at the Gly184O effector, and the conserved RY motif.
The adjacent loop segment (residues 314−322), which follows
the RY motif, becomes ordered upon GFSPP binding through
a tight interaction between Tyr313 and the β-phosphate.
Consistent with this partial preorganization, the NSE triad is
already oriented for metal coordination but still undergoes
conformational adjustments upon substrate binding (Figure
1f). Specifically, Asn226 and Glu234 rearrange to complete
octahedral coordination of the third Mg2+, and Lys233 within
the NSE-triad shifts by approximately 6 Å inward to engage the
β-phosphate of the surrogate. By comparison, the ARM
displays a more pronounced structural change. In the GFSPP-
bound complex, this region adopts a compact helix-turn-helix
architecture bridging helices C2 and D. Upon substrate
binding, Asp89 shifts by approximately 7 Å to coordinate
one of the adjacent Mg2+, triggering a transition from a flexible
loop to an α-helical structure. In contrast, the ARM region in
StvirSapo remains extended and flexible, adopting a well-defined
loop conformation. Therefore, we assume that the coordina-
tion of the two Mg2+ delivered with the incoming substrate
induces the conformational switch between the two states.
These rearrangements also affect active-site accessibility:
whereas StvirSapo still presents a cavity open to bulk solvent
(Figure 1d), the GFSPP-bound structure reveals a catalytically
compartmentalized environment (Figure 1e). Strikingly,
despite these structural transitions, the hydrophobic residues
lining the isoprenoid-binding pocket maintain their positions
in both apo and ligand-bound forms, suggesting a rigid scaffold
optimized for selective substrate engagement. Thus, the
conserved hydrophobic framework implies that molecular
recognition begins with selective binding of the C25-hydro-
carbon backbone, which is followed by OPP coordination,
Mg2+ binding, and active-site closure. Taken together, the apo
and ligand-bound structures capture sequential conformational
snapshots of the reaction cycle and form the basis for a
structure-guided dissection of the catalytic mechanism.
To explore how these structural changes affect the catalytic
function, we studied the conformation of GFSPP in the active
site of StvirS in more detail. As noted, the ligand adopts a
preorganized trajectory that brings C1 and C15, as well as C14
and C18, into close proximity, with interatomic distances of
3.7 and 2.8 Å, respectively. This arrangement facilitates the
initial cyclization step leading to intermediate A (Figure 1b).
All five methyl groups point toward Phe81, thereby predefining
the configurations of the stereogenic centers that are formed
during the cyclization cascade. The prenyl-binding pocket is
lined almost entirely with aromatic and aliphatic residues,
establishing a strictly hydrophobic environment. Superimposed
on this framework, four aromatic side chains, Trp76, Trp154,
Trp189, and Phe81, surround GFSPP and are positioned to
stabilize carbocation intermediates via cation-π interactions.
Furthermore, the carbonyl oxygen of Gly184 is directed toward
the active site and contributes via cation-dipole interactions
(Figure S3a). Trp189 lies opposite the C19 atom of GFSPP at
a distance of approximately 4 Å, suggesting a role in stabilizing
intermediate B (Scheme 1 and Figure S3b), and may
additionally support the nearby cationic charge distribution
in the later intermediate K. As the reaction proceeds, and the
positive charge migrates toward the central part of the
molecule (C6, C7 and C10), Trp76, Trp154, and Ile303 are
ideally positioned to stabilize intermediates D, E, F and I
(Scheme 1 and Figure S3c). In later stages, including
intermediates E to M, the carbonyl oxygen of Gly184 remains
in proximity to the reactive centers and could facilitate charge
delocalization (Scheme 1 and Figure S3a). At the final step, the

Site-Directed Mutagenesis and Labeling Experi-
ments. Building on our structural insights, we next evaluated
the functional roles of selected residues through site-directed
mutagenesis and biochemical assays (Figures 2 and S4 −S12,
Tables S2 −S6). Guided by the StvirS:GFSPP complex, 23
variants were generated to probe their involvement in substrate
activation and catalytic control. Although no ligand is bound in
StvirSapo, the OPP sensor R180 is already positioned in a
catalytically competent orientation, while access to the binding
site appears partially restricted. To further explore the
contribution of this region, we first targeted the OPP sensor.
The R180K mutation, despite being a conservative sub-
stitution, reduced product formation to ∼20% of the wild-type
level, highlighting the importance of this residue. This effect
was even more pronounced in the D227N variant. Asp227,
naturally substituting the more usual Glu residue in the NSE
triad, forms a salt bridge with R180 and stabilizes its position
relative to the OPP group. Replacement with asparagine
completely abolished enzymatic activity, revealing the func-
tional coupling between the OPP sensor, the NSE motif, and
helix G1. Together with the role of Gly184 at the G1/2 helix-
break-helix motif, which is crucial for carbocation stabilization,
these findings define a compact and tightly coordinated
activation module.
In the following, we investigated whether targeted
perturbation of the C25-binding site (Figure 3) could be
utilized to modify the product spectrum of StvirS. The
exchange of several active site residues only resulted in a
reduced or even abolished enzyme productivity, but did not
yield interesting new products. This was the case for the
positions of Ser188 and Ile303, that were interrogated through
the enzyme variants S188T (61 ± 1% productivity for the sum
of all compounds, compared to the wild-type = 100%), S188A
(50 ± 7%), I303Y (3.1 ± 0.6%) and I303D (inactive). More
interesting were the results obtained targeting the positions of
Leu77, Leu223 and Phe81. The enzyme variants L77I (60 ±
6%) and L77Y (107 ± 3%) showed moderately reduced or
unchanged productivity, respectively, whereas the L77F variant
(81 ± 10%) resulted in enhanced production of 4, which is
also observed in small amounts in the wild-type protein.
Isolation and structure elucidation by NMR spectroscopy
revealed its identity as sesterviridene D, the deprotonation
product of intermediate C (Table S7, Figures S13 −S20).
From the L223V variant sesterviridene I (9) was isolated
(Table S8, Figures S21 −S28). This compound is a trace
product of the wild-type enzyme, but becomes the main
product of the L223V variant, demonstrating that even subtle
structural changes can cause a profound shift in the product
spectrum. Compound 9 originates from deprotonation of
intermediate H. The slightly enlarged active site resulting from
the L223V substitution may reposition the intermediate,
thereby triggering proton loss at an earlier stage of the
reaction. Indeed, compound 4 is also the main product of the
W154L variant (43 ± 2%), which supports the hypothesis that
this residue plays a critical function in forming intermediates D
and E, while in the absence of this stabilizing effect, premature
deprotonation of B produces compound 4. Both 4 and 9 are

Next, we analyzed the effects of engineering Val57. No
pronounced effects were observed for the V57A (25 ± 1%),
V57M (62 ± 24%) and V57I variants (34 ± 5%). In contrast,
the V57L mutant (92 ± 6%) yielded 3a as the main product
and additionally enabled access to sesterviridene H (8) and 18-
epi-sesterviridene J (10a) (Tables S12 and S13, Figures S53 −
S68). Compound 8 is particularly surprising, as it exhibits
inverted configurations at C2 and C12, which implies a
different cyclization mode at intermediate F, changing from the
usual 2Si,12Si to a 2Re,12Re cyclization. This change might
result from a conformational flip in F, involving a rotation of
the olefinic planes at C2 = C3 and C11 = C12 by ca. 180°.
Alternatively, a different starting conformation with reorienta-
tion of the C2 = C3 plane may explain the observed pattern,
and leaves the stereochemistry at C6, C7, C14, and C15
unchanged. Strikingly, the V57F variant revealed a significantly
increased production yield (226 ± 54%), and additionally
formed several new compounds. Besides 3a and 10a, 18-epi-
sesterviridene G (7a), 18-epi-sesterviridene H (8a), and 18-epi-
sesterviridene I (9a) were isolated (Tables S14 −S16, Figures
S69 −S92), demonstrating that the enzyme has a strong
preference for products from the 18-epi series. This effect is
likely caused by the bulkier Phe side chain in position 57,
which induces a repulsive interaction with the substrate GFPP
and shifts the terminal isopropylidene group toward Ala222.
Another compound obtained from the V57F variant is seco-
sesterviridene (13) (Table S17, Figures S93 −S100). This
molecule may arise from intermediate B via ring expansion to
O, a secondary cation potentially stabilized by cation-π
interaction with Phe57 in the V57F variant. A subsequent
ring opening to P and deprotonation then yield compound 14.
Finally, two additional compounds, sesterviridene E (5) and
sesterviridene F (6), were isolated from the V57L/W189A
variant (57 ± 7%) (Tables S18 and S19, Figures S101 −
S116). Hydrocarbons 5 and 6 were also recorded at lower
levels upon single site mutation (W189A, 30 ± 8%), and are
derived from intermediates E and D, respectively. In contrast,
the engineered variant containing the double mutation V57L/
I303Y was found to be inactive.
To support the proposed mechanistic pathway and product
assignments, it was crucial to determine the absolute
configurations of the newly identified terpenes. We therefore
applied a comprehensive isotopic labeling strategy (Table S20)
based on the known stereochemical course of oligoprenyl
pyrophosphate biosynthesis, as elucidated by Cornforth and
co-workers.50 The stereoselectively deuterated precursor (R)-
(1-13C,1-2H)IPP51 was enzymatically converted into labeled
GFPP using E. coli isopentenyl pyrophosphate isomerase
(IDI)52 and the prenyltransferase (PT) domain of the fungal
bifunctional ophiobolin F synthase from Aspergillus calidous-
tus17 (hereafter referred to as GFPPS). Additional labeled
precursors were generated from DMAPP and (E)-
(4-13C,4-2H)IPP53 using GFPPS. The resulting GFPP
isotopomers were cyclized by StvirS or its engineered variants,
and the products were analyzed by HSQC spectroscopy. This
approach enabled diastereotopic resolution of the deuterated
methylene groups, allowing for confident assignment of the
absolute configurations of compounds 3a, 4, 5, 6, 8, 8a, 9, 9a,
10, 10a and 12 (Figures S117 −S138). These data provide
independent confirmation of the proposed cyclization
sequence and further validate the stereochemical rationale of
terpene formation.

intermediates along the cyclization cascade, providing
compelling evidence for their mechanistic relevance. As
discussed above, formation of the main product 1 involves
top face deprotonation of the 1-pro-S hydrogen, with Gly184O
optimally positioned to act as a general base. A comparable
stereochemical course involving top face deprotonation can be

the final steps of the reaction cascade. The absence of
stereoisomers of 8/8a exhibiting the standard configurations at
C2 and C12 further supports this interpretation. This finding
suggests that Me25 must adopt an upward orientation to
approach Gly184O and enable proton abstraction, which
becomes possible only after rotation of the C2 = C3 olefin
plane.
Although the I303Y and V57L/I303Y variants lost activity
with GFPP, they still converted GGPP into cembrene A,27 a
natural product with recognized biological roles as a termite
trail pheromone54,55 and in crocodilian chemical signaling.56,57
To understand how such substrate selectivity persists despite
mutational changes, we solved the crystal structures of the
V57L and I303Y variants at 1.65 Å resolution (PDB IDs: 9RS4
and 9RS5; Table S1). The prenyl-binding pocket is virtually
identical to that of the apo and GFSPP-bound forms of StvirS,
with structural deviations confined to the engineered side
chains (Figures 3 and S1d). This strict conservation shows that
a single residue exchange can redirect product formation
without global rearrangement of the active site. The ability to
tune carbocation cascades through such minimal changes
underscores both the promise and the challenge of structure-
guided terpene-synthase engineering, and it provides a clear
mechanistic foundation for future rational redesigns.
■CONCLUSION
In this study, we report the crystal structure of the bacterial
sesterterpene synthase StvirS bound to the substrate analogue
GFSPP, providing detailed insights into active site organization
and its impact on cyclization cascades. As observed in several
DTS complex structures,41,43 the bound ligand adopts a folded,
product-like conformation, stabilized by defined side-chain
interactions that enforce a precise stereochemical trajectory.
These structural insights guided the rational design of 23
enzyme variants targeting key residues within the active site.
The mutational analysis showed that even subtle changes can
redirect the product profile, resulting in the discovery of 13
previously unknown sesterterpenes. Several substitutions
resulted in premature termination or alternative pathways,
illustrating how active site geometry and local electrostatics
shape carbocation cascade progression. In particular, mutations
affecting cation-π interactions frequently compromised ad-
vanced cyclization steps, highlighting their central role in
reaction fidelity. While previous engineering studies on STSs
have achieved nearly complete functional switches,39,58,59 work
on sesterterpene synthases has not yet yielded highly selective
StTSs. This likely reflects the greater complexity of
sesterterpene biosynthesis, as all known bacterial25−29 and
most plant wild-type enzymes21−24 are multiproduct synthases,
whereas fungal bifunctional enzymes often display higher
product specificity.6−10,14
To validate the proposed mechanism and product assign-
ments, we employed a stereoselective isotopic labeling strategy.
This approach enabled the determination of the absolute
configurations of 11 sesterterpenes. The resulting data
confirmed the regio- and stereochemical course of the
cyclization and provided independent support for the proposed
reaction pathway. Notably, the sesterviridenes E, H and I are
stereoisomers of aspergiltriene A and aspergildienes B and C
from the sesterterpene synthase AuAS from Aspergillus ustus.15
This demonstrates that our catalyst design enables access to
stereoisomers of natural product scaffolds that are otherwise
difficult to obtain. It is interesting to observe that aspergildiene
B, the stereoisomer of sesterviridene H, is formed without
configurational inversions at the junction between the 6- and
the 8-membered rings. This is well explained by previous
labeling experiments that revealed deprotonation from the
same hemisphere for all AuAS products, including aspergil-
diene B.17
The phenomenon of stereoisomer generation in cyclo-
pentane-forming DTSs and StTSs was recently summarized in
a review.60 In this context, our work identifies V57 as a key
residue that flips the stereoselectivity of StvirS, while another
study on fungal bifunctional StTSs uncovered residues within
the G2 helix as stereochemical switches.61 Taken together, our
findings illustrate how spatial constraints and defined non-
covalent interactions govern terpene cyclization with high
precision. Notably, the diverse product profiles observed in this
study emerged from a limited set of primarily single-residue
mutations. This underscores the pronounced sensitivity of
terpene synthases to subtle structural changes and highlights
the potential of structure-based approaches for enzyme
diversification. Although accurate prediction of terpene
synthase reactivity remains a major challenge, the combined
structural and mechanistic insights presented here lay a solid
foundation for future efforts to develop terpene synthases with
customized catalytic properties.
