ABSTRACT: The time-consuming and repetitive rediscovery of
known molecules has long hindered the efficient identification of
novel drug leads from natural sources. Metabologenomics, the
synergistic integration of metabolomics and genomics, has
emerged as a powerful strategy to circumvent these challenges,
enabling targeted discovery of new chemical entities. In this study,
we developed a 2D-NMR-based metabologenomics workflow
integrated with high-throughput activity screening to rapidly
identify a novel family of antibiotic macrolides, samsumycins
(1−6), from the deep-sea Streptomyces sp. OUCT16-12. The
planar and stereochemical structures of these compounds were
elucidated through comprehensive spectroscopic analysis and NOE-constrained quantum chemical calculations. Remarkably, 2−6
feature an unprecedented 6/6/26-fused macrolide scaffold, biosynthesized via the hex gene cluster and subsequent late-stage Diels−
Alder reactions. Among the analogues, 1 demonstrated the most potent antibacterial activity against multidrug-resistant (MDR)
pathogens that were comparable to the positive control vancomycin. This work not only unveils the samsumycins as a structurally
unique antibiotic family, detailing their discovery to biosynthetic logic, but also establishes an innovative, streamlined strategy for
fast-mining of novel natural products in microbes.
A
ntibiotic resistance has emerged as a critical global health
threat, recognized by public health authorities world-
wide.1,2 This crisis is exacerbated by infections caused by
multidrug-resistant (MDR) pathogensEnterococcus faecium,
Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter
baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Enterobacter spp.
designated as critical pathogens in the WHO 2024 Bacterial
Priority Pathogens List.3,4 These pathogens contribute to
escalating morbidity and mortality rates globally. Natural
products serve as the cornerstone of antimicrobial discovery
and remain indispensable for addressing antibiotic scarcity;5,6
however, conventional discovery pipelines face two funda-
mental constraints: time-intensive processes burdened by
frequent rediscovery of known compounds. Consequently,
developing innovative strategies to unlock nature’s structural
repertoire constitutes a pivotal challenge in contemporary drug
discovery.7,8
NMR-based metabolomics has emerged as an indispensable
tool for scaffold discovery, offering untargeted structural
elucidation capabilities applicable across diverse natural
sources. This approach establishes critical foundations for
comprehensive compound characterization through its ca-
pacity to decode complex metabolite networks.9−13 In parallel,
genome mining leverages biosynthetic gene cluster (BGC)
analysis to predict microbial chemical novelty, enabling
prioritization of strains with evolutionary-selected biosynthetic
potential.14−16 The synergistic integration of these method-
ologies, termed metabologenomics, represents a paradigm shift
in natural product research. By interlinking chemical signatures
with biosynthetic blueprints, this dual-omics strategy signifi-
cantly accelerates structural novelty identification while
circumventing rediscovery pitfalls inherent to conventional
approaches.17,18
In this study, we developed a multistage workflow (Figure 1)
to achieve fast-mining of novel antibiotics. Initially, Strepto-
myces sp. OUTCT16-12, exhibiting potent activity against
MDR bacteria, was selected via high-throughput dual-layer
coculture screening of over 200 marine bacterial strains. To
streamline the discovery of structurally novel compounds, we
implemented a metabologenomics-driven approach integrating
2D-NMR-based metabolomic profiling with genome mining.
This combined strategy enabled efficient prioritization and
targeted isolation of a new family of macrolide antibiotics,

■EXPERMENTAL SECTION
General Experimental Procedures. 1D and 2D NMR
spectra were provided by Bruker Avance III 600 spectrometers
and chemical shifts were reported with reference to the
respective solvent peaks and residual solvent peaks (δH 3.31
and δC 49.0 for CD3OD; δH 2.50 and δC 39.5 for DMSO-d6;
and δH 7.27 and δC 77.2 for CDCl3). HPLC-HRESIMS data
were obtained on an Agilent 1260 HPLC system combined
with a Q-TOF Ultima Global GAA076 MS spectrometer.
Analytical HPLC was performed on an Agilent 1260 Infinity
apparatus with a diode array detector (DAD). The ECD
spectra in MeOH were obtained using a JASCO J-1500
spectropolarimeter. Optical rotations were measured on a
JASCO P-1030 digital polarimeter. The IR spectra were
determined on a Nicolet NEXUE470 FT-IR. The optical
density (OD) measurements were recorded on a Biotech
Epoch2 microplate reader.
Small-Scale Cultivations and LC-MS Analysis. Strepto-
myces sp. OUCT16-12 was cultivated in 50 mL of TSBY seed
medium for 48 h at 30 °C, 220 rpm. Then, each seed culture
broth was added to 50 mL of Medium Oats, Millet, Rice, F2,
A, B, AF/MS, V6, RA3, INA5, V, M8, D/Seed Vegetative, and
GYM, respectively, and cultured at 30 °C for 5 days. The
culture broths were extracted twice with an equal volume of
EtOAc, followed by vacuum evaporation. Sequentially, each
EtOAc extract was dissolved in 1 mL of MeOH, and was
subjected to HPLC-HRESIMS analysis. Analytical HPLC was
performed with a linear gradient from 10% to 100% B/A
(phase B: 100% ACN; phase A: H2O; Agilent Eclipse Plus
C18, 100 × 2.1 mm, 3.5 μm; wavelength: 220, 240, 260, 280,
300, 320, and 365 nm; flow rate: 0.2 mL/min).
SMART 2.1 Analysis. The 1H and 13C NMR tables from
annotated HSQC spectrum of the culture extract or Fr.8 of
Streptomyces sp. OUCT16-12 were subjected to SMART 2.1
platform (https://smart.ucsd.edu/classic). SMART Analysis
compared query HSQC spectrum against a library of >100,000
natural products and provided with SMILES, structures, cosine
similarity, and molecular weight of compound of interest.
Fermentation, Extraction and Isolation. The spores of
Streptomyces sp. OUCT16-12 was grown on MS agar medium
plates. Then the OUCT16-12 strain was cultured for 38 L for
the compound isolation. A pure colony of Streptomyces sp.
OUCT16-12 which spores were obtained on MS agar medium
plates was inoculated into 1000 mL Erlenmeyer flasks
containing 200 mL of M8 broth and incubated at 30 °C on
a rotary shaker at 220 rpm for 5 days. The fermentation broth
was extracted with EtOAc, and yielded the organic extracts
(6.5 g) which was further subjected to a reversed-phase (C18)
open column chromatography with 65−100% MeOH to afford
11 fractions (Fr.). Compound 1 (12.3 mg) was isolated from
Fr.8 by C18 (YMC ODS-A column, 250 × 10 mm, 5 μm)

Stereochemical Study Based on NMR-Constrained
Conformational Modeling Coupled with Quantum
Chemical Calculations. The 3D-models of the stereoisomers
of the compounds were preconstructed based on the NOESY
correlations using the program Chem3D, which were followed
by the energy minimization driven by MM2/MMFF94 force
field. This step was repeated until the conformer of each
stereoisomer supported key NOEs in the experimental NOESY
spectrum. Then, the preconstructed conformers were sub-
jected to geometry optimization with DFT calculations at the
B97D/TZVP level of theory using the Gaussian 09 program.
The TD calculations were performed on each optimized
conformer using the long-range-corrected hybrid CAM-
B3LYP. The number of excited states per molecule was 50.
Solvent (MeOH) effects were considered by using the
polarizable continuum model (PCM). The ECD spectra
were generated by the program GaussView 5.0. All the DP4
calculations were managed by the PyDP4 Python script within
DP4-AI that is available from http://www-jmg.ch.cam.ac.uk/
tools/nmr/ and GitHub https://github.com/KristapsE/DP4-
AI/. The raw 1H and 13C NMR spectra were prepared as input
files. Each conformer was conducted to molecular mechanics
calculations in the gas phase utilizing the MMFF force field
using MacroModel, which was set to find all low energy
conformers within 10 kcal/mol at least 5 times. The NMR
calculations were carried out with GIAO method at the
mPW1PW91/6-311G(d) level of theory (with MeOH as the
solvent). The DP4 probabilities of stereoisomers were
calculated using the Applet within DP4-AI. The stereoisomer
showing a calculated ECD consistent with the experimental
data and a predominant DP4 NMR probability represented the
correct absolute configurations.
Dual-Layer Coculture Assay. Culture media (see
Supporting Information) for the growth of marine bacteria
were prepared as agar plates, and each plate was divided into
dozens or hundreds of equal grids, with the number depending
on plate size. Marine bacterial strains from our in-house library
(comprising >200 isolates) were inoculated at the center of
each grid and incubated at 30 °C for 2−3 days until colony
formation. Concurrently, 1 mL cultures of MDR strains (S.
aureus CCARM 3090, E. faecium CCARM 5203, and E. faecalis
CCARM 5172, purchased from Seoul Women’s University,
Korea) were mixed with 50 mL of 0.8% agar medium (LB for
S. aureus; BHI for E. faecium and E. faecalis) at 40−50 °C. The
mixtures were then overlaid onto the marine bacterial plates
and incubated at 30 °C for 12 h. Strains producing anti-MDR-
bacterial metabolites were identified by measuring inhibition
zones (in millimeters).
Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) Assay. The
MDR bacterial strains were grown overnight at 37 °C in liquid
LB medium and diluted to 106 CFU/mL. A series of
concentrations of samples were obtained by the compound
solutions diluted with MeOH. Twenty μL of the sample
solutions with different concentrations was dispensed into 180
μL of the bacterial suspension in the 96-well plates and
incubated at 37 °C for 16 h. The growth of bacterial strains
was measured with a microplate reader at the wavelength of
620 nm. LB broth was used as a blank. MeOH was used as a
negative control. Vancomycin was used as a positive control.

To further evaluate the structural novelty of the components
in Fr.8 tracked by 2D-NMR-metabolomic analysis, genome
mining of HCFM coding genes (fumarate adenyltransferase
and 5-aminolevulinate synthase) in Streptomyces sp.
OUTCT16-12 was performed, which resulted in the presence
of two sets of homologous genes located on bfl (GenBank No.
OR147678)20 and hex (GenBank No. OP353995)21 bio-
synthetic gene clusters (BGCs), respectively (Figure S10). The
bfl BGC has been identified to be responsible for bafilomycin
biosynthesis20 and a heterologous expression of hex led to the
production of hexacosalactone A.20,21 However, UV-HPLC-
MS analysis of the major component in Fr.8, plausibly
responsible for the antibiotic activity, is consistent with neither
known bafilomycins20 nor hexacosalactone A.22 These findings
provided strong evidence that Fr.8 contains new antibiotic
chemical scaffolds that are worthy of further exploration.

OUTCT16-12 was carried out, leading to the targeted isolation

Structure Identification of Type-A Samsumycin (1).
Samsumycin A (1) was isolated as a light green amorphous
solid. Based on (−)-HRESIMS analysis, the molecular formula
of 1 was established as C46H67NO11. The COSY spectrum of 1
revealed the presence of H-3/H-4/H-5, H-7/H-8/H-9, H-11/
H-12/H-13/H-14/H-15/H-16/H-17/H-18/H-32/H-33, H-
19/H-20/H-21/H-22/H-34/H-35, and H-24/H-25/H-26/H-
27/H-28 proton spin systems (Figure 3A). The HMBC
correlations (Figure 3A) from H3-29 to C-1, C-2, and C-3;
from H3-30 to C-5, C-6, and C-7; from H3-31 to C-9, C-10,
and C-11 suggested these methyl groups were substituted on
C-2, C-6, and C-10, respectively. The downfield shifted C-23
(δC 102.1), together with the HMBC correlations from H-19,
H-21, H-24, H-25, H-35, and 23-OMe to C-23 identified a six-
membered ketal ring moiety. Then, the HMBC correlations
from H-3 and H-25 to the carbonyl carbon C-1 (δC 167.7)
defined the 26-membered macrolide framework. The down-
field shifted C-13, C-15, C-17, C-19, and C-25 revealed them
to be hydroxylated carbons. The location of a methoxy group
was established based on the HMBC correlation from 15-OMe
to C-15. Two carbonyls (C-1′ and C-4′) and trans olefinic
methines (H-2′ and H-3′) combined with the COSY
correlation of H-2’/H-3′ and the HMBC correlations from
H-3′ to C-1′, from H-2′ to C-4′, and from H-3′ to C-4′
identified a fumarate moiety. The HMBC correlations from H-
8′ to C-5′, C-7′, C-9′, and C-10′; and from an amide proton
5′-NH to C-6′, C-7′, and C-10′ established a C5N moiety,
which was connected to the fumarate at C-4′ to form HCFM
side chain. The HMBC correlation from H-13 to the C-1′
revealed that the HCFM group was substituted on C-13. The
planar structure of 1 was thus established.
Compound 1 features a flexible macrolide skeleton with five
conjugated double bonds and ten chiral centers. Due to its
conformational flexibility, crystallization attempts were un-
successful, necessitating a correlative analytical approach to
resolve its stereochemistry. Double bond geometries were
determined to be 2E, 4Z, 6E, 8E, 10E based on the coupling
constants (J) (Table S3), together with NOESY correlations
(H-3/H-7, H-4/H-29, H5/H-30, H-8/H-30, H-8/H-31, H-9/
H-11) (Figure 3A). For the chiral centers (C13−C25), J-based
configurational analysis (JBCA) employing 3JHH,
3JHC, 2JHC
couplings established the relative configurations as 13S*-14R*-
15R*-17R* and 19S*-20S*-22R* (Figure 3B). The stereo-
chemical outcomes at C13−C25 are dictated by ketoreductase
(KR) and enoylreductase (ER) domains within modules M1
and M4−M7 of the polyketide synthase (PKS) in the hex BGC
(Figure S14). Sequence analysis classified the KRs in M1 and
M5−M7 as type B1, while M4 harbored a type A KR (Figure
S15). These domains collectively determine the configurations
13S, 14R, 15R, 17S, 18R, 19S, 20S, 22R, 23R, and 25S. Joint
JBCA and NOE analysis corroborated these assignments at all
positions except C-17: although KR catalysis inherently favors
a syn-configuration between H-15 and H-17, JBCA data
revealed an anti-configuration. This discrepancy suggests an
unconventional stereochemical outcome at C-17, potentially
implicating auxiliary enzymatic influences or substrate-level
constraints during biosynthesis. To further establish the
absolute configurations of 1, a recently developed hybrid
stereochemical analysis, integrating NMR-constrained con-
formational modeling with quantum chemical calculations, was

Proposed Biosynthetic Pathway of Samsumycins.
Samsumycins 1−6 share a core structural scaffold with
hexacozalactone A, featuring a 26-membered macrolide
backbone that incorporates an HCFM unit. Therefore, their
biosynthesis in Streptomyces sp. OUCT16-12 is proposed to be
mediated by hex BGC.21 This proposal is supported by the hex
BGC exhibiting 99% identity to the hcl that is responsible for
hexacozalactone A biosynthesis in Streptomyces sp. LZ35
(GenBank No. MT994316.1),22 and by the confirmed
production of hexacozalactone A following heterologous
expression of the hex BGC.21 Four PKSs HexI-IV are
responsible for the assembly of the 26-membered macrolide
backbone using isobutyryl-CoA as a loading unit (Figures 6A
and S14). HexE, a fumarate adenyltransferase, catalyzes the
adenylation of fumarate, and HexD, a fumaryl transferase,
transfers the fumarate moiety to the hydroxy group at C-13
(1a). The methyl substitution at 15-OH is proven to be
catalyzed by the O-methyl transferase HexF.21 HexA and HexC
cooperatively synthesize the C5N unit, and HexB catalyzes the
transfer of C5N to the fumaryl moiety of the macrolide
intermediates to form HCFM unit. The ketal/hemiketal rings
in 1, 2, 5 and 6 are proposed to be formed via nonenzymatic
acetalization in the presence of H2O or MeOH. This
spontaneous, reversible process generates structural isomers
(23-keto/23-hemiketal forms), contributing to the instability
observed in specific samsumycin analogues, including 2−4.
Consequently, samsumycins were stored at −20 °C to suppress
isomerization and maintain compound stability. The Diels−
Alder reactions is likely to occur between C-3′ dienophile and
C-9 dienes, which construct the 6/6-heterobicyclic core of 2−
6 (Figures 2 and 6B). Compounds 2, 4 and 5, featuring 8R,
11R configurations, represent endo Diels−Alder products,
while 3 and 6, with 8S, 11S configurations, are exo adducts
(Figures 2 and 6B). In the biosynthetic pathway, Diels−Alder
reactions can proceed nonenzymatically or depend on
enzymes, either monofunctional or multifunctional.25 Since
the reported Diels−Alderases mainly show single stereo-
preferences,26−31 the Diels−Alder reactions in the samsumycin
biosynthesis are more likely to occur spontaneously instead of
via enzymatic catalyzation.
Antibacterial Activity Evaluation. In antibacterial
activity evaluations against MDR pathogens, 1 demonstrated
the most potent activity among all samsumycin analogues
tested, exhibiting MIC values of 3.1 μg/mL against S. aureus
CCARM 3090, E. faecalis CCARM 5172, and E. faecium
CCARM 5203. This efficacy was comparable to the positive
control vancomycin (MICs 1.6−6.2 μg/mL) (Table S8).
Compound 2 showed selective inhibitory activity against E.
faecalis (MIC 6.2 μg/mL), and 5 inhibited both S. aureus and
E. faecalis at an MIC of 12.5 μg/mL. However, none of
samsumycins showed inhibition against Gram-negative MDR
pathogens (A. baumannii 25033, E. coli CCARM 1009, K.
pneumoniae ATCC 13883, and P. aeruginosa ATCC BAA-
2108) up to 25 μg/mL. These findings align with our bioassay-
coupled NMR metabolomic analysis, which identified 1 as the
primary antibiotic component in the series.
■CONCLUSIONS
In summary, by integrating a 2D-NMR-based metabologe-
nomics approach with high-throughput dual-layer coculture
activity screening, we enabled the targeted discovery of
structurally novel samsumycin-family macrolides (1−6) from
the deep-sea Streptomyces sp. OUTCT16-12. Compounds 2−6
possess a rare 6/6-heterobicyclic ring system fused to 26-
membered macrolide skeleton. The biosynthesis of the parent
macrolide scaffold in the samsumycins is believed to be
mediated by the hex BGC, with subsequent nonenzymatic
acetalization or/and Diels−Alder reactions driving the
formation of the final structures (1−6). Among the analogues,
1 exhibited the most potent antibacterial activity against MDR
pathogens, including S. aureus CCARM 3090, E. faecalis
CCARM 5172, and E. faecium CCARM 5203 (MIC 3.1 μg/
mL), with efficacy rivaling that of the positive control
vancomycin. NMR-metabologenomics has emerged as a
powerful strategy to overcome redundancy challenges and
streamline the discovery of novel compounds. This integrated
approach is particularly effective when active components
contain functional groups that are diagnostic via both NMR-
based metabolomics and genome mining. This work not only
unveils the samsumycins as a structurally unique family of
antibiotic macrolides, spanning their targeted discovery,
structural elucidation, and biosynthetic pathway; but also
establishes a streamlined workflow for the accelerated mining
of novel microbial natural products.
