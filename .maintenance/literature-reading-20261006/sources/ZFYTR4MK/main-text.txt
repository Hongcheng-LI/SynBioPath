ABSTRACT: Mass spectrometry-based dereplication and priori-
tization led to the discovery of four multi-N-methylated cyclo-
decapeptides, auyuittuqamides E−H (1−4), from a soil-derived
Sesquicillium sp. The planar structures of these compounds were
elucidated based on analysis of HRESIMS and NMR data.
Absolute configurations of the chiral amino acid residues were
assigned by a combination of the advanced Marfey’s method,
chiral-phase LC-MS analysis, and J-based configuration analysis,
revealing that 1−4 contain both
D- and
L-isomers of N-
methylleucine (MeLeu). Differentiation of D- and L-MeLeu in
the sequence was achieved by advanced Marfey’s analysis of the
diagnostic peptide fragments generated from partial hydrolysis of
1. Bioinformatic analysis identified a putative biosynthetic gene
cluster (auy) for auyuittuqamides E−H, and a plausible biosynthetic pathway was proposed. These newly identified fungal
cyclodecapeptides (1−4) displayed in vitro growth inhibitory activity against vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus faecium with MIC
values of 8 μg/mL.
M
icrobial peptide natural products (PNPs) and their
semisynthetic derivatives have an unrivaled track record
as antibacterial therapeutics in the clinic.1,2 The β-lactams (e.g.,
penicillins and cephalosporins) represent the most successful
member of this class, accounting for about 60% of antibiotics
in clinical use. The glycopeptide vancomycin is used as the
first-line antibiotic to treat infections caused by widespread β-
lactam-resistant bacteria, and the lipopeptide daptomycin is
currently held in reserve as the drug of last resort against
multidrug-resistant Gram-positive pathogens.3 Nevertheless,
the emergence of daptomycin resistance in both staphylococci
and enterococci has already been reported.4,5 There is now an
urgent need to develop new classes of antibiotics with unique
modes of action to combat the ever-threatening antimicrobial
resistance.
Filamentous fungi are prolific producers of bioactive natural
products, from which the penicillins and cephalosporins are
isolated. However, ever since the discovery of these β-lactam
antibiotics, no promising antibacterial lead compound has been
identified from this source. Recent genome sequencing
projects revealed that many fungi contain biosynthetic gene
clusters capable of producing up to 90 specialized metabolites,6
many of which are yet-to-be-identified nonribosomal or
ribosomally synthesized and post-translationally modified
peptides. This denotes that filamentous fungi still represent
an underexploited reservoir of antibacterial PNPs.
We have recently developed a mass spectrometry-based
dereplication and prioritization approach to expedite the
discovery of novel PNPs from filamentous fungi.7,8 Among
the fungal strains we have screened using matrix-assisted laser
desorption ionization-time-of-flight mass spectrometry
(MALDI-TOF MS), the ascomycete fungus Sesquicillium sp.
QL0466 was found to produce a cluster of ions at m/z 1079.9
and 1095.9 (Figure 1A), corresponding to the [M + Na]+ and
[M + K]+ adducts of a putative PNP with a molecular weight
of 1056 Da. Liquid chromatography−high resolution electro-
spray ionization tandem mass spectrometry (LC-HRESIMS/
MS) analysis of the QL0466 crude extract revealed four
different compounds (1−4) that displayed the [M + H]+ ion
around m/z 1057.66 (Supplementary Figures S1−S4). Further
comparison of the MS/MS profiles of 1−4 showed that they

■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
The EtOH extract of the fungal culture was subjected to a mass
spectrometry-guided separation by column chromatography on
D101 resin and octadecylsilyl (ODS) silica gel, to afford
fractions containing compounds 1−4. Further purification of
these fractions using semipreparative high-performance liquid
chromatography (HPLC) yielded 1−4 as single compounds.
Compound 1 was isolated as a white amorphous powder. Its
molecular formula was determined as C53H88N10O12 upon
analysis of the HRESIMS data, indicating 15 degrees of
unsaturation. Strong absorptions at 3298 and 1634 cm−1 were
observed in the infrared (IR) spectrum of 1 (Supplementary
Figure S5), characteristic of the presence of amide functional
groups. The 1H NMR spectrum of 1 displayed resonances for
seven exchangeable amide protons at δH 7.4−8.8, five aromatic
protons at δH 7.2−7.3, multiple protons ranging from δH 2.7 to
5.4, three N-CH3 groups at δH 3.14, 3.29, and 3.30, a large
envelope of CH and CH2 protons at δH 1.0−2.0, and several
overlapping CH3 signals at δH 0.7−1.0 (Table 1). Analysis of
13C and HSQC spectra revealed the presence of 10 amide
carbonyl carbons at δC 167.7−172.6, six aromatic carbons at δC
126.5−137.1, 21 carbons at δC 30.8−63.3, and 16 aliphatic
carbons at δC 10.4−24.7 (Table 1). These signals strongly
suggested that 1 was a decapeptide.
Analysis of COSY, TOCSY, and HMBC data (Figure 2)
revealed the presence of one phenylalanine (Phe) residue,
threonine (Thr) and serine (Ser) residues, and seven aliphatic
AAs, including isoleucine (Ile), glycine (Gly, 2×), and leucine
(Leu, 4×). The N-CH3 groups at δH 3.13, 3.29, and 3.30
showed HMBC correlations with the α-carbons of Thr (δC
61.2) and two of the four Leu moieties (both at δC 63.1). The
benzene ring of Phe and 10 amide carbonyl functionalities
accounted for 14 of the 15 degrees of unsaturation, suggesting
1 as a macrocyclic peptide. Connection of the established AAs
was possible by observing key HMBC correlations from the N-
H or N-CH3 protons to the carbonyl carbons of adjacent
residues (Figure 2). Specifically, the N-H of Gly1 (δH 7.57), the
N-H of Phe2 (δH 7.67), the N-CH3 of N-methylthreonine33
(MeThr3; δH 3.13), and the N-H of Ile4 (δH 8.55) were
individually correlated to the carbonyl carbons of N-
methylleucine10 (MeLeu10), Gly1, Phe2, and MeThr3, establish-
ing a partial sequence of MeLeu10-Gly1-Phe2-MeThr3-Ile4.
Similarly, another partial sequence of MeLeu5-Gly6-Leu7-Ser8-
Leu9 was deduced from the HMBC data. Despite the
overlapping N-CH3 signals of MeLeu5 and MeLeu10 (δH 3.29
and 3.30, respectively), one of them showed correlation to the
carbonyl carbon of Ile4 (δC 171.6), while the other displayed
an HMBC cross-peak with the carbonyl carbon of Leu9 (δC
172.0). After ruling out all possibilities that possess Ile4-
MeLeu10 and Leu9-MeLeu5 linkages, the structure for 1 was
deduced as cyclo(-Gly1-Phe2-MeThr3-Ile4-MeLeu5-Gly6-Leu7-
Ser8-Leu9-MeLeu10-). Analysis of ROESY data showed
interactions of N-H or N-CH3 protons with the α-CH or α-
CH2 proton(s) of neighboring AAs (Figure 2) that further
supported the established planar structure of 1.
Compound 1 was subjected to acid hydrolysis and advanced
Marfey’s analysis to determine the absolute configuration of
the chiral AAs.9 Comparison of the liquid chromatography−
mass spectrometry (LC-MS) profiles of the 1-fluoro-2,4-
dinitrophenyl-5-L-leucine amide (L-FDLA) and 1-fluoro-2,4-
dinitrophenyl-5-D,L-leucine amide (D,L-FDLA) derivatives of
the acid hydrolysate of 1 (Supplementary Figure S9) revealed
that the Phe, MeThr, Ile, Leu, and Ser residues were all L-
configured, while the MeLeu adopted both
D- and
L-
configurations. Since the FDLA-derivatives of
L-Ile and
L-
allo-Ile are inseparable on a reverse-phase C18 HPLC column
(Supplementary Figure S9),10 chiral-phase LC-MS was used to

Compounds 2 and 3 were both obtained as white
amorphous powders. They were assigned the molecular
formula of C53H88N10O12 on the basis of the HRESIMS
data. The 1H and 13C NMR data of 2 and 3 were nearly
identical to those of 1 (Tables 2 and 3). Analysis of COSY,
TOCSY, and HSQC spectra revealed that the only difference
between compound 2 and 1 was the presence of a NH−CH−
CH(CH3)−CH2−CH3 spin system in 2 instead of the NH−
CH−CH2−CH(CH3)2 fragment in 1 (Supplementary Figure
S18), indicating the replacement of a Leu residue by an Ile
residue. The HMBC correlations from the N-H of this Ile (δH
8.83) to the carbonyl carbon of Ser8 (δC 170.1) and from the
N-CH3 of MeLeu10 (δH 3.35) to the carbonyl carbon of the Ile
(δC 172.1) confirmed that the replacement took place at the
ninth position (Supplementary Figure S18), establishing the
planar structure of 2 as cyclo(-Gly1-Phe2-MeThr3-Ile4-MeLeu5-
Gly6-Leu7-Ser8-Ile9-MeLeu10-). Likewise, analysis of the 2D
NMR spectra of 3 confirmed that the only difference between
compounds 3 and 1 was the exchange of the Leu7 in 1 with an
Ile moiety in 3 (Supplementary Figure S18). Thus, the planar
structure of 3 was elucidated as cyclo(-Gly1-Phe2-MeThr3-Ile4-
MeLeu5-Gly6-Ile7-Ser8-Leu9-MeLeu10-).
Compound 4 was isolated as a white amorphous powder
with a molecular formula of C53H88N10O12 as determined by
HRESIMS. The 1H and 13C NMR data of 4 were also highly
similar to those of 1 (Table 4). Analysis of COSY, TOCSY,
and HSQC spectra of 4 revealed the presence of three NH−
CH−CH(CH3)−CH2−CH3 spin systems and the absence of
any NH−CH−CH2−CH(CH3)2 fragment, suggesting that
both Leu7 and Leu9 in 1 were replaced by Ile moieties in 4
(Supplementary Figure S18). This was supported by the
observed HMBC cross-peaks from the N-H of Ile7 (δH 7.44) to
the carbonyl carbon of Gly6 (δC 167.8) and from the N-H of
Ile9 (δH 8.90) to the carbonyl carbon of Ser8 (δC 170.1).
Detailed analysis of HMBC and ROESY data confirmed that
the identity and position of the other AAs in 4 were the same
as those in 1 (Supplementary Figure S18). Accordingly, the
planar structure of 4 was defined as cyclo(-Gly1-Phe2-MeThr3-
Ile4-MeLeu5-Gly6-Ile7-Ser8-Ile9-MeLeu10-). The absolute con-
figurations of the chiral AAs presented in 2−4 were
determined as shown in Figure 1B by using similar approaches
to those described for compound 1 (Supplementary Figures
S10−S13).
The structures of 1−4 are unprecedented. However, they are
structurally related to the recently discovered auyuittuqamides
A−D,12 four marine-derived fungal cyclodecapeptides having a
general primary sequence of cyclo(-Gly1-Leu2-MeThr3-Val4/
Ile4-MePhe5-Gly6-Val7/Ile7-Ser8-Val9/Ile9-MeVal10-). The AA
content of compounds 1−4 is similar to that of auyuittuqa-
mides A−D, both featuring one Phe, one Ser, one Thr, two
Gly, and five aliphatic residues such as Leu, Ile, and Val (Figure
1). Noteworthily, the positioning of the Ser, Thr, and two Gly
and the N-methylation pattern in the backbone of compounds
1−4 and the auyuittuqamides are highly conserved. Despite
these similarities, the structures of compounds 1−4 and
auyuittuqamides have three substantial differences. First, the
reported auyuittuqamides are rich in Val units, while
compounds 1−4 contain an abundance of Leu/Ile residues
instead; second, the overall sequences of 1−4 are different
from those of auyuittuqamides, due to the presence of
dissimilar AAs at the second and fifth positions; last, the
chiral AAs in auyuittuqamides A−C are exclusively
L-
configured, while compounds 1−4 contain a D-MeLeu residue.
Since compounds 1−4 and auyuittuqamides A−D are
structural analogs isolated from taxonomically close Sesquicil-
lium strains, these newly discovered peptides are named as
auyuittuqamides E−H.
The flexibility of the seventh and ninth amino acid residues,
as well as the presence of D-amino acid and multiple N-
methylated residues in 1−4, strongly suggests a nonribosomal
origin of these PNPs.13 Bioinformatic analysis of the sequenced
genome of Sesquicilium sp. QL0466 using antiSMASH 6.014
revealed the presence of 17 secondary metabolite biosynthetic
gene clusters (BGCs), with six harboring a nonribosomal
peptide synthetase (NRPS) as the core biosynthetic enzyme.
However, only one of these NRPSs (designated as AuyB)
contains the appropriate number of modules required for the
collinear assembly of the decapeptide backbone of 1−4 (Figure
4A and Supplementary Table S1). The third, fifth, and 10th
module of AuyB each contains an N-methylation (M) domain,
consistent with the N-methylation pattern of 1−4. Moreover,
AuyB has a C-terminal condensation domain (CT), which is
characteristic of fungal NRPSs that synthesize macrocyclic
peptides.7 The predicted substrate specificity codes of the
adenylation (A) domains15 in all modules of AuyB were in
broad agreement with the amino acids found in 1−4. The
conserved aspartic acid (Asp) and lysine (Lys) residues, which
have been proposed to mediate key interactions with the α-
amino and the carboxyl groups of the α-amino acid substrate,15
are replaced by Gly and Thr, respectively, in the identified

■EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
General Experimental Procedures. Optical rotations were
measured on an Anton Paar MCP-200 polarimeter (Anton Paar
GmbH, Graz, Austria) at 25 °C. Ultraviolet (UV) spectra were
recorded on a Shimadzu UV-2450 spectrophotometer (Shimadzu
Corporation, Kyoto, Japan). IR spectra were obtained on a Bruker
VERTEX 70v Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectrophotometer
equipped with a diamond ATR device (Bruker Corporation, Billerica,
MA, USA). NMR experiments were carried out on an Agilent DD2
600 MHz NMR spectrometer (Agilent Technologies Inc., CA, USA).
Chemical shift values (δ) are given in parts per million (ppm), and
the coupling constants (J values) are in Hz. Chemical shifts were
referenced to the residual solvent peaks of DMSO-d6. MALDI-TOF
MS spectra were obtained on a Bruker Autoflex Speed mass
spectrometer (Bruker Corporation). LC-HRESIMS/MS spectra
were recorded on an Agilent 1290 II ultra high performance liquid
chromatograph (UHPLC) coupled with an Agilent 6530 QTOF
instrument (Agilent Technologies Inc.) operated in positive ion mode
using capillary and cone voltages of 3.6 kV and 40−150 V,
respectively. The collision energy was optimized from 15 to 50 eV.
For accurate mass measurement, the instrument was calibrated each
time using a standard calibration mix in the range of m/z 150−1900.
Routine LC-MS analysis was performed on an Agilent 1290 II
UHPLC equipped with an Agilent G6125B single quadrupole mass
spectrometer (Agilent Technologies Inc.). For column chromatog-
raphy, macroporous D101 resin (Macklin Biochemical Co. Ltd.,
Shanghai, China) and Fuji ODS silica gel (40−75 μm, Fuji Silysia
Chemical Ltd., Osaka, Japan) were used. Semipreparative HPLC
experiments were performed on an Agilent 1260 II HPLC system at
the flow rate of 2.5 mL/min (Agilent Technologies Inc.).
Fungal Strain and Fermentation. The culture of Sesquicillium
sp. QL0466 was isolated from a soil sample collected in the
Niubeiliang National Nature Reserve, Zhashui, Shaanxi, in October
2019. Identification of this fungus was based on morphological
characteristics and sequence homology of the ITS1-5.8S-ITS2 region
of the rDNA (GenBank accession number OP235392). The fungal
strain was allowed to grow on potato dextrose agar (PDA; potato
starch 4 g/L, dextrose 20 g/L, agar 15 g/L) medium at 28 °C for 7
days. The colony surface was gently washed with 5 mL of sterile H2O
containing 0.1% Triton X-100, and the resulting spore suspension was
filtered through a 40 μm cell strainer (Biologix Group Ltd., Jinan,
China). The filtrate was diluted with sterile H2O to give the final
seeding solution (spore concentration about 1 × 106/mL), which was
used to inoculate a Petri dish (90 mm in diameter) containing 20 mL
of PDA medium. The cultures were incubated at 28 °C under static
conditions for 14 days, followed by MALDI-TOF MS measurement
to monitor the production of target products. For scale-up, 14 L of
PDA medium (700 Petri dishes) was inoculated with the spore
solution of Sesquicillium sp. QL0466 and cultivated under the same
conditions used for the small-scale fermentations.
MALDI-TOF MS Data Acquisition. MALDI-TOF MS matrix
solution was prepared by dissolving trans-2-[3-(4-tert-butylphenyl)-2-
methyl-2-propenylidene]malononitrile (Sigma-Aldrich, Saint Louis,
MO, USA) at a final concentration of 15 mg/mL in 90% aqueous
MeOH solution with 0.1% HCO2H. Small amounts of the fungal
mycelia or 2 μL of solution obtained from column chromatography
was spotted directly on the stainless steel 384 target plate and
immediately overlaid with 2−5 μL of matrix solution. The MALDI-
TOF MS spectra were recorded in reflector/positive ion mode, and
each sample was ablated 500 times within 1 s with a laser energy
output of 80%. A mass-to-charge (m/z) window of 500−2500 was
applied to monitor the production of peptide secondary metabolites.
All mass data were analyzed in the FlexAnalysis software (Bruker
Corporation).

Determination of Absolute Configuration by the Advanced
Marfey’s Method and Chiral-Phase LC-MS Analysis. Appro-
priate samples of auyuittuqamides were hydrolyzed in 6 N HCl at 115
°C for 18 h. The hydrolysate was dried under vacuum and dissolved
in 100 μL of H2O. A 50 μL aliquot of this solution was treated with 20
μL of 1 M NaHCO3 and 100 μL of 1% L-FDLA in acetone, while the
other 50 μL aliquot was treated with 20 μL of 1 M NaHCO3 and 100
μL of D,L-FDLA. The reaction mixtures were heated at 45 °C for 2 h.
After cooling to room temperature, the reactions were quenched by
adding 10 μL of 2 N HCl. Then, the resulting residues were diluted
with 1 mL of 50% aqueous MeCN for subsequent LC-MS analysis.
LC-MS runs were performed on an Agilent Poroshell 120 EC-C18
column (2.7 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm) at the flow rate of 0.4 mL/min,
eluting with gradient MeCN−H2O supplemented with 0.1% HCO2H
(0−15 min, 20% →60% MeCN; 15−17 min, 60% →95% MeCN;
17−20 min, 20% MeCN). The FDLA derivative of each AA residue
was identified in the extracted ion chromatogram (EIC) for the
corresponding m/z value. The absolute configurations of constituent
AA residues were determined by comparing the retention times of
AA-L-FDLA with those of the AA-D,L-FDLA derivative. The LC-MS
EIC chromatograms of the AA-L-FDLA and AA-D,L-FDLA derivatives
are shown in Supplementary Figures S9−S12. For chiral-phase LC-
MS analysis, the L-FDLA-derived acid hydrolysate of each peptide was
analyzed on a DAICEL CHIRALPAK IC chiral column (5 μm, 4.6
mm × 250 mm) at the flow rate of 1.0 mL/min, eluting with gradient
MeCN−H2O (0−40 min, 10% →95% MeCN). The
L-FDLA
derivatives of authentic L-Ile and L-allo-Ile were used as references
(Supplementary Figure S13).
Determination of the Location of D- and L-MeLeu Residues
in 1. An aliquot of 1 (5 mg) was partially hydrolyzed in 20% TFA
solution at 100 °C for 5 h. The reaction was quenched by adding a
saturated NH4HCO3 solution and then subjected to solid-phase
extraction using a C18 cartridge. The cartridge was first washed with
10% aqueous MeOH to remove salt and then with 100% MeOH to
elute the peptide fragments. The MeOH fraction was subjected to
LC-MS-guided fractionation on a Kromasil 100-5-C18 column (5 μm,
4.6 × 250 mm) at the flow rate of 1 mL/min, eluting with gradient
MeCN−H2O supplemented with 0.1% HCO2H (0−3 min, 10%
MeCN; 3−8 min, 10% →35% MeCN; 8−13 min, 35% MeCN; 13−
16 min, 35% →95% MeCN; 16−19 min, 95% MeCN) to afford 10
subfractions (Fr1−Fr10). Fr6 and Fr8 were further purified,
respectively, on an Agilent Poroshell 120 HILIC-Z column (2.7
μm, 4.6 × 150 mm) at the flow rate of 0.5 mL/min, eluting with
gradient MeCN−H2O (0−15 min, 95% →65% MeCN; 15−17 min,
95% MeCN) to yield two diagnostic peptide fragments, 5 (MeThr3-
Ile4-MeLeu5) and 6 (MeThr3-Ile4-MeLeu5-Gly6-Leu7) (Supplemen-
tary Figures S14−S16). These peptide fragments were separately
hydrolyzed in 6 N HCl and derivatized with L-FDLA. LC-MS analysis
of the L-FDLA derivatives of the hydrolysates confirmed that the
MeLeu5 residues in both 5 and 6 are L-configurated (Supplementary
Figure S17).
Genome Sequencing and Bioinformatic Analysis. The
genome of Sesquicillium sp. QL0466 was sequenced using a
combination of Illumina NovaSeq and PacBio technologies at Fuyu
Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Quality control was
performed at each stage of the sample preparation and sequencing.
The obtained PacBio subreads were corrected and then assembled
using Falcon (v0.3.0)42 and Celera Assembler (v8.3).43 Single base
errors in the assembly were further corrected with Illumina NovaSeq
data using GATK (v1.6-13).44 Contigs were linked to scaffolds with
long insert size pair-end reads using SSPPACE_Basic (v2.0).45 The
gaps were filled using pbjelly2 (v15.8.24).46 Gene prediction was
performed using genewise (v2.20),47 SNAP (v2010−07−28),48
Augustus (v3.2.1),49 and Genemarkes (v4.21).50 The predicted
proteins were compared to Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto
Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), Cluster of
Orthologous Groups of proteins (COG), and Swiss-Prot databases
using BLASTp, and the highest quality alignments were chosen.
antiSMASH 6.014 was used to identify the putative biosynthetic gene
cluster of auyuittuqamides E−H. The genomic DNA sequences of
Sesquicillium sp. QL0466 containing the auy cluster and the putative
amino acid racemase coding genes aar1 and aar2 were deposited in
GenBank under accession numbers OQ787034−OQ787036.
Bioassays. The cell-based antimicrobial assay was conducted in
96-well microtiter plates using procedures reported previously.8
Compounds were tested against four Gram-positive bacteria, S.
aureus ATCC 6538, MRSA 11646, vancomycin-sensitive E. faecium
160119481, and VRE 151458137, and one Gram-negative bacterium,
E. coli ATCC 25922. All experiments were carried out in triplicate,
and the data were shown as mean ± SD of three individual
experiments.
